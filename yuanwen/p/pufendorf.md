# Samuel Freiherr von Pufendorf
**First published Wed Jul 29, 2026; substantive revision Fri Aug 21, 2026**

*Seidler, Michael*

[*Editor’s Note: The following entry replaces and is based on the former entry titled [Pufendorf’s Moral and Political Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2026/entries/pufendorf-moral/).*]

Samuel Freiherr von Pufendorf (1632–1694) was almost as unknown during most of the 19th and 20th centuries as he had been familiar during the preceding hundred years and more. His fate shows well how philosophical interests shape historical background narratives. More or less consciously, individual thinkers and the traditions they spawn frame themselves in terms of an edited past which – as in other forms of genealogy – they either appropriate, reject, revise, or ignore. Thus intellectual ancestry is always more controversial than biological inheritance, and the mere presence or absence of thinkers in particular developmental accounts is not necessarily an accurate indication of their actual historical role or importance.

Pufendorf’s thought is situated in post-Westphalian Europe, as the Holy Roman Empire underwent a reconfiguration of power relationships among its quasi-autonomous constituent states (Austria, Bavaria, Palatinate, Saxony, Brandenburg, and others), while maintaining a guarded posture toward external, competing powers such as France, England, the United Provinces, Sweden, Denmark, Poland, and Spain. Its main goals were to avoid relapse into confessional warfare, and to ward off the respective French and Ottoman threats on its western and eastern borders. In this context Pufendorf developed a theory about the moral relationships of agents (individuals and groups), the authority and duties of states, and the lawful interactions among these. His chief objective, too, was to avoid destructive social conflict and the devolution on any level into the antagonistic and, he thought, self-defeating condition known as the state of nature.

Pufendorf’s approach was secular, non-metaphysical, and anti-authoritarian; it eschewed religious appeals, scholastic dogma, essentialism, teleology, and the frequent mix of these that appealed to many German thinkers, Catholic and Protestant alike. Instead, it built on Bodin, Lipsius, Grotius, Hobbes, the applied sciences (e.g., medicine), and the Italian reason-of-state tradition. Because of it, Pufendorf is known as a voluntarist in ethics, a sovereignty theorist in politics, and a realist in international relations theory. His kind of natural law is called ‘modern’ or ‘Protestant’ (Tuck 1987, Haakonssen 2004)[^haakonssen-2004][^tuck-1987], in contrast to the medieval, metaphysical, neo-scholastic, rationalist, or even Platonic versions of the genre represented not only by the 16th-century School of Salamanca (Suarez, Vitoria, et al.) but also Leibniz and then Wolff. Known mainly as a philosopher and a jurist, Pufendorf was also a respected historian whose accounts of (mostly, the external relations of) various European states reflect his basic philosophical concepts. He wrote notably on church-state relations, on intellectual and religious toleration, and on the Baconian theme of innovation and the Cartesian claim to independence in philosophy. Because of the clarity and comprehensiveness of his works, their intellectual acuity, as well as their polemical edge and consequent notoriety, they were translated into many European languages and thus provided – often in the form of pedagogical tools – many of the basic concepts and distinctions operative in the 18th-century discourse about morality, society, politics, history, and international affairs. Indeed, both directly and through his many editors, translators, and imitators, Pufendorf was largely responsible for the so-called ubiquity of natural law as a shared discourse during the following century (Haakonssen 2012)[^haakonssen-2012].

## 1. Life & Works

Samuel Pufendorf was born on January 8, 1632 in Dorfchemnitz, a small town southwest of Chemnitz in Saxony. The region’s Protestant elector, Johann Georg, had just been compelled by the Imperial Edict of Restitution (1629), and by Tilly’s sack of Magdeburg (1631), to abandon his neutral stance in the Thirty Years War and join the newly arrived (1630) Swedes under Gustavus Adolphus in an anti-Habsburg alliance. This, and his return to the Imperial fold in 1635, exposed his territories to previously avoided predations that would influence the young Pufendorf’s outlook on the Empire, religion, and political power – if not also the human condition more generally. Samuel’s father, Esaias Pufendorf, assumed the Lutheran pastorate at nearby Flöha in 1633, and it was there a few years later that the family experienced first-hand the terrors of the war (Döring 1996a)[^döring-1996a]. There were ten children beside Samuel, six of whom also survived to adulthood. Of these he was the second youngest of four brothers, some three years behind Esaias (1628–1687), who would become one of Europe’s most accomplished diplomats and remained, as well, Samuel’s lifelong confidant and benefactor (Bérenger 1993, Fiedler 2003)[^bérenger-1993][^fiedler-2003]. The dedication in Pufendorf’s *Select Academic Dissertations* (DAS: VII.1675b, GW7) acknowledges the debt.

Like his brothers, Pufendorf was home-schooled until age thirteen (1645), when his poverty and Latinity qualified him for admission to the subsidized *Fürstenschule* at Grimma, near Leipzig. There he studied grammar, logic, rhetoric, the Bible, Lutheran theology, and the Greek and Latin classics (Meyer 1894)[^meyer-1894]. His particular relish for the latter laid the foundations of the broad humanistic competence evident in and formative of his natural law writings. Indeed, shortly before leaving for Leipzig in the fall of 1650 he was chosen to compose a Latin poem celebrating the 100th anniversary of the school.

Leipzig: The university at Leipzig was far more cultivated and diverse than acknowledged by 19th-century authors like Treitschke (1897; Döring 2004, 1994b)[^döring-2004][^döring-1994b], though it remained a center of Lutheran orthodoxy, polemically anti-Calvinist and anti-Catholic, and generally devoted to a metaphysical scholasticism and Aristotelianism closely tied to theology (Sparn 1976)[^sparn-1976]. Samuel’s studies there initially included this discipline, which his father had intended as a preparation for the ministry. However, like his brother Esaias, and as indicated by his own extra-curricular activities in the *Collegium Anthologicum* – a student academic society before which he made some fifty presentations on philological, biblical, historical, and political themes (KVS: III.1995, pp. 21–86]; Döring 1988, 1992)[^döring-1992][^döring-1988] – he soon expanded his studies into other areas including philosophy, jurisprudence, and mathematics. He remained at Leipzig until early 1658, with a brief stay (in fall 1657) at Jena where he received his *Magister* (i.e., Masters) degree. In both Leipzig and Jena, Pufendorf lived and (like Leibniz after him) studied with Erhard Weigel (1625–1699), the eclectic mathematician and polymath who introduced him to Galileo, Grotius, Descartes, and Hobbes – authors without whom his future views would have been unimaginable (Specht 2023, Habermann & Herbst 2016)[^habermann-2016][^specht-2023]. It was from Weigel, too, that Pufendorf derived the important distinction between physical and moral entities, as well as the methodological ideal of a mathematically rigorous, or demonstrative system of moral philosophy (Sprenger 1996, Behme 2016, Schino 2014)[^behme-2016][^schino-2014][^sprenger-1996] to which he aspired, especially in distinguishing himself from the imprecisions of Aristotelian virtue theory (Heydt 2018b)[^heydt-2018b].

Denmark: Sweden’s involvement in the Thirty Years War and its stake in the Westphalian treaty that ended it increased the presence of its aristocrats at many German courts and universities, including Leipzig and Heidelberg (with which it also had dynastic ties). Esaias had already parlayed such contacts into a Swedish diplomatic career (Gossel 1940, Fiedler 2003)[^fiedler-2003][^gossel-1940] and now urged his brother to do the same, in view of the limited professional prospects in Saxony. Thus, in April 1658 Samuel assumed the post of tutor to the family of Sweden’s special envoy to Copenhagen, Peter Julius Coyet. This choice proved eventful, for when Charles X. Gustav unexpectedly renewed the First Northern War (1655–1660) against Denmark, the Swedish delegation was imprisoned for eight months. It was then, without access to library or books, that Pufendorf wrote his first major work, *Elements of Universal Jurisprudence* (EJU: VII.1660), which is explicitly based on Grotius and Hobbes. Upon being released in 1659, and after a serious illness and nearly dying by shipwreck in the Baltic, Pufendorf went to Holland as Coyet’s secretary. In spring 1660 he was enrolled at Leiden University where – though we have few details about the period – it appears that he turned to philology again. He became acquainted with the classical scholar Johann Friedrich Gronovius and began editing several Neo-Latin works, including Johannes Meursius, *Miscellanea Laconica* (1660) and Johannes Lauremberg, *Graecia antiqua* (1661). As important, though, was his mediated acquaintance with Peter de Groot, Hugo’s son and the Palatine Elector’s representative in Holland. For it was at his suggestion that Pufendorf strategically dedicated his captivity manuscript to Karl Ludwig (1617–1680), who responded promptly with the offer of a position at the renascent University of Heidelberg (Döring 2006)[^döring-2006].

Heidelberg: The Palatinate was one of the most devastated and depopulated regions during the Thirty Years War, and its university reopened only in 1652. Karl Ludwig sought initially to install Pufendorf in the (upper) law faculty there, as there was a vacancy in Roman law (Justinian’s *Institutes*). However, when both Pufendorf and the faculty senate demurred, the offer became a professorship in “philology and the law of nations” within the (lower, or preparatory) philosophy faculty – a position that addressed both the university’s needs and Pufendorf’s more obvious qualifications. There is no clear basis for Pufendorf’s later claim to have held the very first chair in “the law of nature and of nations” in Germany, and no support for the academic legend that he later sought a position in the law faculty and, when passed over, spitefully wrote *The Present State of Germany* (Monz: VII.1667, V.2007, II.1994) – a severe critique of legal theories about the Empire – to prove his qualifications (Döring 2006)[^döring-2006]. Much of Pufendorf’s time in Heidelberg was taken up with lecturing (on Grotius and Tacitus), and with developing the ideas of the *Elements*, particularly through a series of dissertations that – according to the practice of the time – were written mostly by himself but defended orally by his students (DAS: VII.1675b). The latter included a number of Swedish nobles – such as his former charge, the young (Wilhelm Julius) Coyet, and the son of the Swedish chancellor Gabriel Gabrielsson Oxenstierna, and the (illegitimate) son of the late Charles X. Gustav (Gustav Carlsson) – some of whom also resided with Pufendorf. These and other services on behalf of Sweden’s interests in Heidelberg, and the continuing support of Esaias, led to the offer, in late 1666, of a position at the newly established University of Lund (in Scania, the Danish province recently acquired by Sweden). Pufendorf’s departure from Heidelberg was apparently a career move and he always spoke positively of his time there, explicitly valuing the “freedom to philosophize” that he had enjoyed (Jägerskiöld 1985)[^jägerskiöld-1985].

Sweden: Pufendorf arrived in Lund in early 1668 to assume his position as *professor primarius* of the law of nature and of nations (in the Law faculty), and of ethics and politics (in the Philosophy faculty). Immediately popular as a teacher, well-connected at court, as well as confident and outspoken, he was soon embroiled in personal and academic conflicts with some of his colleagues, particularly the jurist Nikolaus Beckmann and the theologian Josua Schwartz, who recruited the Lutheran bishop of Lund (Peter Winstrup) to their cause. Their discontent with Pufendorf’s *On the Law of Nature and of Nations* (DJN: VII.1672), whose publication they actively tried to hinder, was articulated in *An Index of Certain Novelties … against the Foundations of Orthodoxy* (*Index quarundam novitatum … contra orthodoxa fundamenta* [1673]; ES: VII.1686c; GW5, pp. 52–56) – a list of thirty-one supposed errors in that work. The religious coloration of some accusations made it a dangerous matter for Pufendorf, yet he managed through his court connections to suppress both the *Index* and the relentless attacks in Lund (Beckmann was eventually banished). Still, the *Index* led to the prohibition of Pufendorf’s work in Saxony and embroiled him in bitter conflicts with professors there for many years. His articulate responses during this prolonged debate, including his self-justificatory autobiography, were later published under the title of *Scandinavian Quarrel* (ES: VII.1686c). This collection not only reveals Pufendorf as a consummate polemicist but is also valuable for the clarification of important points in his natural law treatises, and as an entry into the bitter debates (in Germany) between secular natural lawyers and the Lutheran scholastics and theologians whom they challenged (Palladini 2023)[^palladini-2023].

In 1673 Pufendorf published *On the Duty of Man and Citizen According to Natural Law* (DO: VII.1673, III.1991), a short pedagogical compendium based on DJN that guaranteed him a place in university curricula for a century. Moreover, a collection of his dissertations from both the Heidelberg and Lund periods appeared in 1675 as *Select Academic Dissertations* (DAS: VII.1675b, GW7 2025). That year also saw the appearance of a lengthy political tract (*Discussion of Certain Writers of Brandenburg* [Discussio: VII.1675a; KVS: III.1995, pp. 235–336]) justifying Sweden’s entry into the Dutch War (1672–1678) on the side of France, particularly its unprovoked attack (1674) on Brandenburg. This war also occasioned a Danish invasion of Scania, including Lund, and even though the young Charles XI (1655–1697) defeated the invaders, the university remained closed for years **(**1676–81**)**. So Pufendorf moved to Stockholm (1677), assuming the duties of royal Swedish historiographer and, later, privy councillor and private secretary (1682) to the queen, Ulrika Eleonora. He continued to cultivate his relations with the Swedish ruling class, wrote additional political opinions (including *Dissertation on the Alliances between Sweden and France* [Occas: VII.1680; KVS: II.1995, pp. 337–85; Saunders 2009)[^saunders-2009], and began the laborious archival work that underlay his large histories of Sweden (Gust Ad: VII.1686a, C Gust: VII.1696). These accounts focused mainly on external, international affairs (Krawczuk 2014, Olden-Jørgensen 2023)[^krawczuk-2014]. However, Pufendorf’s detailed knowledge of internal Swedish politics is evident in *Les anecdotes de Suède* (VII.1716, VIII.1719), which was written during Sweden’s turn toward royal absolutism in the 1680s (the *Reduktion*). That piece is typically attributed to Esaias, though there is an autograph in Samuel’s hand (Döring 2012, p. 199, n. 58; Bérenger 1993)[^bérenger-1993][^döring-2012].

Pufendorf had also lectured on history at Lund and Heidelberg, and in 1682 after an authorized Swedish version (by Petro Brask) of this material in 1680, he issued (one of only two works in German) *An Introduction to the History of the Principal Kingdoms and States of Europe* (Einl: VII.1682, V.2013). The twelfth chapter of this work contains a long, critical account of the papacy that had appeared pseudonymously several years earlier: *Basilii Hyperetae historische und politische Beschreibung der geistlichen Monarchie des Stuhls zu Rom* (VII.1679). And through its 1695 English version it subsequently acquired a thirteenth chapter, which is a actually a condensation – by the translator (Jodocus Crull, V.2013) – of the independent volume on Sweden (Cont Einl*:* VII.1686b, VI.1976]) which Pufendorf had added to the *Introduction* in 1686. Both *Introductions* underwent multiple editions in many languages well into the 18th century. During the early 1680s Pufendorf also prepared a significantly revised edition of DJN (VII.1684) which emphasized the similarities to Cumberland and Stoicism (Palladini 2020, Saastamoinen 1999–2000)[^palladini-2020][^palladini-2000][^saastamoinen-1999] to make its problematic Hobbesian inheritance less obvious and, perhaps, his life easier. This was reissued in 1688.

Berlin: During one of his trips (1684) to the Continent to gather archival materials and see to the publication of his *Twenty-six Books of Commentary on Swedish Affairs from the Expedition of King Gustav Adolph into Germany up to the Abdication of* [*Queen*] *Christina* (Gust Ad: VII.1686a), Pufendorf entered into negotiations with the Great Elector, Frederick William of Brandenburg, who issued a formal invitation in 1686. This was doubly attractive at the time. For not only had Brandenburg (*de facto*) replaced Sweden as the main defender of an increasingly beleaguered Protestantism, now squeezed between Austria and France, but Sweden’s internal affairs had taken a sharp turn to the right, toward monarchical absolutism and religious conservatism. In Berlin, by contrast, Frederick William’s long-standing toleration politics (Seidler 2003)[^seidler-2003] met Louis XIV’s recent revocation of the Edict of Nantes with the Edict of Potsdam (1685), which invited the fleeing Huguenots to his territories. Also in Stockholm, despite Pufendorf’s still close relations to the royal family, some of his main supporters at court had died or been dismissed, and his brother Esaias – whose pro-French stance was now out of favor – would soon leave Swedish service (1687) and be sentenced to death *in absentia* (1689). Among the last works (late 1687) written by Pufendorf in Sweden was a defense of Cartesianism (in the sense of independent philosophical inquiry) against the Swedish clergy, who had sought to ban it from the universities there (KVS: III.1995, pp. 432–47), and *Of the Nature and Qualification of Religion in Reference to Civil Society* (Hab: VII.1687, V.2002b), a treatise that condemned the French dragonnades and argued for the formal separation of church and state as a means to religious toleration.

Given the Swedes’ past hostilities with Brandenburg, Pufendorf’s thorough knowledge of their archives, and the fact that he had yet to publish his history of Charles X. Gustav, they were loath to see him move to Berlin. However, after considerable delay he was given what was technically a leave of absence (Döring 1996b, Riches 2004, 2013)[^döring-2004][^döring-1996b][^riches-2013][^riches-2004]. He arrived in Berlin in early 1688, actually to prepare a history of Frederick William (who died later that year) but formally as an aulic and privy councillor, positions with greater status at court (and better pay) than a royal historian. During the next six years Pufendorf completed his history of the Great Elector (Fr Wilh: VII.1695b) and began an account of his successor, Frederick III (1657–1713), which is notable for its long (and often unregarded) narrative of the 1688 English Revolution (VII.1784, Seidler 1996)[^seidler-1996]. Moreover, he prepared a revised edition of *The Present State of Germany* (published by Nikolaus Gundling in 1706) and he wrote *The Divine Feudal Law* (Fec: VII.1695a, V.2002a), a work on inter-confessional reconciliation (among Lutherans and Calvinists) and, barring that, religious and political toleration. In mid-1694 Pufendorf returned briefly to Sweden to arrange for the publication of his *On the Deeds of Charles Gustav, King of Sweden* (C Gust: VII.1696), to collect a baronetcy (thus, his *Freiherr* status), and to conduct some diplomatic business between the two monarchs. A stroke while in Stockholm greatly reduced his capacities, and a serious infection contracted back in Berlin led his death there on Oct. 26, 1694. Quite appropriately, given his life-long interest in lay theology and his involvement with informal religious gatherings called *collegia pietatis*, as well as his emphasis on the biblical and moral dimensions of religion rather than its doctrinal aspects, the funeral sermon at the Nikolai-Kirche, where he lies buried, was delivered by his close friend, Philipp Jakob Spener (1635–1705), one of the founders of German Pietism (Döring 1994a)[^döring-1994a].

## 2. The Method of Modern Natural Law

### 2.1 Modern Natural Law

The intellectual tradition with which Pufendorf was associated, and to which he was later seen as central, has been variously termed modern, secular, or Protestant. Indeed, the first chapter of his *Sample of Controversies* (*Specimen controversiarum*, 1678; in ES: GW5) originated the historiography of the genre. (See section 5.2 below.) To be sure, as critics of that self-interpretation have pointed out and Pufendorf himself admitted, elements of that approach were already present in previous traditions, including Stoicism, Roman law, Christian and scholastic thought (particularly Vitoria, Vasquez, De Soto, and Suarez – see Aichele 2014, for instance)[^aichele-2014], as well as the Grotius commentary literature (Tierney 1997, Brett 1997, Haakonssen 1998 and 1999, Seelmann 2000, Oakley 2005, Behme 1996, Hartung 2008, Reibstein 1953–54)[^behme-1996][^brett-1997][^haakonssen-2000][^haakonssen-1999][^haakonssen-1998][^haakonssen-1996][^hartung-2008][^oakley-2005][^reibstein-1953][^seelmann-2000][^tierney-1997]. Still, Pufendorf and those who shared his outlook claimed a kind of Baconian novelty for their enterprise. This lay, they thought, in its eschewal of metaphysics and theology as foundations for philosophy (Tully 1991)[^tully-1991], in its rejection of appeals to authority, and in its assertion of an ‘eclectic’ privilege not only to mix and compare different perspectives but also to place new bodies of knowledge on a so-called scientific footing (BW #137, I.1996, p.194; Hochstrasser 2000)[^hochstrasser-2000]. The founder (in the sense of first practitioner) of this so-called “noble discipline of natural law” was according to Pufendorf “the incomparable Hugo Grotius,” who “set out to construct a work wherein he was not ruled by the influence of his predecessors” and that resisted the fragmenting tendencies of discrepant opinions (ES: I.2002, p. 126). Pufendorf’s assessment was seconded by his French translator Jean Barbeyrac (1674–1744) who noted, too, in his famous Preface (1706, §§ 28–29), that with Bacon and Grotius “the Science of Morality was … rais’d again from the Dead” (DJN: VI.2005 [1729], pp. 78–79), and that it was Pufendorf who had corrected, improved, and systematized it (§31).

The controversial beginning (and eventual *locus classicus*) of the new approach, which aspired to rigor and persuasion in morals and politics as Descartes had sought them for science and metaphysics, was Grotius’s “Preliminary Discourse” or “Prolegomena” (§§ 5–8) to *On the Right of War and Peace* (1625, IX.2005). For there, the Dutch classicist, ambassador, and corporate counsel for the Dutch East India Company challenged the moral relativism and associated egoism propounded by the ancient skeptic, Carneades, by laying out the basic requirements of communal living. Unlike Carneades, his concerns were not so much epistemological or ontological but rather the mitigation of real-world conflicts and the maintenance of peace. Appealing to a so-called principle of sociality or sociability (understood as both fact and norm), he sought to identify the most general or minimal, and thus most widely acceptable requirements (rights, duties, laws) of human association – norms which could be discovered even if, as he dared to say (*etiamsi daremus*), one supposed that there is no God (Grotius 2005, p. 1748)[^grotius-2005]. These focused mainly, in his own case, on international matters of war and peace, but in other natural lawyers like Pufendorf they addressed the entire range of human affairs. The modernity of the project, as well as its secularism, lay as noted in the intentional avoidance of biblical, theological, and confessional presuppositions (Tully 1991)[^tully-1991], and in the rejection of the essentialism and associated teleology of classical (abstract) realism – which were thought to generate or exacerbate rather than to resolve such controversies (Forbes 1975, Tuck 1979 and 1987, Todescan 2001, Ekardt and Richter 2006, Hunter 2001)[^ekardt-2006][^forbes-1975][^hunter-2001][^tuck-1987][^tuck-1979]. Instead, and in the manner of the physical sciences, the new discipline devised explanations that emulated mathematical reasoning and appealed to concrete observation, attempting thereby to create a shared outlook possessing theoretical coherence, empirical plausibility, and pragmatic effect (Röd 1970, Dufour 1980)[^dufour-1980][^röd-1970].

This reading of the modern natural law project was promoted by Barbeyrac, and it has been persuasively articulated by Tuck (1979, 1987)[^tuck-1987][^tuck-1979]. However, aside from concerns about its casting of Grotius and Hobbes (Mautner 2005, Sommerville 2001, Zagorin 2000)[^mautner-2005][^mautner-2000][^sommerville-2001][^zagorin-2000], it also seems wanting as an interpretation of Pufendorf, at least by being insufficiently attentive to his immediate social and intellectual contexts. These included the political and confessional status of the Empire and, more specifically, the metaphysical, theological, and strictly Lutheran culture of Scandinavia and northern Germany, Saxony in particular – as represented by conservative, Platonizing scholastics like Johann Benedict Carpzov, Adam Scherzer, Valentin Veltheim, and Valentin Alberti, with whom Pufendorf and his younger colleague Christian Thomasius (1655–1728) contended through much of their academic lives (Hunter 2007b, 2001; Palladini 1978, 2023; Sparn 1976)[^hunter-2007b][^hunter-2001][^palladini-2023][^palladini-1978][^sparn-1976]. These contestations were immediate and practical, not only scholarly or theoretical, and they continued to roil German society for another century up to and including Kant (Hunter 2006, 2013b, 2009)[^hunter-2013b][^hunter-2009][^hunter-2006]. For they involved, beside efforts to deconfessionalize and, more broadly, ‘secularize’ the state (Cerman 2023, Hunter 2015)[^cerman-2023][^hunter-2015], a struggle over formative participation in its basic institutions (judiciary, academy, church), pitting those who would (continue to) require a certain kind of cognitive, spiritual, and moral grooming against others whose orientation was more permissive and pragmatic (Friedeburg 2008, Dufour 2005, Schmoeckel 2013b, Haakonssen 2002)[^dufour-2005][^friedeburg-2008][^friedeburg-2005][^haakonssen-2002][^schmoeckel-2013b].

Clearly, early modern Protestantism was in this as well as other respects a house divided, pitting those who adhered to tradition, doctrine, sect (party), or authority against innovators who rejected such exclusive mindsets. It is this latter stream, of course – to which Grotius, Hobbes, Pufendorf, C. Thomasius, (and sometimes Wolff) are seen to belong – that is intended by the characterization of early modern natural law as ‘[P]rotestant’ (seemingly in a double sense). Because of its eschewal of static universalisms – all somehow transcendently insulated or purportedly given – and its attention to the particular, the irregular and the mutable, it is also aptly described as ‘individualistic’ (Haakonssen 2004)[^haakonssen-2004]. And this feature links it, in turn, to the characteristic ‘voluntarism’ of the genre (Schneewind 1998, 1996b; Haakonssen 1998)[^haakonssen-1998][^schneewind-1998][^schneewind-1996b], in contrast to the generally ‘intellectualist’ bent of historically prior natural law (Haakonssen and Seidler 2015)[^haakonssen-2015][^seidler-2015]. For voluntarism acknowledged not only God’s supreme and singular omnipotence (exercised through unique, individualistic willing), and the consequent (to us) arbitrariness, contingency, and cognitive imperviousness of the (created) world; but also humans’ individual and collective power over and thus responsibility for their own thoughts, actions, and the institutions facilitating and regulating these. In sum, it leaves less for God and more for humans (of all stripes) ‘to do’. This realization also appears to be a sort of response to Carneades’ challenge.

### 2.2 Pufendorf’s Philosophical Method

Like other early modern reformist schemes, Pufendorf’s project faced the central question of method in more specific terms. Initially he followed the example of Weigel, his Cartesian mentor (whose own *Arithmetic Description of Moral Wisdom* would appear only in 1674; see IX.2003), by employing a quasi-mathematical approach that aspired to demonstrative certitude in moral matters in place of Aristotelian probabilism (Behme 2016)[^behme-2016]. Thus, the early EJU (1660) was formally divided into Definitions (Bk.1) and Principles (Bk.2), with the latter subdivided into Axioms and Observations. The truth and necessity of the Axioms flowed directly “from reason itself” and a “bare intuition of the mind,” without the apprehension of particulars, or interpersonal discussion, while the certainty of the Observations was known by the “collation and perception of individual things corresponding to one another,” and by “common sense and experience” (EJU: GW3, pp. 6, 117; Dufour 1980, Röd 1970, Bach 2013)[^bach-2013][^dufour-1980][^röd-1970].

However, Pufendorf soon adapted this approach. Though he claimed still to favor a ‘mathematical’ over an empirical (“lubrica, infinita, et vere invia”) approach when writing to Johann Christian von Boineburg (1622–1672) in January 1663 (BW #16, GW1, p. 24), criticisms by Hermann Conring (1601–1681) and especially Johann Heinrich Boecler (1611–1672; see Palladini 1997a, Gango 2022)[^gango-2022][^palladini-1997a], which urged him to include consideration of other writers, particularly the ancients, soon led to a change in style. Thus, beginning already with his dissertations at Heidelberg, and then in the major natural law writings at Lund that emerged therefrom, Pufendorf eliminated the formal scaffolding of the *mos geometricus* and interacted more explicitly and directly with other, historical authors. This new approach retained the ideals of systematic rigor and probative power, but in a less formalized, merely quasi-mathematical way. Closer to the reasoning of the physical sciences (including medicine – one of his abiding interests) wherein experimental evidence and deductive coherence sought mutual accommodation, it drew on the deep humanistic legacy in which Pufendorf had been steeped as both source and confirmation of an abductively established principle of sociality (Seidler 2023)[^seidler-2023]. Here Pufendorf could draw on his considerable erudition (both DJN and DAS cite hundreds of authors and works; Palladini 1999a)[^palladini-1999a] – in a transitional culture where a reputation for being learned remained important – while avoiding mere commentary or appeal to traditional authority, procedures which he explicitly dismissed. Instead, he now deployed a so-called ‘eclectic’ method that still involved rational analysis and argument, to be sure, but that also acknowledged the contingency of both his worldly subject matter and its ongoing intellectual re/construal (Hochstrasser 2000)[^hochstrasser-2000]. His goal remained systemic understanding and demonstrative certitude, but one informed by widely shared and reflectively sifted experience (derived from history and contemporary events), together yielding a more empirically grounded, realistic (think Thucydides, Tacitus, Machiavelli, Lipsius, Hobbes), and pragmatically useful moral and political argument.

## 3. Moral Philosophy

Pufendorf’s ethical voluntarism is articulated through the notion of “moral entities” (*entia moralia*, derived from Weigel), to which most of EJU Book I is devoted (Röd 1969, Kobusch 1996, Lutterbeck 2009, Hunter 2019)[^hunter-2019][^hunter-2019-2][^kobusch-1996][^lutterbeck-2009][^röd-1969]. Moral entities specify the basic terms, concepts, categories, distinctions, and classifications – in a sense, the metaethical grammar and vocabulary – out of which shared moral discourse is constituted. Conversely, they designate the ontological correlates or referents of this discourse, which are reliant on and yet distinct from the merely physical beings or settings in which they actually inhere (Statu #1: III.1990, pp. 83–84, 109–10, for the compositional metaphor). Much of the machinery of moral entities was carried forward from EJU to DJN (though not the simplified DO) where, after being formally elaborated in the early chapters, it clearly structures the whole work.

### 3.1 Moral Entities

Pufendorf begins DJN by noting the relative inattention to moral entities in comparison to the much-studied objects of the empirical sciences (i.e., physical entities), even though “it greatly behooves man to know the nature of such entities, which he has received the faculty to produce, and whose power deeply suffuses itself throughout his life” (I.1.1; trans. III.1994, p. 99). He calls them “modes” – in accord with their dependence on other, self-subsistent entities (cf. Forde 2011, on Lockean mixed modes)[^forde-2011] – and says that they are invented (*inventis*) by reflective beings who, by comparing things, form certain notions “superadded” to or “imposed” on the world in order to direct or temper free and voluntary human acts (DJN I.1.2–3). Some moral entities are said to “flow naturally from things themselves, as it were, while others are superadded to physical things … by an intelligent power” (DJN I.1.3; III.1994, p. 100). This is a relative distinction within Pufendorf’s general voluntarist framework, wherein God is thought to impose a basic normative dimension on the world (conjointly and compatibly with its creation), but where humans must – to survive and flourish – devise further distinctions to regulate their lives together – both in philosophizing about and by enacting concrete practices and institutions to regulate them. Accordingly, one might describe the former as foundational or structural categories, and the latter as subsidiary, instrumental, or discretionary tools of analysis. All moral entities, however, of whatever type, depend for their existence on the will of intelligent beings who externally assign normative, action-directing significance to things intrinsically lacking this, but nonetheless somehow compatible with or receptive to it.

There are four types of moral entities, says Pufendorf, analogous to categories applied to physical things: (1) states or conditions (*status*), (2) substances (specifically, persons), and modes in a more specific sense – namely, (3) moral qualities (affective/effective modes) and (4) moral quantities (estimative modes). As in the physical realm, moral ‘states’ designate a kind of (moral-legal) space in which persons (like bodies) operate and orient themselves; they are sub-posed by/to the latter even though they also, in a sense, depend on them for their existence. Pufendorf distinguishes two such states, natural and adventitious, the former referring to the natural state occupied by all humans as such (thus, the “state of humanity”), and the latter to the special conditions, institutions, or standing (e.g., cultural, commercial, marital, civil) that differentially accompany it, in being created by and imposed on particular sorts of persons. Personhood or moral substantiality refers in turn to the various roles or agencies that humans play or assume in such spaces, either simply as individuals or as composites or collectives, and either on their own behalf or for others (e.g., parents, citizens, sovereigns, ambassadors). Since we typically enact multiple, overlapping moral personae it is possible for these to conflict, not only in the case of individuals but also collectives like economic associations, religious groups, and political entities like states, empires, or confederations. It is therefore important to assess and rank them in terms of their relative moral importance, and to articulate their respective obligations and rights. Clearly, this analysis continues the work of Grotius, but in a wider, more organized and systematized way.

There is also need for moral qualities and quantities, which are respectively termed affective and estimative modes. The latter involve the valuation of persons, things, or actions in terms of their social status or esteem, their price (economic value), or their desert (as in punishment and reward) – all of which are comparative, inexact, and subject to alteration or adjustment: i.e., moral imposition is an ongoing process. Moral qualities in turn affect (have an effect on) persons and are either formal (e.g., mere titles) or operative, with the latter subdivided into passive and active varieties. Passive operative qualities enable someone “rightly to have, suffer, admit, or receive something” (DJN I.1.20), while active operative qualities are those by which we can morally affect or move others, as it were. (See Auer 2008, for an association with Hohfeld.)[^auer-2008] They are divided into the more familiar notions of power/authority (*potestas*), obligation (*obligatio*), and right (*ius*) – which constitute the moral levers of social life. All such notions or entities, it is important to keep in mind, are imposed or assigned rather than given or found; the entire scheme is fundamentally voluntarist, positivistic, or brutally factitious (at least to us) – which is what so discomfited Leibniz and Kant.

The inherited term ‘right’ (*ius*) is ambiguous, says Pufendorf, and it sometimes stands for law (*lex*). Though it may also be classed as a passive moral quality (as in allowing someone rightly [*recte*] to receive things), it is active insofar as it permits us to command persons and possess things. It is similar, in the latter respect, to power or authority (*potestas*), which is subdivided into four types depending on whether it is wielded over persons or things – either one’s own or those of others. Authority over one’s own person and actions is freedom (*libertas*), and over those of others sovereignty or command (*imperium*); moral power over one’s own things is ownership (*dominium*), and over those of others lordship (*servitus*). Each kind of power may be acquired, lost, regained or held in certain ways: for example, the efficacy or strength of authority may be ‘perfect’ (complete, exceptionless, enforceable) or ‘imperfect’ (limited, flexible, merely hortatory or desirable), as may the force of rights; and the rest of DJN is essentially an account of how this plays out in the different areas (spaces, states) of life. The influence of Pufendorf’s distinctions and their exhaustive application to particular life sectors was enormous, creating the basic scaffolding of modern moral discourse (Hruschka 1998b, 1986)[^hruschka-1998b][^hruschka-1986].

### 3.2 Moral Necessity

Obligation refers to the “moral necessity” at work in such relationships, by which one is “bound” to do, admit, or suffer something (DJN I.1.21), and thus it applies to all spheres where authority, right, or other forms of moral normativity operate. Pufendorf clarifies obligation at DJN I.6, in his discussion of law, sanction, and the claims of lawgivers. Law differs, he says there (referring to Hobbes, *On the Citizen* [*De cive* 1642] 14.1), from counsel, agreement, and right alike, because by it an obligation is “introduced into a man’s mind by a superior, that is by someone who not only has the strength to threaten some evil against those who resist him, but also legitimate reasons allowing him to demand that our freedom be restricted at his discretion” (DJN I.6.9; III.1994, p.123). That is, obligation has a dual aspect involving both reason and strength, neither of which can separately give rise to it: the former generates reverence or respect in those who are (or take themselves to be) obligated, and the latter fear – both in regard to perceived human interests. And both are needed, since reverence without fear does not explain the (internal) compellingness of obligation, while fear without reverence does not explain its ‘rationality’ or legitimacy (DJN III.4.6). This analysis applies to obligation under both divine and human law, and in the former case, especially, it raises the question of whether voluntarism, intellectualism, or an amalgam of these offers a more adequate account of moral duty. The matter has been much debated, starting with Leibniz’s critique (1706, in IX.1972) and Barbeyrac’s defense (1718, in V.2003) of Pufendorf (see Schneewind 1996a, Hunter 2003 and 2004, Lipscomb 2005, Hartung 1998, Bach 2015b, Haakonssen 1998, Grunert 2000, Ramelet 2020)[^bach-2015b][^grunert-2000][^haakonssen-2000][^haakonssen-1998][^hartung-1998][^hunter-2004][^hunter-2003][^lipscomb-2005][^ramelet-2020][^schneewind-1998][^schneewind-1996a], and continuing still in recent discussions of normativity (see section 5.3 below) that refer back to those earlier treatments (Darwall 2012, Irwin 2011a–b, Pink 2009)[^darwall-2012][^irwin-2011a][^pink-2009].

More so than Grotius, Pufendorf clearly distinguished natural and moral goods (DJN I.2.6), maintaining that the former are morally indifferent even if generally agreed upon by humans (DJN I.4.4, II.3.4). Thus, since moral norms are not directly linked to the intrinsic natures or purposes of things, or derived from a cumulative outcome of actions already assumed to be morally compelling (i.e., maximized good), he was neither a teleologist nor a consequentialist in ethics strictly speaking (Hunter 2019, Dawson 2020)[^dawson-2020][^hunter-2019][^hunter-2019-2]. Rather, the so-called natural goodness or evil of things – their ability to benefit or harm us – provides the rationale for the imposition of moral entities by intelligent beings from which obligation springs. That is, they have (especially in the case of natural goods) a sort of evidentiary function, explaining why the obliger wills such-and-such, and why the obligee has “good reasons” (or might be motivated) to comply. Thus, when discussing moral persons Pufendorf says that the impositions which produce that rank or status should have a positive effect (*solidus effectus*) on humankind and not be made frivolously, as when Caligula declared his horse a senator, when the ancient Romans deified their emperors, and when papists, still, declare saints by a similar sort of post-mortem canonization – all with normative impacts (DJN I.1.15). Similarly, when explaining the legitimating aspect of commands or obligations (human or divine), or that in them which evokes our reverence, respect, or willingness to obey, Pufendorf stresses the commanding party’s ability, desire, or record of having benefitted those on whom the obligation lies (DJN I.6.12, DO I.5). In sum, while natural good and evil are not the source of obligation per se, and moral necessity is not hypothetically linked to their attainment, they do explain why there is such a thing as obligation at all, or what the point of it is. Moreover, since what benefits or harms humans is known through shared experience, empirical investigation, and a knowledge of the past (history) and present (diplomacy, travelogues, literature, etc.), these operate as heuristics for determining our obligations and explain why, in some cases, we disagree about them (Bach 2015a)[^bach-2015a]. Of course, such differences do not concern whether moral commands as such formally obligate or not (they do: by definition), but whether specific actions are in fact morally commanded, as well as by whom. That at least some of them in fact are (Pufendorf allowed for indifferent actions as well), and that in particular moral spaces or contexts we can usually determine which, is a basic assumption of his theory.

### 3.3 The Natural State

The concept of a natural state is so important to Pufendorf’s theory that he considered political tracts which ignore it “gravely defective” (Statu #1, III.1990, p. 110). The idea is discussed in no less than five of his works (EJU, DJN, DO, DAS, and ES), which basically agree on its characterization and distinctions (Seidler 1990, Medick 1973, Palladini 2008)[^medick-1973][^palladini-2008][^seidler-1990]. DJN alone contains two versions: one (II.2) to establish the general need for moral norms among humans, and the other (VII.1) to justify civil authority or political sovereignty (*imperium*). The two treatments share a common conception of legal obligation as justified (for “good reasons”) imposition by a presumptively beneficent albeit sanctioning superior. This does not make them redundant, however, even if the political institutions created to eliminate the pre-civil state of nature are enjoined by same natural law that also commands (or induces) humans to leave or avoid the prior, pre-cultural (i.e., brutish) state. For civil sovereignty and its mechanisms are needed precisely because – given human’s intractable imperfection and depravity (DJN II.1.6, 4.1) – the cooperative institutions (e.g., language, property, contract, marriage, family, household) that lift them out of their previous, natural condition eventually create analogous coordination problems, as it were. Indeed, similar difficulties arise among civil states themselves, in an inter-national state of nature, creating the need for yet another (fourth) solution at that level (Linklater 1980; Devetak 2015; Fiorillo 2017, 2016)[^devetak-2015][^fiorillo-2017][^fiorillo-2016][^linklater-1980]. In sum, Pufendorf’s overlapping conceptions of morality, politics, and international law are each set against corresponding and likewise intersecting natural states; this facilitates the respective analyses of these, though the remaining differences also prevent their standardization.

The first DJN treatment of the natural state is preceded by a chapter (II.1) on the real-world importance of law. This discussion provides a bridge between the conceptual analysis of law and obligation in Book I, and the following chapter (II.2) on the natural state, which leads in turn to the derivation of the natural law in DJN II.3. In it, Pufendorf presents a concrete moral anthropology suggesting why and what kinds of moral entities, and what laws, should be imposed on humans in view of “the natural inclinations and pursuits of us mortals” (DJN II.1.6; III.1994, p. 138). The latter reveal – especially in comparison to beasts – that humans’ greater freedom would actually be disadvantageous to them without further regulation, or, that unbounded freedom or license is self-defeating. For humans are weak, contradictory, and perverse: not only anxious, ambitious, envious, superstitious, resentful, vain, vindictive, and the like; but often – even under the best of circumstances – at odds with one another and even themselves. Yet unlike other living things they are also unable to secure their needs without the aid of creatures like themselves. For the latter part of this push-pull relationship to be possible, however, and to avoid the difficulties continually generated by the prior traits, human action must be constrained by laws of freedom, as it were. Without these, humans would sink (even) below brutes (whose welfare is non-voluntarily secured by physical laws) and remain at liberty only to disrupt and destroy one another. This sort of inconsistency in human nature – which would contradict God’s providential intention that human action display a certain “order, decorum, or beauty” (DJN II.1.5; III.1994, p. 138) was on religious, rational, prudential, and it seems aesthetic grounds unacceptable to Pufendorf.

The natural state is a theoretical mechanism for further articulating this problem and its possible solution. Always a relative notion, it appears in three distinct but overlapping modalities that map loosely onto the three (four) conditions above. Each also involves a particular polarity or implied contrast: the natural state toward God (*ad Deum*), that in regard to oneself (*in se*), and that toward other human beings (*ad alios homines*). The first of these designates the human condition as such, conceived negatively as a “state of humanity” differing from both the amoral condition of beasts but also that of a perfect God. Humans emerge from it – using their raw but distinctive abilities (which Pufendorf associates with “soul”) – by improving themselves through social interaction and gradually defining a normative sphere of rights and duties formally imposed by their creator. The second natural state – with respect to oneself (*in se*) – designates the rudimentary helplessness of solitary humans and their almost inevitable lapse into an uncultivated, sub-human life without mutual assistance. Classical accounts of this state depict individual humans as frustrated in their most basic needs and desires, and as incapable of productively refining their special faculties by exercising their freedom. Lawful interaction facilitating cooperation (leading to technical and cultural growth) can and does to some extent eliminate the natural state in this sense. Yet as the third natural state shows, increasingly cultivated humans may still threaten, endanger, or interfere with one another unless their individual and collective freedoms are further regulated by law. Here law means more than the self-interpreted, self-imposed (internal) moral law already operative in the first two states of nature (which, *pace* Hobbes, are in fact not entirely lawless or amoral); rather it refers to the civil mechanisms wielded by political superiors with both the authority and the power to command. Hence this third natural state is best described as pre-civil, in contrast to its pre-cultural and merely human (not divine) antecedents. Note: in none of the three states does ‘natural’ function normatively in the sense of an ideal to be recovered or still realized, as in some other forms of natural law theory. Instead, norms emerge as humans leave such contrastive natural conditions (Rüdiger 2010, Hunter 2023a)[^hunter-2023a][^rüdiger-2010].

As previously suggested, the three natural conditions and their remedies overlap. Imperfect humans always remain (somewhat tenuously) in the initial state of mere humanity (DJN II.2.9–10), even when the other two natural states have been partially sublated by culture and politics. For, neither beasts nor gods, they lack the automatism of the former and the spontaneous goodness of the latter, and always carry a burden of self-control. The latter two states may coincide as well, though also incompletely, in that humans may exit a pre-cultural state of need while remaining in a pre-civil state of insecurity, or they may enjoy the security of a civil state while relatively deprived of cultural goods. As well, even though need satisfaction and cultural development typically occur before and apart from political order, in cities as opposed to states, the latter sometimes seem a precondition of the former – as Pufendorf knew well from his experiences during the Thirty Years War.

All natural states, therefore, but particularly the pre-cultural and pre-civil instances, are either full/perfect or partial/limited. The former sort are purely mythical or hypothetical, according to Pufendorf, in that no humans can exist entirely without their fellows. Complete non-cooperation or hostility are simply impossible since no one would survive, and the only reason to consider such fictional scenarios is as heuristic devices highlighting the conditions that humans actually inhabit (Behme 2009a, Evrigenis and Somos 2024, Grewal 2022)[^behme-2024][^behme-2022][^behme-2009a][^evrigenis-2024][^grewal-2022][^somos-2022], and the ways they might be improved. Indeed, as Rousseau iterated in his own way, extreme versions of the natural state are mere conjectures or extrapolations from the limited or mixed conditions found in actual human experience – as when rival families, clans, and states, or formerly cultivated and civil-ized individuals (e.g., Crusoe), are temporarily left to their own devices. Much of Pufendorf’s discussion on the topic engages Grotius, Hobbes, and Spinoza, whose comments on the natural state he duly appropriates, alters, or rejects. Thus, Hobbes was correct about a full, pure, or perfect natural state, which would be barbarous, bellicose, and deadly. However, as Pufendorf says in rhetorical opposition, he rather overstated the case based on selective evidence, as any real natural state is actually a state of peace (DJN II.2.7) – albeit an imperfect, unstable, and insecure peace (DJN II.2.12). Accordingly, too, humans cannot ever – at least in this life – entirely leave the natural state (in any sense) behind, for they are always imperfectly socialized, partially cultivated, and incompletely or inadequately civil-ized (politic-ized) (Ploof 2023)[^ploof-2023]. This explains their lifelong subjection to moral law and their need – Pufendorf thought – for some sort of civil subjugation. That is, insofar as they manage to survive and thrive at all, they do so through incompletely realized norms of social cooperation that must (in a variety of ways) be constantly re/constructed, maintained, and improved.

### 3.4 The Law of Nature

Accordingly, “the most common rule [*regula*]” of actions, called the right (*ius*) or law (*lex*) of nature, enjoins that humans “must, inasmuch as [they] can, cultivate and maintain toward others a peaceable sociality that is consistent with the native character [*indoles*, fr. *inolesc*o = to grow on] and end [*scopus* = target] of humankind in general” (DJN II.3.1 and 3.15; III.1994, pp. 148, 152). This is not typical metaphysical language. For the law does not rest on a rationally discerned configuration of natures or essences, an intrinsic morality of actions, or the absolute value of persons in such a scheme, nor merely on common agreement among humans or the long-term utility that generally follows compliance with its injunctions (DJN II.3.4, 7–8,10) – though the latter two are indirectly relevant (Puerto 1998, Fiorillo 2026)[^fiorillo-2026][^puerto-1998]. Rather, it converges on a divine superior who is taken to enjoin precisely that which right reason can itself discover to be in humans’ interest (DJN II.3.19). That is, while the law’s concrete stipulations or practical ends are specified by right reason informed about “man’s nature, condition, and inclinations, … other things external to man, especially those capable of benefiting or harming him in some way[,] … and the kinds of assistance and restraint he needs” (DJN II.3.14; III.1994, p. 151), its normative character as law depends formally on the will of a super-human authority also equipped with the power of sanction (DJN II.3.19–20). Pufendorf grants that the latter role remains “somewhat obscure” to those who rely on the light of reason alone, for natural sanctions are imperfect and supernatural ones beyond reason’s purview (DJN II.3.21, III.4.6). Still, the classic voluntarist problem of arbitrary stipulation is seemingly avoided by positing (or proving – by natural theology [DO I.4]) divine benevolence (providence) and consistency: that is, human nature and the law regulating it are willed together by God for human benefit (DJN II.3.4–5). And the audacity of this claim, in turn, may be mitigated by transcribing it into a different, more agnostic key: to wit, one could refer (in place of divine providence) to the benignity of things, as it were, namely the assumption that the world is (like God) not always or entirely against us. Both versions can be partially supported by actual experience (historical and contemporary), which is in any case the only (indirect) way to discern the law’s requirements and (thus) God’s will (DJN II.3.12).

Pufendorf acknowledged the fundamental reality of self-love and the possibility of reading the natural law as a merely instrumental rule. However, he denied that self-love is the only human motive or necessarily in conflict with other motives, and argued that its aims can be fully achieved only through the natural law (DJN II.3.14 and 18). That is, humans are not totally selfish or totally altruistic but partially both; hence natural law does not forbid the pursuit of self-interest but merely regulates it, enjoining both the care of self and the care for others that humans already pursue (albeit in limited and inadequate ways). In fact, if human nature tended toward one or other extreme the law’s injunctions would be either impossible or pointless: it is precisely humans’ medial nature, their motivationally mixed constitution, that accounts for the law’s prescriptive load in both rational and sanctional respects. The argument is certainly hospitable to its voluntarist (theistic) premise, but it relies as well on the compatible (and confirmatory) deliverances of general human experience. In practice, given the stakes involved according to either interpretation, it may often not matter which is emphasized. This was undoubtedly why (beside its non-theological foundation) Pufendorf’s more perceptive religious critics resisted it and accused him of Hobbism, and why in turn his secular adherents were not repelled by the deity in the background.

### 3.5 Types of Duties

Toward the end of his discussion of the natural law’s main principle, Pufendorf joins Hobbes in subsuming international (*ius gentium*) under natural law (DJN II.3.23) – which is critical for understanding both – and makes some distinctions that shape the rest of the work (Reibstein 1955, Jensen 2023, Edelstein 2008, Boisen 2024)[^boisen-2024][^edelstein-2008][^jensen-2023][^reibstein-1955]. Like Grotius, he contrasts “matters which the natural law disposes by *direct precept or prohibition* from those that reason has persuaded men to institute on account of sociality, or that may be undertaken by virtue of a license flowing directly from these institutions – in which case they are said to have a *reductive relation* to the natural law” (DJN II.3.22; III.1994, p. 156; emphasis added). More formatively, he divides the law’s injunctions – loosely reflecting the divisions of the natural state – into those toward God (*adversus Deum*), toward ourselves (*adversus seipsum*), and toward other humans (*adversus alios homines*). The first category is treated separately in the pedagogically oriented DO (I.4), written while Pufendorf was fighting charges of atheism in Lund. However, DJN (II.4.3–4, III.4.4) folds it into the second category, particularly the law’s (i.e., God’s) command that we cultivate our minds by developing appropriate beliefs about God through careful consideration of the supposedly convincing arguments of natural theology. The third category’s precepts regarding other humans are divided into absolute and hypothetical injunctions, the former obligating all of them as such, whatever their status, role, or condition, and the latter – as in the direct/reductive distinction above – presupposing certain states or institutions introduced by them (DJN II.3.24). Hypothetical injunctions are also distinguished from positive civil laws that may appeal to them for justification, even if based on particular states’ advantage or specific legislators’ volitions. The most important human institutions or practices governed by hypothetical laws of nature are speech, property, price (the valuation of things), promising, and contract; and then the different kinds of sovereignty or rule (including the civil state) – which are respectively the subjects of DJN IV–V and VI–VIII. Book II itself concludes with three more chapters treating the various duties toward ourselves (II.4), the right and limits of self-defense (II.5), and the right of necessity (II.6).

Before turning to various kinds of hypothetical duties in Book III, Pufendorf examines several absolute requirements toward other humans. The first is the prime natural law directive – also found in Grotius and Hobbes – without which social life could not exist: that no one should injure another and, if they have, that reparation should be made (DJN III.1). Second, since injury can be done to others not only by damaging their bodies or things, but also their esteem or value as persons, humans must show mutual respect and treat one another as natural equals: for “one cannot lead a social life with someone by whom one is not esteemed at least as a man, …” (DJN III.2.1; III.1994, p. 159; Haara and Saastamoinen 2024; Blank 2025, 2022a)[^blank-2025][^blank-2022a][^haara-2024][^haara-2024-2][^saastamoinen-2024]. Here it is important to note that the the basis of this requirement is not a shared human nature regarded metaphysically (or transcendentally) as an absolute value, but a so-called equality of right or law (*ius*) consisting merely of our joint obligation (per natural law) to cultivate a social life (DJN III.2.2; (Fiorillo 2013a; Saastamoinen 2006, 2010; Auer 2015; Behme 2024; Haakonssen 2010)[^auer-2015][^behme-2024][^fiorillo-2013a][^haakonssen-2010][^saastamoinen-2010][^saastamoinen-2006] – which is practically impossible without mutual respect. Human moral equality so conceived also leads Pufendorf to reject the notion of natural slavery, even though he allows for servitude as a kind of contractual subjection in both pre-civil and civil society, as well as in warfare (DJN III.2.8–9). Third, beside observing the minimalist requirements not to injure or insult others, Pufendorf notes that one must also seek to benefit them, “so that I am glad that others who share my nature also live upon this earth” (DJN III.3.1; III.1994, p. 165). This introduces the important distinction between perfect and imperfect duties, the latter consisting of so-called duties of humanity which, though owed in the same sense as perfect duties, cannot usually be compelled. There are a number of interesting discussions here, addressing the right of passage “for legitimate reasons” (*justas causas*, DJN III.3.5), the duty of hospitality, and the right of immigration – some of which recur in the fuller treatment of international law at the end of DJN VIII. Duties of humanity often depend on special circumstances. Thus, Pufendorf could consistently approve of Frederick William’s liberal (and self-serving) pro-Huguenot immigration policy in Brandenburg while supporting Charles XI’s tighter restrictions in Sweden (KVS: III.1995, pp. 472, 505; Hab: GW6, §54, Devetak 2007)[^devetak-2007]. Moreover, in the immediate context he disagrees with Vitoria (DJN III.3.9) by limiting the so-called right of commerce (including territorial access for such purposes), especially as it was used by Europeans to justify their conquest of America (Cavallar 2002, 2008; Hunter 2007a; Bach 2015a)[^bach-2015a][^cavallar-2008][^cavallar-2002][^hunter-2007a].

Since neither perfect nor imperfect duties of humanity suffice for maintaining social relations, particularly those involving more exact mutual expectations, Pufendorf introduces (DJN III.4) another class of absolute duties arising from pacts among individuals. The natural law stipulates no specifics here but merely commands that we enter into some such consensual arrangements, since without them sociality would be hampered and humans remain in the natural state. Such pact-generated obligations are both adventitious or based upon some antecedent human deed responsive to circumstances, and perfect in the sense of specific and compellable. They arise from either unilateral promises or bilateral agreements, and they create perfect rights (i.e., justified expectations of exact performance) in others (DJN I.7.7). It appears in this context that Pufendorf is an obligation theorist in that rights derive from obligations (DJN III.5.1; cf. VIII.3.5), yet there are also rights without corresponding obligations; that is, correlativity exists, but incompletely (Mautner 1991, 1989)[^mautner-1991][^mautner-1989]. He also rejects once more Hobbes’s notion of a right to all things and maintains that rights claims to natural objects must rest, if they are to have normative force, on the express or tacit consent of others (DJN III.5.2–3): that is, they arise out of social interaction and are not presupposed by it. Given its importance for the development of human social life, five more chapters are devoted to the topic of promises and pacts: on their nature, subject-matter, types, and conditionality, as well as the sorts of individual and collective agents capable of generating consent-based obligations by promising or agreeing with one another. The discussion as a whole is fundamental to the hypothetical laws of nature articulated in the rest of the work, in the context of various consensually created, adventitious human institutions.

### 3.6 Speech, Property, and Commerce

Since humans’ emergence from the pre-cultural and pre-civil states of nature is neither strictly concomitant or parallel, nor sequential, the development of DJN after Book III cannot be strictly continuous in either a topical or temporal sense. However, it does fall into two partially overlapping sectors. Books IV–V deal with human efforts to leave the natural state of neediness (*indigentia*), while Books VI–VIII address the problem of security created by their weakness (*imbecillitas*). Both require the creation of new, complex, and formally organized kinds of human cooperation that address both the growing diversity and inconsistency of human desires, and the increase in human numbers.

Fundamental to both developments is the institution of human speech and the rules that make it effective, or possible (DJN IV.1; Hochstrasser 2000; Aarsleff 2011; Piirimäe 2023)[^aarsleff-2011][^hochstrasser-2000][^piirimäe-2023]. For without speech in the sense of symbolic communication human sociality would remain at a primitive and unsustainable level, in that the terms of human cooperation could not be articulated and agreed upon. Conversely – and hinting at the vague providentialism that Pufendorf substitutes for traditional metaphysical teleology – speech, or the communication of thought to others via sound, would be “of hardly any use” without a social life (DJN IV.1.1). Unlike natural signs or associations (e.g., dawn, smoke, groans), speech is an artificial construct of humans over time: learned rather than congenital, and contingent or accidental instead of naturally uniform; it is a clear instance of the willful imposition (perhaps by mere acceptance) of moral entities upon the world so as to give rise to new obligations (DJN IV.1.4–5). Its utility depends upon (tacit or express) consent and on the concomitant requirements of consistency and veracity, which are both instrumentally and – insofar as humans are obligated to be social – morally necessary. However, though humans are bound by the general conventions of language and the requirement of not lying to one another, they retain a certain flexibility of action based on others’ varying right to know one’s thoughts, and the actual consequences of such revelations. Thus Pufendorf distinguishes between lies (*mendacia*) and untruths (*falsiloquia*) and also allows for noninjurious deceptions (DJN IV.1.9). This proviso applies not only to everyday life and its “harmless arts of simulation and dissimulation,” but also to the realm of politics and international relations where candor, carelessness, or naivete may be counterproductive and defeat the injunctions of sociality (DJN IV.1.17, Martin 2009)[^martin-2009]. Pufendorf returns to it repeatedly in discussing the duties of (esp. contemporary) historians (Seidler 1997, Piirimäe 2008, Olden-Jørgensen 2023)[^piirimäe-2023][^piirimäe-2008][^seidler-2023][^seidler-1997].

It is clear in this as well as other discussions how Pufendorf’s anthropological and social realism allows or actually requires a certain flexibility in articulating context-specific obligations of the natural law, even or especially within particular human institutions created to enact sociality. It is because of this flexibility and consequent slipperiness of language that he goes on to treat of oaths (DJN IV.2; *De fide*, in DAS: GW7) which, presuming “human diffidence, infidelity, ignorance, and impotence” (DJN IV.2.2), are a special means for assuring others (through associated divine or civil sanctions) of the reliability of our speech acts and the promises and pacts that rest thereon. Of course, since oaths themselves involve presumptions and tacit conditions arising from the nature of particular cases (DJN IV.2.14), they too may require further regulation. Thus, like other human institutions based on pacts, the rules of speech both antedate civil sovereignty but come eventually to depend on it in certain ways as well.

A fundamental use of speech is to regulate humans’ use of external things by appeal to a right of appropriation or dominion over them. This rests on things’ necessity or utility for human life, and their presumed (or divinely intended) availability for man’s use because of these features (DJN IV.3.1–3; Puerto 1998)[^puerto-1998]. Property is not inherent or a given, however, but a moral quality imposed on things, and it has a moral effect on other humans rather than (or beside) a physical impact on the things or animals owned (DJN IV.4.1). That is, it establishes a justified social relationship among humans (solitary Adam’s relation to things was not ownership per se, and could not be transmitted) based on express or tacit agreements that may vary according to circumstances, so long as the ends of human sociality are served thereby (DJN IV.4.4). Before or without such agreements, worldly objects are in a state of negative community, meaning that they belong to no one in particular but lie open for anyone to use as needed (DJN IV.4.2–3, 4.13).

While Pufendorf acknowledges a kind of primitive community within family units, under the aegis of the father, he distinguishes this from positive communion in the sense of common ownership (Buckle 1991, Piirimäe 2023)[^buckle-1991][^piirimäe-2023]. Both this and private dominion properly speaking arise gradually with the proliferation and dispersion of family units, as humans and their needs multiply (DJN IV.4.12). For under such conditions it becomes necessary to distinguish mine from thine through certain exclusionary conventions, if quarrels are to be avoided. Typically (but optionally), humans agree to a right of first occupancy or direct use, limited by the proviso of actual utility and the ability to defend what is occupied (DJN IV.6.3) – though this does not suffice for ownership without consent. Some things like the open oceans may (or can) not be owned, and so peaceful navigation thereon “belongs to everyone and is free.” (Nine 2022, 2018)[^nine-2022][^nine-2018] However, other kinds of exclusive restrictions such as unequal trade relationships among nations are permitted, and “that vaunted freedom of trade does not prevent a state from favoring its own citizens over outsiders” (DJN IV.5.9–10). Also, since property is a moral relationship rather than a physical quality, the same things may be subject to different types of overlapping ownership (DJN IV.4.2). Dominion need not be exclusive, and humans may grant others a perfect or imperfect right over their possessions. Indeed, they may be obligated to do so (DJN IV.8).

Since owned objects serve different uses and are not equally valued by everyone, their exchange requires a common measure of comparison to determine their so-called moral quantity or price (DJN V.1.1–2). Price is either ordinary or eminent, the latter determined by money as an independent or objective unit of value. Money (in whatever form) is explicitly created to serve the ends of exchange among both individuals and states, and without it commercial pacts, or contracts, regarding both goods and services are impossible (DJN V.2.4). While civil rulers have a general authority to regulate property relationships in their realms (particularly specific forms of acquisition like hunting, fishing, and mining), as well as trade with outsiders, they are less free to determine the relative price of things since such arbitrariness would disrupt economic exchange, especially interstate commerce (DJN IV.6.5; V.1.14). In general, sovereign determinations are less apt or useful in pre- or non-civil institutions that relieve human need, than in regard to those that affect human security – if these can be distinguished. Nonetheless, under certain circumstances economic tools like contracts may need a civil sanction to insure their reliability and thus efficacy; and rulers may (indeed, must) always regulate property insofar as it affects the state’s basic security interests and welfare – not only by taxation and eminent domain but also through controls on foreign trade and luxury (e.g., sumptuary laws), whereby the state’s economic strength and independence are affected (DJN VII.4.7, VIII.5; Saether 1996, 2017; Gaertner 2005; Hont 1987, 2026; Young 2008; Skinner 1995; Seidler 2018; Grewal 2022)[^gaertner-2005][^grewal-2022][^hont-2026][^hont-1987][^saether-2017][^saether-1996][^seidler-2018][^skinner-1995][^young-2008].

## 4. Political Philosophy

### 4.1 Pre-Civil Societies

Like physical bodies, the moral body of the state is substantively composed of lesser members, particularly the simple and thus primary associations (*collegia*) of marriage, family, and household (DJN VI.1.1). In their reproductive, rearing, and economic functions, these basic societies mainly address the problem of human want or need, though they also offer a minimal level of security. Their existence shows that social cooperation is not suddenly imposed on isolated human beings with the creation of states, but that the latter is an additional form of congregation introduced to protect and maintain already existing social units. Despite the natural inclinations that induce individuals to form or enter them, these proto-civil arrangements are like states based on agreements, and they exhibit quasi-authority relations more or less analogous to civil sovereignty (DJN VI.2.11). The latter becomes necessary only when pre-civil societies increase in size, number, and complexity to the point where their interactions become dysfunctional and security problems unmanageable. As in the case of other moral entities superimposed on one another, states do not replace or eliminate pre-civil societies but only protect, order, and – under certain conditions – utilize them. At the same time, especially in the case of other types of sub-state associations such as churches, they may also liberate individuals from them (by divorcing civil from religious authority, and allowing religious diversity in the state) (Palladini 1997b, Zurbuchen 2013, Sparn 2023)[^palladini-2023][^palladini-1997b][^sparn-2023][^zurbuchen-2023][^zurbuchen-2013].

#### 4.1.1 Marriage

Since it regulates reproduction, without which human society would cease to exist, the institution of marriage is “the foundation of social life” (DJN VI.1.7; Dufour 1972; Ehrle 1952; Zurbuchen 2023, 2022)[^dufour-1972][^ehrle-1952][^zurbuchen-2023][^zurbuchen-2022]. Humans are obligated to enter it, though only in a general way that presupposes capacity, opportunity, and other facilitating conditions. While formally commanded by God – like other natural law obligations – its immediate origins lie in each instance in a consensual pact between basically equal partners (DJN VI.1.12). This holds even where a woman’s (express or tacit) consent – like terms of captivity in general – follows upon (a just) war (DJN VI.1.9, 1.12; VI.3.5–6). Though the end of marriage differs from that of the state (reproduction vs. security), its unification of wills may also establish a hierarchical, quasi-sovereign relationship between spouses, one of whom agrees to submit to the other’s authority; on the other hand, marriage may have the character of an “unequal confederation” that requires diverse but mutual performance (i.e., command and obedience; DJN VI.1.11). What is impermissible in both cases and institutions, however, because of its supposed dysfunctionality for governance, is a divided command or two heads.

This forces a choice upon Pufendorf’s contractual egalitarianism, which is tempered here by his substantive assumptions about the “preeminence” of the husband’s sex, and about women’s allegedly unreasonable demand for additional responsibilities. These are not absolute pronouncements, however, but conclusions of the “ruling prudence” that is the “pleasant sister of natural law”; that is, they rest upon an empirical, pragmatic assessment of spouses’ respective abilities and burdens (DJN VI.1.11, 1.18). It is important to recall in this context the difference between modern and medieval natural law, and that Pufendorf does not conceive human nature in essentialist terms but rather generalizes (rightly or wrongly) from experience. That is, apart from personal presuppositions, this is an empirical and not a metaphysical argument, making it both circumstantial and corrigible. To dismiss it out of hand in search of greater indefeasibility may ironically reaffirm the categorical approach that Pufendorf rejected, and overlook the inherent flexibility and liberatory potential of his conception of natural law (Drakopoulou 2013)[^drakopoulou-2013].

Besides, Pufendorf reassures us, in marriages and states alike, sovereignty does not exclude the possibility of mutual affection, and – more importantly – spouses do not command each other in all respects but only those that serve the institution’s fundamental end. Thus, matters like the disposition of a wife’s dowry and other assets depend on the stipulations of the original marriage agreement (DJN VI.1.11), much like the preliminary constraints imposed upon limited civil sovereignty by a state’s fundamental laws or other, specific agreements. Pufendorf also extends his analysis into the controversial subject of polygamy – which he deemed rationally elusive and sometimes permissible (DJN VI.1.16–18; Vogel 1991; Heydt 2018d)[^heydt-2018d][^vogel-1991] – and into the topic of divorce, where he qualifies Milton’s views on the subject (DJN VI.1.24; Palladini 2001)[^palladini-2001]. These and other discussions are historically and circumstantially embedded, which accounts for their considerable length and detail – especially when compared with shorter treatments in other early modern authors like Hobbes and Locke. Comparative appraisals among them can be useful if the respective contexts are kept in view (Sreedhar 2014, Doyé 2012, Hull 1996, Buchholz 1988, Rinkens 1971, Dufour 1972, Odzuck 2024)[^buchholz-1988][^doyé-2012][^dufour-1972][^hull-1996][^odzuck-2024][^rinkens-1971][^sreedhar-2014].

#### 4.1.2 Family

One human institution generates and qualifies another as the parental role supervenes on that of marital partner. Here Pufendorf follows Hobbes in holding that mere generation cannot ground parental authority (just as place of birth alone does not give rise to patriotic duty), and that this depends rather on a tacit pact motivated by utility (DJN VI.2.2). That is, children supposedly assent to their subjection in order to survive, and because otherwise the beneficial ends of parenthood, which include the rearing, socializing, and civic formation of offspring, cannot be attained. Moreover, he concurs, though mothers have primary authority over children in the state of nature where parents are roughly equal, fathers typically acquire supremacy through marriage pacts, which may be dictated by civil laws (DJN VI.2.4–5). As in the spousal relationship and for similar reasons, parents cannot be equally authoritative or sovereign vis-a-vis their children, but one or the other must prevail. Though this is usually the male, Pufendorf excepts Amazons and female rulers in whom the civil sovereignty reposes for dynastic reasons, and whose husbands are not kings but mere “spouses of queens” (DJN VI.2.5). Also, as with marital authority, parental power is limited by the institution’s end and normally excludes the right of life and death, as well as the ability to void grown children’s marriages (DJN VI.2.14). It ceases when children come of age and are emancipated, though (as in the case of emigration, DJN VIII.11.3–4) it may be extended beyond that point because of special circumstances, including other pacts. Children always owe their parents imperfect duties of equity and gratitude (DJN VI.2.11).

#### 4.1.3 Household

Herilic or master-slave relationships are similar to but stricter than parental ties, and they have no natural expiration point (Saastamoinen 2024)[^saastamoinen-2024]. They too are based on express or tacit pacts, with no natural basis beside the qualifying suitability of some persons to rule and others to be ruled (DJN VI.3.1–2) – which, alone, are insufficient to justify them. Pufendorf conceives them as sovereignty- rather than as property-relationships (DJN VI.3.7) strictly speaking, and regards slaves not as things but as “perpetual mercenaries” (DJN VI.3.4) who always retain an “intrinsic liberty of the will” (DJN VI.3.10). Slavery (or servitude – the category is wider and more diverse than often acknowledged; Mallinckrodt 2023)[^mallinckrodt-2023] does not extinguish the laws of humanity or erase all traces of humans’ original equality, even though it is possible for them to bargain away (permanently) their freedom to command themselves (DJN VI.3.9). Even the offspring of slaves may be retained in that status, especially when it has been entered through war, although humanity (vs. right) favors their liberation. States may regulate herilic relationships like marital and parental ties, for appropriate reasons, even though they often do not interfere. Slavery may be ended by manumission, banishment, a failure to specify the status of slaves in one’s will, and – notably – by putting a slave into chains (except for a crime, or as punishment). In the last case and any other unjustified deprivation of bodily freedom, a slave is released from his initial, pact-based obligation, which depends on reciprocity: “For there is no pact unless contractors trust each other, and faith cannot be violated where it does not exist.” That is, physical bonds replace or sublate moral ones, and in such a case a slave is free to go (i.e., run away) (DJN VI.3.6, 3.11). These reasons are similar to those why the civil bond between ruler and ruled may be broken. Also to be noted is that, as with marriage and family, Pufendorf’s discussion of slavery was squarely situated within the historical debates on the topic (Franke 2009, Leppin 2016, Ressel 2016)[^franke-2009][^leppin-2016][^ressel-2016] and – because of its frank address of complicated issues – went on to influence eighteenth-century treatments such as those of Carmichael and Hutcheson, which went beyond it (Jorati 2024)[^jorati-2024].

### 4.2 The State

Pufendorf’s political philosophy or doctrine of the state, including the latter’s internal and external functions, is continuous and consistent with his ethics; in fact, they are not really distinct. Both rest on the same natural law foundation, namely the sociality law which regulates not only pre-civil relations, institutions, and societies but also the civil condition needed to secure them. As thoroughly social beings who are incapable of living alone, humans are subject to sociality’s requirements at all stages of their lives, both temporally and organizationally, and the establishment of political authority does not leave morality and its obligations behind but rather facilitates and extends their reach. The tempered realism of the political sphere is not a forced abandonment of moral norms but an articulation of the natural law’s demands amid the increasing complexities of collective human existence, including its inevitable constraints, exclusions, and compromises. This means as well that the so-called private sphere of morality, conscience, and even religiosity is not independent of broader social and political concerns – which it should support and not obstruct (*De concordia verae politicae cum religione Christiana* [1673], DAS: GW7). That is, in Pufendorf’s natural law theory politics is another form of social ethics. (Note: this relationship differs from that of natural law and positivism in modern jurisprudence, where natural law, at least, is typically understood in a way that Pufendorf – as a kind of positivist – rejected [see Boisen 2024].)[^boisen-2024]

#### 4.2.1 The Origin of the State

Speaking generally (since the formation of individual states varies greatly), the moral entity of ‘the state’ is self-imposed by humans at the (hypothetical) command of natural law when their pre-civil marital, parental, and particularly herilic associations – as well as other pact-based institutions such as property – become dysfunctional because of multiplying conflicts that lead increasingly to the unjustified use of force and, thus, reciprocal injury and insecurity. It is a defensive and precautionary response to such emergent conditions, a kind of cooperative scheme (*conspiratio*) created as needed against the growing threat posed by other, organized human beings (DJN VII.2.1–2). Contra Aristotle, the state is not a natural or ideal stage of human development emerging spontaneously from humans’ desire to live together (DJN VII.1.2–3), but an adventitious, willed self-help device broadly enjoined by natural law in either or both a prudential (i.e., recommended by sound reason) or deontological (i.e., commanded by God) sense (DJN VII.3.2). It is not required to eliminate human need (*indigentia*) in the pre-cultural natural state, since cities and other forms of association usually suffice for that (DJN VII.1.3 and 6; Brett 2011)[^brett-2011]. Rather it secures these social arrangements and their cultural gains in the context of increased human numbers, relative scarcity and competition, growing disagreements, and the ensuing insecurity that comes from the interaction of individually weak and imperfectly social beings.

State formation is explicitly driven by fear, and there is ample reference to human wickedness, self-concern, and a willingness to injure in Pufendorf’s sketch of the pre-civil condition (DJN VII.1–2). Still, his comments are qualified even here, compared with Hobbes, and they do not amount to a sweeping anthropological claim about the total (*de facto* or *in se*) selfishness of all human beings – which is not needed for the argument (DJN VII.1.5, 7–8; but see Palladini 2020)[^palladini-2020]. In fact, the discussion of pre-civil conditions and institutions clearly tempers such pessimism. For while those arrangements rely on formal promises and pacts, they are also induced by and certainly compatible with mutual affection, a desire for friendship, and the enjoyment of conviviality (DJN VII.1.5, 2.4; VIII.4.3, 6.2; II.2.7, 3.16). To be sure, such means and motives become increasingly incapable of maintaining them, as the complex feelings regulating smaller, simpler, and more intimate human interactions evolve into a colder and cruder psychology of scale among composite moral persons or groups. That is, with the reduction of intimacy, familiarity, predictability, and commonality of purpose comes a sort of primitivizing of motives in the direction of wariness and fear. Still, Pufendorf’s state is not built on a thick, essentialist, teleological, or religious anthropology, even of a negative sort (Saastamoinen 1995)[^saastamoinen-1995], but on an empirical, historical, and realistic assessment of actual human affairs. This means both that the state’s minimalist function as a mutual protection agency may not be controlled or overridden by other, substantive (e.g., religious, ethical, cultural) ends, but also that – since fear does not necessarily eliminate other human motives but only suppresses or distorts or overrules them – the state as guarantor of mutual security may actually allow these to function in their proper (subordinate) contexts. Surely, this aspect of modern natural law earns it a place in the history of political liberalism (Krieger 1957, Fenske 2013, Hunter 2009, Berest 2011b, Ramet 2023)[^fenske-2013][^hunter-2009][^hunter-2023][^krieger-1957][^ramet-2023].

#### 4.2.2 Contractualism

Beside its sovereignty, what distinguishes Pufendorf’s state from other pact-based institutions is its size (DJN VII.2.2) – that is, its numbers must suffice for security in particular social and historical circumstances – and, more importantly, its manner of formation. Conceptually, at least, the state owes its origin to two agreements and an intervening decree (DJN VII.2.7–8). An initial contract of association occurs when members of a multitude, or the would-be citizens (in practice, family-fathers or patriarchs, who are the primary or direct citizens: DJN VII.2.20, 5.12), agree with one another to bond together for mutual security. This requires the accession of all (full or formal) members of the resultant group, who consent to it either absolutely or conditionally depending on whether they agree individually to bind themselves to any form of state selected by the majority, or only to a certain, preapproved form satisfactory to themselves (Ramelet 2020)[^ramelet-2020]. In either case, the band (*coetus*) thus constituted still lacks unity and has only “the rudiments and beginnings of a state” (DJN VII.2.7; III.1994, p. 212), even if it suffices to prevent an immediate return to the state of nature if the second pact is temporarily suspended, as during an interregnum (DJN VII.7.6–7, VIII.11.1; *De interregnis* [1668], in DAS: GW7).

The second pact follows the selection (by the pragmatic expedient of majority vote; see Pasquino 2010, Schwartzberg 2008)[^pasquino-2010][^schwartzberg-2008] of the specific form of state to be instituted (monarchy, aristocracy, or democracy), and through it each citizen of the future state subjects himself to the specific governing agent thereby established. It is needed to prevent the random defection and individual exceptionalism that threaten all pre-civil agreements (DJN VII.2.3, 2.10), including the first contract. At this stage or level, the individual contractors unite their wills through separate promises of obedience to a new moral persona equipped with distinctive (i.e., not simply transferred) rights and obligations, and capable of using their combined strength to discipline, compel, and thus govern effectively (DJN VII.2.5, 3.4, 8.5; VI.1.12; *De civitate* [1676], in DAS: GW7; Boucher 1998; Skinner 1998, 2002; Holland 2017)[^boucher-1998][^holland-2017][^skinner-2002]. Civil subjection is analogous to herilic subjection or slavery, though more focused in intent, more limited in scope, and – because of reciprocal promises between governors and governed – potentially sublatable in narrowly defined and (thinks Pufendorf) avoidable circumstances (DJN VII.3.1, 2.10). Moreover, in combining a title to command with the strength to sanction or compel, it also recalls the general structure and rationale of the moral obligation that enjoins humans to constitute it in the first place (DJN VII.3.1, 2.13; I.6.9, 6.12; and section 3.2 above).

#### 4.2.3 Sovereignty

Like Bodin and Hobbes, Pufendorf identified sovereignty (*imperium*) as the “vivifying and sustaining soul” of the state. It is a new moral quality that emerges from the respective commitments of rulers and ruled, consisting of the former’s right to command and the latter’s duty to obey (DJN VII.3.1–2: III.1994, pp. 217–18; VII.4.12; Holland 2012; Smith 2022)[^holland-2012][^smith-2022]. Like other souls, as it were, sovereignty comes from God (as author of the natural law), though only indirectly in this case, or through the instrumentality of reasoning human beings (who interpret God’s will in this way). Thus, throughout Book VII of DJN Pufendorf rejects the arguments of J. F. Horn (*Politicorum pars architectonica de civitate* [1664]), the German Filmer. And in his correspondence with C. Thomasius (BW #158 and #192: GW1) he vigorously approves – by sarcastically describing him as ‘fit to hang’ (*henckermäsius* [p. 290]) – Thomasius’ recent (1688) critique of Hector Gottfried Masius (1653–1709, the German theologian and Danish court preacher who had argued that only (Lutheran) monarchs were capable of ensuring civil peace (Grunert 2004, Wenzel 1997)[^grunert-2004][^wenzel-1997]. Instead, Pufendorf held, natural law bestows sovereignty on no one form of government or specific individuals or groups; instead, such decisions are made by human contractors themselves in particular circumstances. It is always required, however, that sovereignty be supreme in the sense that there be no superior or equivalent powers within the state. Also, sovereignty cannot be divided since that would fragment the unity of will undergirding the state as an effective authority (DJN VII.4.11) – which is its *raison d’etre*. Accordingly, all governing functions, including legislative, judicial, penal, economic, and war-related powers must ultimately reside in the same persona or agency. Otherwise (as in the Holy Roman Empire whose political diseases Pufendorf diagnosed in *The Present State of Germany* (Monz 1667) the state would have two or more heads and, by reproducing the conflicts of the pre-civil condition, would invite its own destruction (*De republica irregulari* [1668], in DAS: GW7).

#### 4.2.4 Forms of State

States where sovereignty is unified – whatever its form (monarchy, aristocracy, democracy) – are called regular, and those where it is divided irregular (DJN VII.5.2, 5.14–15). Irregularity always weakens a state, but each form also has its own, peculiar difficulties and weaknesses. Pufendorf does not in principle prefer one regular form over another: God is the author of free states (democracies) and monarchies alike (DJN VII.5.5), he says, and sovereignty or majesty belongs equally to all states and not just to monarchies (DJN VII.3.3; Haakonssen 2023a, Zurbuchen 2009)[^haakonssen-2023a][^zurbuchen-2009]. However, humans incline differently toward particular forms of state (DJN VII.5.11), and it is up to them to decide which ones to implement for themselves (DJN VII.3.2). Given the (to us) contingencies of experience, the choice appears to be prudential or technical in nature: i.e., certain forms of state may in certain situations require specific human dispositions in order to function effectively (DJN VII.6.5); or, more generally, some forms of state may be more suited for particular peoples or settings. (The idea recurs in the American *Federalist Papers* [1788].) Thus, large territories with scattered populations are better ruled as monarchies, while smaller regions or city-states can be governed well as democracies (DJN VII.5.22). Perhaps monarchy or autocracy are more efficient because of the simple logistics of human association (DJN VII.5.9). Beside such forms and conditions, each type of state is also prone to other faults or illnesses (*vitia*) peculiar to itself, which Pufendorf distinguishes into “vices of men” and “vices of status” (DJN VII.5.10). The former obtain, for instance, when (in a monarchy) rulership goes to someone unqualified for or ill-disposed to that role, and the latter “when the laws and institutions of a state are not adjusted to the character of a people or region,” thereby inclining them to various internal disorders (DJN VII.5.10; PW III.1994, p. 228). In the end, even though states are a remedy for human weakness and imperfection, as human constructs they always remain an imperfect one (DJN VII.5.22). So effective state-construction is necessarily, like moral development, an ongoing process.

This problem cannot be avoided or lessened by mixing forms of state, a traditional expedient that Pufendorf rejected because of the irregularities it produces (Riklin 2006, Scattola 2009b, Zurbuchen 2009, Holland 2023)[^holland-2023][^riklin-2006][^scattola-2009b][^zurbuchen-2023][^zurbuchen-2009]. For mixing yields either an entity (like a federation) that is no state at all because it is held together by mere agreement (without subjection), one that approximates an even looser system of independent states, or something ‘monstrous’ in between (DJN VII.5.12–13). Interestingly, Pufendorf allows that this notorious term “implies no insult or injury,” and that there is a place even for monstrous (extremely irregular) forms if no practical, situation-specific alternatives exist (*De republica irregulari* #7, #22; DAS: GW7). Thus, he ends *The Present State of Germany* by suggesting relatively modest tweaks to the Empire rather than an implausible full-scale reorganization (Monz VI.9). Also, in this regard, Pufendorf makes an important distinction between forms of state and forms of administration. Thus, a monarch may utilize executive mechanisms that are aristocratic or democratic in nature, and similarly for other forms of state (DJN VII.5.1, 5.13). The crucial factor in each instance is the unity and locus of ultimate authority, not how its commands are executed or policies implemented. Such arrangements differ further from so-called systems of (sovereign) states, which themselves exhibit regular and irregular forms depending on the way their collective authority is exercised. By discussing them Pufendorf extends his analysis to the international sphere (DJN VII.5.17–21; *De systematibus civitatum* [1667], in DAS: GW7; Jensen 2023, Holland 2024)[^holland-2024][^jensen-2023].

#### 4.2.5 Absolute and Limited Sovereignty

Contra Hobbes, Pufendorf allows that supreme sovereignty (*summum imperium*) may be either absolute or limited (DJN VII.6.7; Holland 2017)[^holland-2017]. The former obtains where a sovereign accepts his (her, or its) authority with no conditions attached, the latter where citizens’ submission depends on the sovereign’s initial acceptance of certain terms, such as an existing state’s fundamental laws. These – as well as the general expectations of legitimate rule – can be violated by the eventual sovereign, making their actions injurious or illegitimate (DJN VII.8.2–4, 6.10), even though subjects are not typically – except *in extremis* – in a position to deny obedience for this reason because it would undermine the important end of sovereignty altogether (DJN VII.8.5–6). So this presents a dilemma – situationally and dispositionally more difficult for Pufendorf, probably, than for Locke’s similar deliberations about rebellion (Seidler 1996)[^seidler-1996]. Given human imperfections, Pufendorf clearly favors limited sovereignty; however, and for similar reasons, while he criticizes the abuse of absolute sovereignty he also denounces irrational fears thereof (DJN VII.6.8–13). Again, there is no universal recommendation but all depends on what is needed in particular historical circumstances where such choices present themselves.

#### 4.2.6 State and Church

The state or sovereign necessarily controls all sub-state collectives, including economic, political, and religious associations, especially as competitors for citizens’ allegiance (DJN VII.2.21–24). Thus, though piety is a personal matter between the deity and individual believers (as Locke also argued), its official mediation by ecclesial entities or churches, which also direct and organize human actions, has a worldly impact that may interfere with the state’s functions (which are divinely enjoined as well) – as Pufendorf argued by referencing the history of Catholicism and the papacy’s meddlesome role in European affairs (Einl: V.2013, ch. 12). Therefore, sovereigns have the right (and the obligation) to regulate religious organizations, to examine beliefs, and even to foster an official religion – though only to insure compatibility with the more basic, political order (DJN VII.4.11, 9.4). In fact, like public schools (if they do not merely perpetuate scholastic subtleties), religion may also aid the process of civic formation – which is required because states are adventitious moral entities devised by humans for a particular purpose, and because (good) citizens are not born but made (DJN VII.1.3–4; Hunter 2010a)[^hunter-2010a]. However, since they are disparate institutions regulating different moral persons (citizens, believers), neither state nor church exists primarily to serve the other’s purposes. The state’s general authority regarding religion, and its sovereignty over all religious groups alike, also creates room for the toleration of religious minorities and thus restrains destabilizing confessional conflict (Seidler 2002, Zurbuchen 2013, Lehmann 2013, Palladini 1997b, Sparn 2023)[^lehmann-2013][^palladini-2023][^palladini-1997b][^seidler-2023][^seidler-2013][^seidler-2002][^sparn-2023][^zurbuchen-2023][^zurbuchen-2013]. Though Pufendorf had not yet arrived at Christian Thomasius’ notion of a deconfessionalized state (Hunter 2007b)[^hunter-2007b], his historical awareness of religion’s impact on government, and its frequent contribution to political factionalism, made him wary of independent religious institutions and inclined to favor state oversight. In any case, since they issue from the same warranting deity, he held that ultimately true politics and true (religious) doctrine cannot conflict (DJN VII.4.8, 4.11; *De concordia verae politicae cum religione Christiana* [1673], DAS: GW7). This general outlook persisted uneasily in German lands until the end of the eighteenth century, when it was challenged by the trans-denominational religiosity of Kantianism – which still inspires versions of philosophical liberalism today (Hunter 2026)[^hunter-2026].

#### 4.2.7 Civil Law

Despite its natural law foundation, a state’s positive laws will only partially overlap with natural law. That is, since its purpose is not to make humans perfect but secure (Rüdiger 2010)[^rüdiger-2010], only those natural laws without which peace among citizens would be impossible or unlikely should also become civil laws (DJN VIII.1.1, III.4.6). In fact, by focusing on the requirements of human association rather than on personal formation, the sociality principle makes Pufendorf a legal minimalist more generally, so that even where the natural law is silent or indifferent “civil laws should not sanction more things than conduce to the good of the citizens [as such] and the state” (DJN VII.9.5: III.1994, p. 242). (This restraint contrasts with other kinds of natural law, which tend rather to be maximalist in this respect.) Besides, and within this narrow focal area, sociality also grants legislators considerable discretion or flexibility of application. Thus, even though civil laws should not explicitly contravene natural law, it is sometimes excusable if they redefine or “overlook” (*dissimulare*) certain practices – like theft, dueling, and tacitly condoned cuckoldry – for which “the condition of the times and the genius of a people” do not provide a current remedy (DJN VIII.1.3). Such practical adjustments are evident throughout Pufendorf’s detailed discussions of state laws in DJN VIII – regarding punishment, social status (esteem), property, and war – which are strongly prudentialist, consequentialist, and realist in character. They evince not a substantive or combative moralism but the same flexible ‘reason-of-state’ approach that also guides his thinking about inter-state relations. Indeed, through their common grounding in the sociality principle, natural, civil, and international law are in continuity and in dialogue with one another – though in a complex, shifting, and sometimes untidy way (DJN II.3.23). That is, though divinely enjoined natural law is presumed to be ultimately consistent, in practice (as specifically interpreted by particular humans) it often appears less so; for its demands must always be articulated in full view of not only human psychology but also the concrete settings in which human action occurs. (Thus, as in the case of immigration [section 3.5 above], Pufendorf could condemn the English revolution of the 1640s while approving the one in 1688 [Seidler 2001].)[^seidler-2001]

#### 4.2.8 Punishment

The same requirement is evident in Pufendorf’s long discussion of the sovereign right to punish (DJN VIII.3; Hüning 2004, 2009b)[^hüning-2009b][^hüning-2004]. Punishment presupposes a superior, and so (vs. Locke) it cannot occur in either pre- or post-civil (international) states of nature (DJN VIII.3.1–2, 3.7). Of course, both individuals and states have a right to protect themselves or wage defensive (and, in limited circumstances, preemptive) war, but neither of these is to be construed as punishment strictly speaking. The latter is, instead, a means of social control available only to sovereigns for the specific purpose of restraint, deterrence, and reform (DJN VIII.3.9). In their hands, moreover, it is more future- than past-oriented (*ex ante* vs. *ex post*), with the latter being important only for the sake of the former: that is, one should not in punishing pointlessly harm someone, even if guilty, if their infraction cannot be undone or the damage repaired, for they remain after all our conspecific or fellow human being (*cognato*) and potentially deserving of esteem (DJN VIII.3.8). To be sure, those who have injured another certainly owe a duty of reparation, as well as assurances about future behavior, but sovereigns are (vs. Kant) under no obligation to impose – or miscreants to accept – punishment (DJN VIII.3.4; see end of 4.1.3 above), which is imposed for purely consequentialist reasons.

Indeed, in agreement with Hobbes, Pufendorf dismisses revenge as a “vain and unreasonable” motive (DJN VIII.3.8) for punishment, and he rejects Selden’s “satisfaction, purgation, or expiation” (DJN VIII.3.12) as unnecessary or meaningless notions. Moreover, he holds that penalties are not “owed to” or “deserved by” criminals, who are unlikely to assert such a right (DJN VIII.3.5, 3.15). Instead, a sovereign’s resort to punishment should be guided by prudential/political state interest (as in the case of a parent, by the interests of the family: DJN VIII.3.10), which also determines the quantity and quality of punishment needed (DJN VIII.3.23–24). Moreover, the latter should never be excessive but guided by the actual advantages to be gained (DJN VII.9.6–7). In some cases, punishment may even be foregone altogether if the welfare of the state so demands (DJN VIII.3.14, 16). This is because punishment is an instance of neither distributive nor commutative (expletive) justice, but a type of administrative sanction recommended by “prudence … conjoined with the duty to rule others.” (DJN VIII.3.5; Haakonssen 2023a)[^haakonssen-2023a] Note: this and similar discussions (about esteem, price, forms of government, etc.) are instances of Pufendorf’s determination of “moral quantities,” where “latitude” is often required (Seidler 2018, Bee and Sternick 2023)[^bee-2023][^seidler-2023][^seidler-2018].

In sum, Pufendorf’s analysis of punishment as a whole reflects the positive and re/constructive outlook of the law of nature, also in pre- and post-civil conditions, particularly its injunctions to forgive, to accept apologies and reparations, and basically to act always so as to further and facilitate sociality (“inasmuch as [we] can”), rather than to frustrate or make it impossible among human beings as we actually find them (DJN V.13.1; II.3.15: III.1994, p. 152). As such, it appears far removed from more absolutist or categorical views of justice, such as the well-known statement of Kant (in *The Metaphysics of Morals* [*Die Metaphysik der Sitten*, 1797]) that a civil society must before dissolving itself execute every convicted murderer remaining in its prisons – a contrast that reveals well the gulf separating Pufendorf’s natural law theory from essentialist, Platonizing views (such as Leibniz’s) that challenged, and the Critical philosophy (of Kant) that eventually replaced it (Hunter 2001, 2026)[^hunter-2026][^hunter-2001].

### 4.3 Natural Law and History

Pufendorf’s natural law thinking developed in close interaction with his views on other subjects such as history, religion, economics, and international relations; that is, his roles as academic philosopher, court historian, royal advisor, and political apologist were only formally and not intellectually distinct. This is as evident in the seminal reflections of his early *Collegium Anthologicum* lectures (KVS: III.1995, pp. 2–86; Döring 1988)[^döring-1988] as in his later works. Thus, the 1667 treatise on the Holy Roman Empire (Monz: VII.1667) not only studied its history and institutions but also examined the importance of sovereignty and state form, the tension between civil and ecclesiastical authority, and the challenges of foreign relations and collective security that emerged from these. Pufendorf’s so-called works on religion (Hab: VII.1687; Fec: VII.1695a; Bas Hyp: VII.1679) engaged similar topics, as well as associated questions about religious unification and toleration, in the context of the early modern confessional state (Parkin and Stanton 2013; Döring 1998; Zurbuchen 1999, 1996, 1991; Hüning 2002)[^döring-1998][^hüning-2002][^parkin-2013][^zurbuchen-1999][^zurbuchen-1996][^zurbuchen-1991]. Even the somewhat partisan defenses of particular states’ interests (Gundaeus: VII.1659, Prodromus: VII.1665, Discussio: VII.1675a, Occas: VII.1680; and the anonymous [possibly by Esaias] *Les anecdotes de Suède* [1680; VII.1716, VIII.1719] – on the merits of Sweden’s French alliance) might be mentioned here. For though they may have been incidental or demand performances, they all endeavor (in their justificatory aims) to apply the natural law’s injunctions to specific historical circumstances. Most obviously, perhaps, Pufendorf’s historical works – including the long accounts of Sweden (VII.1686a, 1696) and of Brandenburg (VII.1695b, 1784) that so distinguished him, but also his popular *Introductions* (VII.1682, 1686b) – give substance and support to his philosophical analysis of the state. To be sure, the focus there is often on the external relations and negotiations of particular states (Piirimäe 2008, Dufour 1991c, Krawczuk 2014)[^dufour-1991c][^krawczuk-2014][^piirimäe-2008]. But sovereigns must conduct these as judiciously and effectively as their own states’ internal affairs, for it is useless to have internal peace if the state is externally weak, or vice versa (DJN VII.4.5). The two dimensions of statecraft are clearly linked throughout Pufendorf’s opus, which urges sovereigns to consult both the long- and short-term interests of states, always distinguishing real from merely apparent advantages (Einl: V.2013, Preface), both to avoid the re/emergence of an internal or external state of nature and, more generally, to satisfy the natural law’s injunction to further sociality among citizens and other humans.

## 5. Influence

### 5.1 Literary Afterlife

Pufendorf’s (Latin) style is clear and direct, though also sophisticated and colorful. For he not only mastered the arts of citation and example but was also an aggressive, imaginative, and sometimes crass polemicist when attacked – especially when cornered and the stakes were high, or when he deemed his opponents to be bellicose, lazy, or intellectually dishonest. This alone assured that he was eagerly and widely read. Many of his works – including Monz (VII.1667), DJN (VII.1672), DO (VII.1673), Einl (VII.1682) – were published repeatedly and translated into many European languages, both during his lifetime and throughout most of the following century (Othmer 1970; Luig 1972; Denzer 1972; Laurent 1982)[^denzer-1972][^laurent-1982][^luig-1972][^othmer-1970], when they became staples of university education – even in Catholic Europe (Cerman 2024, Schmoeckel 2013a, Waldmann 2022, Luna-Fabritius 2020, Fiocchi and Silvestrini 2024)[^cerman-2024][^cerman-2020][^fiocchi-2024][^luna-fabritius-2020][^schmoeckel-2013a][^waldmann-2022]. Of particular note in this extensive publication history were the influential French translations by Jean Barbeyrac (1706; VIII.1729, V.2005), whose long notes to DJN and DO (including their Lockean leanings) were partially assumed into the standard English translations of these works (if not immediately then in later editions), specifically those by Basil Kennett (VIII.1729) and Andrew Tooke (VIII.1735). It has become clearer over time that the role of Barbeyrac’s translations (including their influence on other editions and translations) in spreading Pufendorf’s reach was deeply ironic, in that they were (explicitly) used to present Barbeyrac’s own ideas as well – which often criticize or revise his source. This means, somewhat worryingly, that to read Barbeyrac is – at least sometimes – not necessarily the same as reading Pufendorf (Saunders and Hunter 2003, Haakonssen 2024, Palladini 2026b)[^haakonssen-2024][^hunter-2003][^palladini-2026b][^saunders-2003].

There was in fact considerable British interest in Pufendorf, starting with Tyrrell and Locke (see Tyrrell 1681, esp. pp. 236–59; and Thompson 1987, Waldmann 2021)[^tyrrell-1681][^thompson-1987][^waldmann-2021], not only in the main works but many others as well. This is evident from the early translations of *The Present State of Germany* (1690, 1696; V.2007), *The History of Popedom* (VIII.1691b), the *Introduction* (1695, V.2013), *Of the Nature and Qualification of Religion, in Reference to Civil Society* (1698, V.2002b), and *The Divine Feudal Law* (1703, V.2002a). Many of these have been reissued by Liberty Fund, Inc. as part of its *Natural Law and Enlightenment Classics* series and are available in its *Online Library of Liberty* (sect. V, and *Other Internet Resources*, below). Pufendorf’s three main natural law works (including the EJU) were reprinted and retranslated in the early twentieth century as part of the Carnegie Endowment’s *Classics of International Law* series (now *Hein Legal Classics Library*), and are still occasionally reissued in this form (sect. IV below). However, there was no collective edition of Pufendorf’s works in any language before the nearly completed *Gesammelte Werke* (GW 1996 – ) issued by the Akademie Verlag (Walter de Gruyter). This edition includes (as GW1, 1996) the extant correspondence which, though limited in comparison to other early moderns like Leibniz, Thomasius, and others, is nonetheless very useful, especially in conjunction with the polemical essays in *Eris Scandica* (GW5) which add biographical and contextual background besides better explicating or sharpening the central arguments. The important shorter works published by Döring (KVS: III.1995) are not part of GW, nor to date is Monz – though this has fortunately seen a number of recent editions (sect. II). The latest volume in GW is DAS (GW7 2025), which gathers fourteen Latin dissertations from the Heidelberg and Lund periods whereby the material and method of EJU (GW3) were transformed into the shape they took in DJN (GW4) and DO (GW2). The long, national histories are also available as facsimile reprints or online scans, but they await more careful editors and, perhaps, translators.

### 5.2 18th-Century Presence

The discourse of natural law became the *lingua franca* of 18th-century moral, political, and social (including economic – Hont 2026; Backhaus 2009; Saether 1996, 2017; Skinner 1995)[^backhaus-2009][^hont-2026][^saether-2017][^saether-1996][^skinner-1995] thought (Haakonssen 2012; see section 2.1 above, and the “Natural Law 1625–1850: Database” in *Other Internet Resources* below)[^haakonssen-2012]. Pufendorf himself contributed much to this by initiating the self-referential genre of the ‘history of natural law’, wherein he situated himself in relation to Grotius, Hobbes and other predecessors (in *A Sample of Controversies* [*Specimen controversiarum*], 1678, ch. 1: “On the Origin and Progress of the Discipline of Natural Law”; later included in *Eris Scandica*, VII.1686). His modest beginning was soon enlarged by others, including Johann Franz Budde (1667–1729) at the University of Halle, who issued a *Historia jurisprudence naturalis* in 1695. This motivated many imitators, including Pufendorf’s young friend and disciple, Christian Thomasius (1655–1728), whose *Nearly Complete History of Natural Law* (*Paulo plenior historia juris naturalis*, appeared in 1719. Most famous, perhaps, was Jean Barbeyrac’s “An Historical and Critical Account of the Science of Morality …,” the Preface to his 1706 French translation of Pufendorf’s DJN (VIII.1729, VI.2005). The trend continued for several more decades, as instanced by Glafey’s *Vollständige Geschichte des Rechts der Vernunft* (1739; IX.1965; Hochstrasser 2000, pp. 65–71)[^hochstrasser-2000], and it did much to nurture the idea of (modern) natural law as an identifiable ‘school’ or ‘stream’.

As in Pufendorf’s own account, a major theme of the genre was sociality (sociability, benevolence), which was variously interpreted, criticized, and defended in both secular and religious contexts (Chiodi 2009, Dawson 2019)[^chiodi-2009][^dawson-2019]. That notion’s appeal in the British domain – where a way had been prepared by Richard Cumberland (1632–1718; IX.2005 [1672]; Haakonssen 2000)[^haakonssen-2000], whom Pufendorf cited repeatedly in the 1684 edition of DJN – is demonstrated by Gershom Carmichael’s (1672–1729) influential student edition of Pufendorf’s *De officio* (1718, IX.2002), and by the 1730 inaugural lecture “On the Natural Sociality of Men” (IX.1993) of his student and successor at Glasgow, Francis Hutcheson (1694–1746). The latter’s thought – though often appropriated by a more narrowly conceived “British empiricism” – was strongly influenced by (Continental) natural law as well (Moore and Silverthorne 1983, 1995)[^moore-1995][^moore-1983], as is particularly evident in the recently edited *A System of Moral Philosophy*, his long-delayed and final work (1755; IX.2025), whose very organization follows the familiar Pufendorfian pattern. So too were many other British moralists and social theorists (see the *Natural Law Classics* series more generally), especially in the so-called Scottish Enlightenment, including George Turnbull (1698–1748), John Millar (1735–1801), Thomas Reid (1710–1796), and Adam Smith (1723–1790) (Haakonssen 1996; Moore & Silverthorne 1983; Hont 2005, 1987; Moore 2006; Haara & Lahdenranta 2018; Bee 2022, 2023)[^bee-2023][^bee-2022][^haakonssen-1996][^haara-2018][^haara-2018-2][^hont-2005][^hont-1987][^moore-2006][^moore-1983]. Through them and others, including direct exposure to Pufendorf’s own, reprinted works, natural law thinking also became part of the intellectual inheritance of the American Revolution and of constitutionalism (Welzel 1952, White 1978, Augat 1985, Heideking 1995, Haakonssen 1991, Logan 2013)[^logan-2013][^augat-1985][^haakonssen-1991][^heideking-1995][^welzel-1952][^white-1978].

On the Continent, Pufendorf had a substantial impact on German political thought (Klippel 1976)[^klippel-1976]. His works were soon taught not only in Philosophy but also and especially Law faculties, not only at the University of Halle – the center of Thomasius’ so-called “civil enlightenment” (Hunter 2007b, 2010a)[^hunter-2010a][^hunter-2007b] – but elsewhere as well. This juridical interest in Pufendorf persisted and grew until the latter part of the century (Lestition 1989, 1993; Klippel 2009; J. Schröder 2008; Grote 2018)[^grote-2018][^klippel-2009][^lestition-1993][^lestition-1989][^schröder-2008], providing a counterweight to the revived realist/rationalist tradition of Leibniz and Christian Wolff (1679–1754) (Hunter 2001; Lutterbeck 2002; Haakonssen 2006; Ikadatsu 2002; Ottmann 2006; Schmidt 2007; Schneider 2001)[^haakonssen-2006][^hunter-2001][^ikadatsu-2002][^lutterbeck-2002][^ottmann-2006][^schmidt-2007][^schneider-2001]. Outside the academy, Pufendorf maintained a presence not only through his continuously reissued natural law works but also his historical writings, particularly the innumerable versions of the *Introduction* (VII.1682); this was continuously reprinted, re/translated, and enlarged to suit the times – in even its French, Dutch, Scandinavian, and Russian versions – into the second half of the century (Seidler 2013, pp. 615–28)[^seidler-2013]. Indeed, it was largely through this work and those of its editors, commentators, translators, and imitators – including Nikolaus Hieronymus Gundling (1671–1729) and Johann Peter Lud[e]wig (1668–1743), at Halle – that Pufendorf remained a focus of the intense 18th-century discourse about law, politics, and history, and stayed relevant as an international theorist. His peculiar mix of these topics fed also into the so-called Göttingen (historical) school (Hammerstein 1972, 1986; Vierhaus 1987)[^hammerstein-1986][^hammerstein-1972][^vierhaus-1987] that eventually superceded Halle, and into cameralism (Brückner 1977)[^brückner-1977] and the associated discipline of *Statistik* (from the Italian *statista* [statesman] and *ragion di stato* [reason of state]) – which further articulated the concrete institutions and functions of states, including the *Polizeiwissenschaft* central to Foucault’s account of modernity (Zande 2010, Valera 1986, Pasquino 1986, Nokkala 2019)[^nokkala-2019][^pasquino-1986][^valera-1986][^zande-2010]. It is here, eventually, that we encounter figures like Gottfried Achenwall (1719–1772), on whose natural law texts Kant lectured for many years (IX.1762, 2020; IX.1995; Hruschka 1986, Streidl 2000)[^hruschka-1986][^streidl-2000]. Natural law’s systematic aspirations also underlay the century’s legal codification schemes, which attempted still to base positive on natural law in some sense (Ferronato 2005)[^ferronato-2005]; and it inspired Jean-Jacques Burlamaqui (1694–1748) and Emmerich de Vattel (1714–1767), whose respective works became notable examples of the genre and of international law (Zurbuchen 2019; P. Schröder 2021; Otsing 2024; Hunter 2013a-b, 2010b)[^hunter-2013a][^hunter-2010b][^otsing-2024][^schröder-2021][^zurbuchen-2019]. Moreover, it merited treatment in the *Encyclopédie* (P. Schröder 2001, Burns 1999)[^burns-1999][^schröder-2001][^schröder-1999] and appeared elsewhere in the complex prehistory of the French Revolution (Caradonna 2006, Edelstein 2009)[^caradonna-2006][^edelstein-2009], including Rousseau. Though the latter criticized Pufendorf and other “friends of despotism” – perhaps a reference to natural law’s use to support royal absolutism – he also leaned heavily on many of its conceptions, including Pufendorf’s (McAdam 1963, Derathé 1992, Wokler 1994, Silvestrini 2010, Douglass 2015)[^derathé-1992][^douglass-2015][^mcadam-1963][^silvestrini-2010][^wokler-1994].

### 5.3 Eclipse & Rediscovery

Kant’s Critical philosophy does not distinguish clearly between the different streams of natural law (metaphysical, positivist/constructivist) that it is usually thought to have surpassed. Indeed, when he arrived on the scene the two had already been thoroughly commingled (thanks to Barbeyrac and others), leaving the discipline straddling an uncomfortable position between the rational and the empirical. But its situation now became increasingly precarious as it was subjected to epistemological challenges that it had not been designed to meet, and whose purist, a priori assumptions it would reject. Like much else, Kant folded the metaphysical (Wolffian) version of natural law into the dialectical pre-history of his own system, where it appears in the sterile conflict between dogmatic rationalism and sceptical empiricism. However, he is also aware of another version – which also gets dismissed for other, opposite reasons. This happens in his ethics through the critique of technical and prudential imperatives, and in his political philosophy through the emphasis on so-called pure Right. The crucial opposition emerges clearly in the essay on “Theory and Practice” (1793), which differentiates two kinds of practicality: philosophically principled versus unprincipled or merely pragmatic; and in the essay on “Eternal Peace” (1795), which sets “moral politicians” against “political moralists.” The latter member of each contrast belongs, thinks Kant, among the so-called “wretched comforters” (*leidige Tröster*) – i.e., Grotius, Pufendorf, and Vattel – whose views he rejects as overly flexible, situational, and empirical in comparison to his own, pure, and absolute stance (which he ironically claims to be more ‘practical’ than the other) (Koskenniemi 2009, Cavallar 2011)[^cavallar-2011][^koskenniemi-2011][^koskenniemi-2009]. The scorned approach (which permitted appeals to *raison d’état* and the right of necessity: Dufour 1991c, Meinecke 1998)[^dufour-1991c][^meinecke-1998] had previously been deemed more justified and effective, but to Kant it seemed but a recipe for recurring wars (instead of eternal peace). Hence he replaced it with another universal, necessary, and absolute metaphysics of Right (now in a transcendental mode) precisely of the sort that the sorry comforters had originally opposed because of its interminable social disruptions. Then as now, the resolution of the issue seems to depend on whether a transcendental ontology is theoretically and practically more successful than a realist metaphysics, or whether either is needed for justifiable and acceptable practice.

The actual refutation or, rather, historical erasure of natural law was the work of post-Kantian historians like C. F. Stäudlin (1761–1826), J. G. Buhle (1763–1807), and W. G. Tennemann (1761–1819), whose versions of the history of moral philosophy ironically left Grotius, Pufendorf, and Thomasius (Barbeyrac’s erstwhile protagonists) with only minor roles to play. Even so, natural law remained a presence in the nineteenth century. Thus, Fichte (*The Foundations of Natural Law* [*Grundlage des Naturrechts nach Prinzipien der Wissenschaftslehre*, 1796–97]), Schelling (*A New Deduction of Natural Law* [*Neue Deduction des Naturrechts*, 1797]), and the young Hegel (*The Scientific Ways of Treating Natural Law* [*Über die wissenschaftlichen Behandlungsarten des Naturrechts*, 1802–3]) all published works on “natural law” (*Naturrecht*) – showing that the idiom remained serviceable (Klippel 1997, 2012)[^klippel-2012][^klippel-1997]. In the British domain, however, Bentham’s dismissive attitude toward (Blackstonian) natural/common law, and the comparatively circumscribed ethics of Mill’s utilitarianism, found little connection with modern natural law as a broader social theory. (Sidgwick mentions Pufendorf only in passing.) The rebirth of Thomism in the Catholic world toward the end of the nineteenth century (as the official philosophy of the Church), and the permutation of natural into human rights before and after the First World War, made the disjunction and the amnesia complete (Moyn 2012)[^moyn-2012].

Still, both of these developments (which interacted) served to revive the genre, even if in a different register than the one we have been following. For about half of the twentieth century (in philosophy, law, and international relations) natural law referred to the neo-scholasticism or neo-Thomism initially reinstituted in support of Catholicism (Jacques Maritain, Etienne Gilson, Heinrich Rommen). As such it formed a significant if inexplicit background to the Nuremberg War Trials and the U.N. Rights Declaration of 1948. It was joined after mid-century by the ‘new’ or ‘secular’ natural law of Germain Grisez (1919 – 2018), John Finnis (1940 – ) and Robert P. George (1955 – ), and later as well as differently by the neo-Aristotelian naturalism of Amartya Sen (1933 – ), Martha Nussbaum (1947 – ), and Terence Irwin (1947 – ). More recently still, there has emerged a distinctive neo-Protestant, Reformed, or Evangelical natural law with roots in sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Calvinism (Grabhill 2006; VanDrunen 2010; Witte Jr. 2008, 2014; Covington et al. 2025)[^covington-2025][^grabhill-2006][^witte-2014][^witte-2008]. Many of these approaches involve essentialist claims, accommodate themselves to theology and revealed religion, or together with a Kantian conception of the person still present in John Rawls (1921–2002) and Jürgen Habermas (1929– ), seek to provide objective, universal foundations to discourse about human rights, justice, and dignity, and to support the international organizations (NGOs) and mechanisms (courts) that implement or police them. That is, like their predecessors, they go beyond history by seeking to actuate specific theories and realize particular practices (Hunter 2001, 2026)[^hunter-2026][^hunter-2001].

The last half century has also seen a growing interest in early modern philosophy, including so-called ‘modern’ natural law. In the British-American context this has been largely driven by concerns about (liberal) rights and freedoms (Krieger 1957; Brett 1997; Saastamoinen 2006; Tierneu 1997, 2014; Tuck 1979; Witte, Jr. 2008)[^brett-1997][^krieger-1957][^saastamoinen-2006][^tuck-1979][^witte-2008], while German and other European interests have been mainly focused on legal and historical matters (Klippel 1976, 2009; Klippel and Müller-Luckner 2006)[^klippel-2009][^klippel-1976][^klippel-2006]. In both instances, though, the phenomenon has also brought a resurgence of Pufendorf studies – not just general, historical accounts (Welzel 1958, Krieger 1965, Denzer 1972, Palladini 1978, Laurent 1982, Goyard-Fabre 1994, Döring 1992; Vollhardt 2023)[^denzer-1972][^döring-1992][^goyard-fabre-1994][^krieger-1965][^laurent-1982][^palladini-1992][^palladini-1978][^vollhardt-2023][^welzel-1958] but also more specific or applied discussions dealing with territorial rights, the duty to assist, the right of refuge, the idea of standing, the resolution of disputes, cosmopolitanism, confederations and systems, as well as social roles and vocational service (Hunter, 2007a; Nine 2018, 2022; Mueser 2021; Linklater 1980; Wight 1977; Ramelet 2024; Wiszowaty 2023; Kratochwil 1989; Devetak 2007; Tuck 2013; Moraes 2016; Gay and Lopdrup-Hjorth 2023)[^devetak-2007][^hunter-2007a][^kratochwil-1989][^linklater-1980][^moraes-2016][^mueser-2021][^nine-2022][^nine-2018][^ramelet-2024][^tuck-2013][^wight-1977][^wiszowaty-2023] – not to mention basic questions in economics (Hont 2026, 2005; Backhaus 2009)[^backhaus-2009][^hont-2026][^hont-2005].

On a more abstract, philosophical level the canon has also begun to acknowledge Pufendorf’s place in established ‘from-to’ trajectories, even while remaining divided about what to do with him there. Thus, given the largely kantianized history of recent moral and political thought, there have been significant attempts to find in Pufendorf premonitions of normative notions like (deontological) obligation, duties, rights, dignity, equality, autonomy, and legitimacy (Saastamoinen 2010; Fiorillo 2013a; Müller 2011; Schneewind 1987, 1993, 1998; Darwall 2023b, 2012, 2004; Mihaylova 2022, 2015)[^darwall-2023b][^darwall-2012][^darwall-2004][^fiorillo-2013a][^mihaylova-2022][^mihaylova-2015][^mihaylova-2015-2][^müller-2011][^saastamoinen-2010][^schneewind-1998][^schneewind-1993][^schneewind-1987]. Such interpretations have suggestive textual foundations, but they seem less convincing than accounts such as the above. These take Pufendorf – regarded in his actual historical and philosophical contexts – to have more in common with the options later rejected by Rousseau and Kant – which also come in various contemporary versions. Loosely, these could include the neo-Hobbesianism of Carl Schmitt (1888–1985; P. Schröder 2012)[^schröder-2012], the agonism of Chantal Mouffe (1943 – ) and James Tully (1946 – ), the realism of Raymond Geuss (1946 – ), and even the pragmatic naturalism of Philip Kitcher (1947 – ) and others. Of course such comparisons also remain selective, incomplete, or even speculative for now. Still, like alternative interpretive approaches, they show that the study and assessment of Pufendorf has moved beyond the historical to include the philosophical.
