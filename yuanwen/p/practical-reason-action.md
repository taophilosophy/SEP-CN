# Practical Reason and the Structure of Actions
**First published Wed Aug 24, 2005; substantive revision Mon Jun 8, 2026**

*Millgram, Elijah, Bowman, Margaret*

A wave of recent philosophical work on practical rationality is organized by the following implicit argument: practical reasoning is figuring out what to do; to do is to act; so the forms of practical inference can be derived from the structure or features of action. Now it is not as though earlier work in analytic philosophy had failed to register the connection between action and practical rationality; in fact, practical reasoning was usually picked out as, roughly, reasoning directed toward action. But in the twentieth century, attention moved away from this initial delineation of the subject area to the interplay of beliefs and desires within the mind (instrumentalist theories, including their Davidsonian and Williamsian variants), or to procedures for checking that a plan of action was supported by sufficient yet consistent reasons (Kantian theories), or to the ultra-refined sensibilities of the practically intelligent reasoner (Aristotelian theories). The hallmark of the emerging family of treatments surveyed here is, first, the sustained attention paid to answering the question, “What does it take to be an action (at all)?”; and second, the use made of a distinction between full-fledged action and lesser relatives often referred to as “mere activity” or “mere behavior”. Very schematically, these arguments for a theory of practical reasoning try to show that reasons brought to bear on choice must have some particular logical form if action is not to descend into something less than that.

The purpose of this overview is to provide a map of this territory. Despite their agreement that practical reasoning is where the action is, and a willingness to accord explanatory priority to action theory in developing theories of practical rationality, these theorists differ as to what the most central features of actions are, and accordingly they disagree about what the legitimate patterns of practical inference are. They also differ in their underlying philosophical motivations, as well as in what they take to be the upshots of their views for substantive moral theory. Interchanges between the theorists in it are infrequent, which means this entry will describe the disparate research programs that have adopted its framing argument. The priority is to highlight both their common ground and the ways these programs talk past one another.

The features of action that have come in for the most attention are: its calculative structure, its attributability, its aspiration to be challenging and ambitious, its role in social practice, and its evaluative features. They will be discussed in that order. That permits us to conclude with remarks about the prospects and agenda of this approach to practical deliberation.

## 1. The Calculative View of Action

The most prominent advocates of the calculative view of action are Candace Vogler and Michael Thompson. We will start with Vogler’s version of the view, then proceed to Thompson’s.

Call *psychologism* the claim that the right way to do logic and the theory of rationality more generally is first to figure out how the mind works. Since the early seventies, almost all mainstream work on practical reasoning has been psychologistic in this sense; a representative example is Michael Smith’s argument for the ‘Humean theory of motivation’, which turns on what desires are, and how they work (Smith 1987)[^smith-1987]. Vogler’s treatment of practical rationality (Vogler 2002)[^vogler-2002] is a sharp departure from the common approach, an attempt to replicate the anti-psychologistic turn which Frege and Husserl imparted to work on theoretical rationality in, respectively, the analytic and Continental traditions. She takes herself to be following G. E. M. Anscombe (Vogler 2001)[^vogler-2001], who was in turn following Aquinas. (We will fill in some of the Anscombean backstory below, but otherwise will not take up the historical background to the work under discussion; however, see the entry on medieval theories of practical reason.)

Vogler notices that instrumentalism – the view that all reasons for action are means-end reasons – is the default view in contemporary philosophy. There must, she infers, be a compelling insight at the bottom of it; but instrumentalism has been much-refuted over the past few decades. What these refutations of instrumentalism show, Vogler believes, is that formulating the underlying insight psychologistically (as a thesis about mental operations and the mental states involved in them) results in weak and unsustainable renditions of it. She concludes that to articulate what instrumentalism is getting right, you have to strip away the psychologism.

On one reading of Frege, the proper way to do logic is not to look inward, as it were to the gears of the mind, but outward, to the logical relations that hold between suitably abstract objects, and to read the correct inference patterns off of these Frege, 1984). In the practical analog of this move, when you look outward, what you find are actions, the external products of any successful deployment of practical reasons. So the logic of practical reasons is to be read off the structure of actions in something like the way that, say, truth-functional logic is to be read off the truth-functional relations between propositions. This form of anti-psychologism preempts a worry one might have had about what was described at the outset as the organizing argument of the action-oriented approach. The proposal, recall, was to read the inference patterns off the structure of actions; if one were to construe this as reconstructing the process of practical reasoning from its product, how far would the reconstruction be likely to get? But Vogler’s anti-psychologism insists that questions about rationality are not questions about what *processes* of thought lead up to a decision.

For Vogler, intentional actions are picked out and segmented into their parts by applications of Anscombe’s ‘Why?’-questions. (‘Why are you chopping the nuts?’ ‘I’m making a salad.’) The internal structure of actions is consequently a series of steps towards a termination point (or ‘end’), a place where the action *stops*. When you make Deborah Madison’s persimmon and hazelnut salad, you first coarsely chop the nuts; then you thinly slice three Fuyu persimmons crosswise and put them in a bowl with the nuts; then you add three handfuls of trimmed watercress; then you toss with the dressing – and you’re done. A step can be shown to be rational by showing it to be a step on the way to the termination point of the action that you are performing. A step can be shown to be *ir*rational by showing that it’s not: for instance, if you’ve finished making the salad, but you obsessively keep chopping nuts. (Vogler allows that there may be “atomic actions”, actions that do not have further actions as their parts; perhaps blinking is an example. But just about any action we care about will be a complex action.)

Vogler’s ‘calculative view’ is accordingly that whenever (or perhaps, *almost* whenever) you have a reason to do something, you have a calculative reason, that is, a reason whose force is: this is a step toward the termination point of my action or a part of its larger whole. She does not insist that there are no other sorts of practical consideration. In fact, she provides what she takes to be an exhaustive list of the logical forms that the remaining considerations can have. (See Millgram 2006 for a brief overview.)[^millgram-2006] Vogler’s claim is that when you have a reason of one of these further sorts, you must *also* have a calculative reason.

This connection only runs one way. Since other sorts of practical considerations organize, modulate and generally control actions, they presuppose calculative reasons. Calculative structures (and the reasons they give you) do not presuppose these other sorts of consideration. Vogler regards theories in which actions must be informed by, (say,) a large-scale conception of the good as modeling practical rationality on a psychopathology akin to paranoia. This asymmetry is what Vogler takes to be the deep insight underlying instrumentalism: actions other than the atomic ones must have a means-end (or part-whole) structure. Consequently, calculative (or part-whole) reasons are fundamental; they are nonoptional, or binding.

This appeal to the structure of action as the foundation for practical rationality has dramatic consequences for substantive moral theory. Vogler casts her view as internalist in the sense that reasons for action do not have to be the same for everybody. Since reasons other than the calculative ones are optional for her, they vary from person to person as well. But if that is correct, then you can be, as the title of Vogler’s book has it, reasonably vicious; immorality of even the most extreme kind need not entail irrationality.

Like Vogler’s calculative view, Michael Thompson’s account of practical reasons is motivated by anti-psychologism. His account reverses the direction of argument we observed in Vogler. In the, what comes first is our grasp of inference (Thompson 2008, Part II)[^thompson-2008]. It is an account of action that is being read off the inference patterns (rather than the other way around), in something like the way Fregeans of this stripe take us to read the logical form of propositions off our command of truth-functional and quantificational inference. The objective of the argument is to exploit an account of action in order to establish a thesis about calculative practical reasons, namely, that those reasons are not psychological states such as desires or intentions.

The argumentative strategy is adapted from Sellars (1997, 37–41); it bears comparison to related arguments in Nagel (1970, 29–31, and Brandom, 2001)[^brandom-2001]. A core practice of reason-giving is identified, one that adduces only actions (and not psychological states); Thompson then exhibits the point of introducing apparently psychological locutions (such as “because I want to…”) into such a practice. The explanation is supposed to demonstrate that these locutions do not really name anything like desires or intentions but are rather a roundabout way of conveying information about the progress of an ongoing course of action. We will first describe the way Thompson sets up the core practice, and then briefly reconstruct his argument for non-psychologistic renderings of apparently psychologically oriented reason locutions.

Like Vogler, Thompson uses Anscombean ‘Why?’-questions to pick out intentional actions. He then draws our attention to the way grammatical aspect figures into their answers. Action descriptions can be either perfective (“I made the salad”) or progressive (“I’m making the salad,” “I was making the salad”). These grammatical forms are not merely ornamental; they carry different implications. From the perfective “I made a salad,” it follows that at some point there *was* a salad. From the progressive “I was making a salad,” it doesn’t follow: perhaps I was interrupted by a phone call. Thompson demarcates ‘naive rationalizations’ as those which deploy the progressive in placing one action as part of another. (“Why are you chopping the nuts?” “I’m making a salad.”) The contrast is with ‘sophisticated rationalizations’, which seem to invoke psychological states like desires (“I *want* to make a salad”).

We can imagine a society (call it the *action-theoretic state of nature*) getting by with only naive rationalization. But there is room for improvement in their practice of calculative reason-giving, and here’s a short way to see how. Agents restricted to naive rationalizations will say “I’m *B*ing because I’m *A*ing,” when *B*ing is an action that is a part of *A*ing, and they are *A*ing. But such parts of actions often come in sequence, one after another. So such an agent can, at a particular time, be executing a containing action, i.e., *A*ing, but not executing a subsidiary action, *B*ing. Now, if, at a particular time, an agent is not executing the subsidiary action (*B*ing), then when someone asks if he’s *B*ing, he’ll say he’s not. This puts agents restricted to naive rationalizations in the position of saying both that they’re *B*ing, and that they’re not *B*ing. (E.g., “I’m *B*ing because I’m *A*ing, but I’m not *B*ing.”) That’s potentially confusing.

We can suppose that such agents will adopt devices to obviate the confusion. For instance, they’ll learn to say (as we do), “I’m *B*ing tomorrow” “I’m going to *B*,” “I want to *A*,” Each such locution carries a different sort of additional information. For instance, “I’m going to *B*” positions one as being in the very preliminary run-up phases of an action; when one says, “I’m going to *B* because I’m *A*ing,” one is giving a naive rationalization, but one which registers that the *B*ing phase of *A*ing has not yet arrived, even though the reason for the former is that it is part of the latter. Likewise, “I intend to *A*” places one merely at the planning phase of *A*ing; “I want to *A*,” allows that one may be at a pre-planning phase. The function of these locutions is to preempt confusion by placing the actions that make up a naive rationalization in their temporal sequence. But once we see that that *is* their function, the temptation to construe them as invoking psychological states (intentions, desires and so on) is misguided. The belief-desire theorist of practical rationality is treating a linguistic device for sorting out the order of subsidiary actions (“…because I want to…”) as naming a motivating psychological state (a desire).

Both Vogler’s and Thompson’s views position themselves as descendants of Elizabeth Anscombe’s short classic monograph *Intention* (1985), so it will be helpful to contrast her model of action briefly with the later appropriations we have just surveyed. The centerpiece of that work is a famous description of a particular structure of nested actions: a man is moving his arm up and down; doing so is operating a pump; that in turn is conveying water up to a house; doing *that* is poisoning the occupants of the house; that, finally, is effecting what goes by the label ‘regime change’. A single core action, the ongoing movement of that arm, is repeatedly recharacterized, by invoking progressively broader contexts. Those in-order-to connections can be elicited from the agent through a series of the ‘Why?’-questions mentioned above: “Why are you pumping?” “To get the water up to the building.” “Why are you doing that?” “To kill every last one of them.” (In this work especially, Anscombe made a point of nonmoralistic illustrations.)

We can flag some of the ways in which her treatment and those of her successors diverge. Where Anscombe attended primarily to single core activities placed in broader contexts, Vogler focuses on sequences of distinct activities. Where Thompson’s actions are always composed of yet-smaller actions, Anscombe allows that below that core action of arm movement, the activity in question is, as she puts it, preintentional: normally, when someone pumping water flexes the muscles in his arm, it is not an additional nested intentional action. This fact is marked by his inability to say how he’s doing it, which is a denial of the central condition for an action’s being intentional, namely, that when you do it, you know what you’re doing.

Anscombe argues that an action as a whole, that is, under the outermost characterization in the matryoshka-doll structure, needs to have a *point* – to come with a ‘desirability characterization’ – if it is to be intelligible as an action at all. For instance, it might be for the sake of pleasure, or health, or, in the pumping illustration, regime change might be cast as matter of aspiring to a better future, where these are not understood as further layers of the onion-like construction (in that latter case, because the agent doesn’t have enough of a sense of *how* regime change is supposed to bring about that better future). But the investigation of desirability characterizations – and of whether there’s a difference between a *point* and a *reason* – is left incomplete. (Amusingly, she says at one point where the question might have gotten attention: “I say ‘Consider this’ really with a view to saying ‘let us not consider it here’” [sec. 13]).

## 2. The Authorship View of Action

A second approach takes the essential feature of action to be that it is *authored*; there is no such thing as an action without an owner. The two most developed positions of this kind are due to Christine M. Korsgaard and J. David Velleman.

On Korsgaard’s view, what gives an action an owner is that it is attributable to the person *as a whole.* Whole-person attributions require a *constitution*, a form of psychic organization and regulation of a person that is a smaller-scale analog of the political constitution of a state. A person’s constitutions contains the procedures by which actions are to be produced; one’s behaviors count as full-fledged actions, only when they are produced in that way (Korsgaard 2008, ch. 3, Korsgaard 2009)[^korsgaard-2008][^korsgaard-2009]. In contrast to the views we have so far surveyed, Korsgaard’s theory of practical rationality is psychologistic. The nature of action is the starting point of an argument used to determine the proper structure of an agent’s psychology: we ask what psychological structure an agent must have in place for him to be able to author actions. And that psychology in turn determines what the correct forms of practical inference must be.

The philosophical motivations of Korsgaard’s position are in part forensic: the connection between actions and agents must be such that we can hold agents responsible for what they do. More importantly, the account is meant to explain why your practical reasons are binding: what their ‘normative force’ is. Her idea is that, failing to meet the constitutive standards for actions produces a defective action; an action that an agent denies as her own would be defective. Now, we *have* to act, because anything we do will be an action. So, the question of why an item should conform to the the constitutive standards for its type ’answers itself’ (Korsgaard 2008, 61)[^korsgaard-2008]. If she can show that deploying reasons satisfying certain requirements is a constitutive standard for action – and the requirements she is gong to argue for are Kantian – that will amount to showing why you should act on reasons that satisfy those Kantian requirements.

We noted that action is properly authored by an agent only when it is produced by the agent as a whole. In order for the attribution of an action to a person to be meaningful, there must be a real distinction between *your* actions, on the one hand, and events that resemble actions but are produced by your psychic parts, on the other. We can make out such a distinction only if you identify with a principle of choice that determines the action, where that principle is universal in form. (We won’t reconstruct her arguments for that last claim here; Millgram 2011 gives a summary.)[^millgram-2011] Having a principle of choice contrasts mere *acts* (setting the table) with *actions* (setting the table to be ready before the guests arrive), where the principle binds the act to the end for which they are performed. The principles of practical rationality are the procedures in a satisfactory individual constitution legislating over principles of choice. Reasons for action must be universal in form, and Korsgaard argues that this entails the substantive correctness of Kantian moral theory, at any rate that part of it which imposes a universalizability requirement on practical reasons: very roughly, it must be possible for you and everyone else jointly to act on the basis of that universal-in-form principle, without assuring the frustration of the end it mentions. (For further description of Kant’s account, see the section on the universal law of nature formula in the entry on Kant’s moral philosophy.)

Let’s pause to mark some of the contrasts between two of the theories of practical inference we have on the table. First of all, Kantian maxims – the structured statements of intention on which the universalizability requirement is imposed – have means-end or calculative structure. So Vogler’s requirement that actions be calculatively well-formed, is compatible with Korsgaard’s view. But Korsgaard and Vogler differ over whether the universalizability of maxims is mandatory. Now because the universalizability test (the so-called CI-procedure) is applied to one’s maxims as they arise, and because they might be novel ones, it remains an open question what the moral theory generated by this theory of practical reasoning is. A handful of canonical dicta are usually thought of as constituting its core: not to lie; to help others; to develop one’s talents. So, secondly, differing conceptions of action give rise to starkly differing views of the moral demands of rationality; on Vogler’s view, the solely calculative conception of action allows for rational agents who lie, exploit others, and don’t bother to improve themselves. Korsgaard’s account is morally more demanding because it is more demanding about what it takes to be an action.

An important contrast between these views has to do with how the attributability of action is understood. Anscombean accounts construe that attribution of action as having to do with the special way in which you know what you’re doing. Since you just *know* what you’re doing, its being *your* action takes care of itself. In Korsgaard’s picture, however, action attribution is a task each agent has to manage by composing oneself to meet the exigencies of agency. If Kantian moral theory is to be nonoptional, the noncalculative Kantian reasons must be also. If the noncalculative reasons are the source of authorship, then that must also be demonstrably nonoptional. That necessity is practical. But if that is the right way to reconstruct Korsgaard’s position, there is a worry to register, one that we are not yet able to meet, but to which we will return briefly when the time comes to assess the state of play of the field we are surveying. (For some of the back and forth, see Enoch 2006, Ferrero 2009, Tubert 2010, Katsafanas 2013, ch. 2, Lavin 2017.)[^enoch-2006][^ferrero-2009][^katsafanas-2013][^lavin-2017][^tubert-2010]

We generally take arguments built around practical necessity to be responsible to and modulated by the urgency and force of the necessity. The point is easier to see when we consider collective agents, so let us take up Korsgaard’s political analogy once more. A state that aspires to having all activity within its jurisdiction be its own actions is totalitarian. Democratic states sometimes mobilize themselves in a way that approximates this extreme: usually, in times of all-out war. For instance, during WWII, the American government imposed rationing, managed a great deal of industrial production directly, and so on. But when there is no such need, liberals regard totalitarianism as an unreasonable and immoral mode of political organization, preferring a state in which most activity within is not attributable to it. Markets are an alternative form of coordination: market outcomes are not directly attributable to the state. In a market economy, competitors act independently and against each other: coordination does not consist in forestalling competition, but in creating a regulatory framework for it. If the analogy goes through, action in Korsgaard’s sense is inevitable only insofar as practical demands on agency require totalitarian rather than, say, market-like approaches to the organization of individual activity. The worry is that we do not yet understand the practical demands that *always* warrant forms of coordination that underwrite the full attributability of action.

Korsgaard’s response is an argument that you cannot choose to have a character with fault lines in it, one whose unity is ‘contingent and unstable’ (2009, 8.5.2). That argument is too opaque to permit an uncontroversial reconstruction; instead I will conclude the exposition of her position by describing the challenge the argument is meant to meet. On Korsgaard’s view, the practical necessity of reconstituting one’s agency from moment to moment must be a formal feature of any human personality. However, in our ordinary ways of thinking, practical necessities are substantive and contingent pressures. The formal feature of agency that replaces those substantive pressures is the commitment to sustain one’s agency in any circumstances whatsoever: ‘our principles, moral principles, are supposed to hold us together in any environment, any circumstance, come what may’ (Korsgaard 2009, sec. 5.5.3)[^korsgaard-2009]. That commitment is supposed to be what makes action, rather than a more loosely organized alternative to it, always our plight.

We turn now to the second of the positions that takes authorship to be the essential feature of action. Like Korsgaard, Velleman takes action, as contrasted with mere activity, to be owned (Velleman 2015, 101 and ch. 5, *passim*)[^velleman-2015]. Like Korsgaard, he takes ownership to require a definite structure in an agent, and again like her, he takes the forms of practical reasoning and substantive practical reasons to be determined by the agent’s structure. However, he disagrees with Korsgaard about what the structure of an agent is, what its practical reasons are, and incidentally, with Korsgaard’s insistence that being authored by the agent as a whole means not being authored by a proper part of the agent. Velleman’s position makes more sense bearing in mind that it has more than one agenda; accordingly we will juxtapose two complementary entry points, and subsequently suggest that what looks like a third and independent motivation for the view is reachable from those.

The sense of ownership at work in Velleman’s view is inherited from an older debate about freedom of the will and autonomy, and it has to do with whether the agent can honestly dissociate himself from his action or motivation (e.g., “It wasn’t really me speaking; it was just the alcohol”). One might think that this sort of dissociation could be overcome by a further endorsement (“It really *was* me speaking”), but a familiar difficulty is that many forms of endorsement merely raise the problem anew: ownership cannot be identified with an endorsement when the agent can dissociate himself from that endorsement as well (Velleman 2015, 295–296)[^velleman-2015]. There is an extensive literature on agency built up of rounds of this back-and-forth, and here is a very quick taste of it. Harry Frankfurt had suggested that a desire is full-fledgedly yours when you have a suitable second-order desire (a desire that the initial desire motivate you to act; Frankfurt 1971)[^frankfurt-1971]; Gary Watson pointed out that Frankfurt’s proposal merely pushed the question back to whether that second-order desire was full-fledgedly yours, and he proposed fit with your values as an alternative account (Watson 1975)[^watson-1975]. But the rejoinder was of course to ask what makes a set of values full-fledgedly your own (Benson 1987)[^benson-1987], and the problem seemed to take on the shape of a hard-to-halt regress. (For more on this debate, see Section 2.4 of the entry on free will.)

Velleman’s way of terminating the regress is to locate a psychological element from which an agent cannot dissociate himself (2015, chs. 5, 7). Because to act is to act for reasons, an agent cannot dissociate himself from a desire to act for reasons – not, that is, without ceasing to be an agent. (Treat the content of that desire as a temporary placeholder; we’ll return to it shortly. In addition, Watson 2004, esp. at 28, 166f, 168 n. 7, registers dissent: to terminate a regress, what’s needed is not a mental state you can’t disown.)[^watson-2004] When such a desire contributes in the canonical manner to producing an action (say, by weighing in on the side of other desires that it endorses, and so tipping the decision toward that action), we say that the agent produces the action. So, to recapitulate, actions are in the first place *owned.* An action is owned, in the relevant sense, when it cannot be disowned; the only anchor for an action that an agent cannot disown without ceasing to be an agent is (roughly) the desire to act for reasons. So what it takes to be an action is to be (appropriately) produced by such a desire.

An action is what is produced by the operation of this desire, and so this desire amounts to the constitutive aim of action (in something like the way, Velleman thinks, that truth is the constitutive aim of belief [2015, ch. 10]). It thus serves to determine what putative reasons for action *are* (good) practical reasons (in something like the way that truth determines what reasons for belief are good reasons). But then its content cannot quite be: to act for reasons. For that would amount to a viciously circular and vacuous specification of the constitutive aim of action. Velleman’s alternative specification of the content is (roughly again, because there is some room for variation) to know what one is doing when one acts, or to make sense to oneself when one acts (Velleman 1989)[^velleman-1989]. So what counts as a (good) practical reason is that which makes one’s actions intelligible to oneself. Velleman seems originally to have had something like the following schema in mind: desires motivate, and so explain actions; when you act on the basis of a desire, your action makes sense to you; when your desire to make sense to yourself, together with more occasion-specific desires, produces an action serving the occasion-specific desires, that is a full-fledged action on your own part, and not mere activity.

That default schema looks to be a version of the authorship view of action supporting an instrumentalist or means-end (rather than, as in Korsgaard’s development of it, a Kantian) account of practical reasons. However, as Velleman’s position has evolved, he has suggested further patterns. Often one emotion naturally gives rise to another, as when (in one familiar pop-psychology meme), denial of a loss is supposed to be followed by anger, which is in turn succeeded by bargaining, followed ultimately by acceptance. Such cascades of emotion are the spines of narratives, and an action is rendered narratively intelligible in following such a cascade (Velleman 2009, 191, 194–197)[^velleman-2009]. Thus, when supported by a desire to be intelligible to oneself, narratives give rise to actions. Instrumental reasons have now been supplemented by what Velleman construes as narrative reasons. In later work, Velleman has further proposed character for the explanatory role (2009, ch. 1): if you stay in character (in the way that actors on stage aim at), what you do will make sense to you, and being motivated by the desire to make sense to yourself, what you do will count as an action. (A bit of nuance: in his earlier work, Velleman took the relevant desire to be a substantive motivation, on a par with other desires in the cognitive mix, but he has since relaxed the characterization; we can perhaps more properly think of it as a drive, implemented as a structural feature of a cognitive system, rather than one of the items moved around inside that system. (Compare Velleman 2015, 169, 246.)[^velleman-2015]

Now, notice what one’s reasons for action will turn out to be, formally. Once again, because reasons for action are picked out as what would explain one’s actions, we cannot, on pain of circularity, gloss what it is to be a satisfactory explanation of an action as being a practical reason (cf. Velleman 2009, 40)[^velleman-2009]. So we will instead rely on an already available notion of explanation and, Velleman concludes, it must be *theoretical*, i.e., logically the same sort of explanation that would account for any matter of fact. Returning to the default schema for an illustration, desires are reasons because they (potentially) explain action, and they explain action in the first place because they *causally* explain it. Here is the a second and equally important motivation for the position.

It is typically common ground in this field that there are two deeply different but parallel modes of rationality: theoretical and practical. Philosophers tend to see inference about matters of fact as relatively unproblematic; to a first approximation, it is when you reliably derive truths from truths. Practical reasoning is seen as an analogous enterprise, one which is, however, much more opaque: in determining what to do, as opposed to what to believe, it is much less clear when it is being executed correctly. Velleman eliminates the commonly accepted distinction by reducing practical to theoretical rationality, reducing problems about the correctness of practical inference to problems in the philosophy of explanation. Facts explain facts; so the move requires construing decisions as factual conclusions. The live candidate is that they are predictions of one’s actions (Velleman 2015, ch. 3)[^velleman-2015]. They differ from other predictions in being self-fulfilling. (It’s noteworthy that Velleman has worked his way around to adopting and defending a view which Anscombe 1985, sec. 2, entertains and rejects.)[^anscombe-1985] Thus, an action proper will be such a prediction fulfilling itself; practical reasoning will be the explanation of future behavior that underwrites the self-fulfilling prediction of that behavior.

A third motivation, which is at first glance independent, is to account for a thesis that has received a great deal of attention recently; namely, that when you act, you know what you’re doing. When you do things in order to make sense to yourself, you end up by and large knowing what you’re doing. Thus, the pervasiveness of practical self-knowledge is explained by invoking a desire for it. The desire is claimed both to be constitutive of agency and announced to be empirically present in a normal human psychology. Now, a common response to Velleman’s insistence (2009, 136–138, 2015, 325) that people want to understand what they’re doing – and that this is part and parcel of their desire to understand the universe – is incredulity. Remember where those responses are coming from: academics teaching large introductory courses look out on seas of apathetic faces that don’t seem to care about understanding much at all. Those same academics are committed to research – which, as the saying has it, is what you’re doing when you don’t know what you’re doing.

Velleman adduces the cognitive dissonance and attribution literatures as further confirming evidence (Velleman 2009, 37f; Cooper 2007 is an overview of the former)[^cooper-2007][^velleman-2009], but his readers balk here also: the striking feature of cognitive dissonance reduction is that subjects are unaware that they exhibit it and are perturbed when it is pointed out to them. Velleman himself notices that what he ‘regard[s] as the height of rationality’ others ‘tend to regard... as irrational’ (2009, 92 n. 4). What Velleman takes as practical rationality in operation seems irrational to the agents themselves; it requires, precisely, not understanding what one is doing. However, the thesis that one desires practical self-knowledge was reached from the entry points already introduced, and if our reading of the pressures on the view is correct, the inalienable drive toward self-knowledge is best construed as a cognitive *requirement*, one derived from the need to anchor action attribution and the demand that practical rationality be reduced to theoretical rationality.

There might look to be a pair of related circularities hidden in Velleman’s account. The presumption that desires or more generally motivations explain action is plausibly a legacy of a theory of practical rationality on which desires are *reasons* for action – and therefore, it is concluded, their causes. (As Quinn 1994, 236–239, points out, if desires are thought of as brute causes, desire-driven behavior looks like Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder, rather than rational action.) Similarly, the idea that a given emotion naturally generates a particular successor emotion has often been taken to be the result of training in narrative forms, patterns that must be *already* culturally available. (For something like this latter view, see Nussbaum 1992, ch. 12; Velleman responds at 2009, 199 n. 15.)[^velleman-2009] So theories of practical or narrative rationality would come first, because they are presupposed by causal explanations appealing to desires or narratives (2009, 26f). Such theories would render the account viciously circular, and (the objection concludes) are off-limits to Velleman’s project.

Velleman has an answer waiting in the wings, however. He, like other theorists we have surveyed, has a finish line in moral theory, a position he calls ‘kinda Kantian’ (2009, 149): because our lives are pervaded by the motivation to understand ourselves, over time that motivation is likely to have deep effects. Specifically, we will simplify our typology of persons and gradually *come to be* agents for whom Kantian moral theory is a pretty decent fit, thereby making ourselves easier to understand. That treatment can serve as a model for understanding the psychological processes to which Velleman appeals, and which renders the circularities we were floating no longer vicious: perhaps we have gradually come to be agents of whom belief-desire psychology is true, and in whom one particular emotion naturally follows on another, because conforming to these simple-minded theories simplified our personal and social lives, making it easier for us to understand ourselves. In that case, the causal factors these views invoke are now elements of straightforwardly causal explanations, and it no longer matters how they got that way.

Recapping, on one authorship view, an action is a prediction being enacted in part on the basis of a designated motivation, and practical reasons are the explanatory support for such predictions. A second such view supports a theory on which only universalizable maxims count as practical reasons. (For a general criticism that both self-knowledge and agential unity are matters of degree, and that neither Velleman nor Korsgaard have the resources to construct the comparative notion we need, see Katsafanas 2013, chs. 3 and 4.)[^katsafanas-2013] We are also seeing that authorship views of action may have strikingly different philosophical motivations: in one case, to account for the bindingness or normativity of reasons; in the other case, to assimilate practical to theoretical reasoning, as well as a need to make room for a certain sort of first-person authority about one’s will.

We now turn to Paul Katsafanas’s positive view.

## 3. The Challenge-Seeking View of Action

Actions, Katsafanas holds, are generated by drives. (Katsafanas 2013, ch. 6; he attributes most of the elements of his view to Nietzsche, this among them, but we won’t be concerned with whether the historical claim is correct.)[^katsafanas-2013] So if there is a characteristic that actions must have insofar as they are generated by drives, then all actions have it. Drives are characterized by their aims and they generate objects, typically, objects of desire. For instance, the aim of the shopping drive is having lots of stuff. When an object is attained (when you bring home that gewgaw from the craft store), the desire for it evaporates, and you are satiated; but after a while, the drive generates a new object (a new trendy shirt.) That drives generate one object of desire after another can be characterized more deeply as directed toward encountering and overcoming resistance.

Now actions are the products of choice, and often enough result from deliberation; when you deliberate, you take the outcome of deliberation to settle what you are going to do. Even when deliberation is absent, choice involves assessment, and that determines you to act only if you approve the action; therefore, actions aspire to be *active*, meaning you approve of what you do. Further, if you understood what the motivational inputs to the deliberative runup to the action really were, you would continue to approve (Katsafanas 2013, ch. 5)[^katsafanas-2013]. That last demand covers all inputs, not just the official ones, as when you claim to be weighing the pros and cons of a request, but are also responsive to the attractiveness, social class, or gender of the one making it. Because actions are produced by drives, you can only be fully active if you approve of your drives’ role; that in turn requires approving of the drives’ primary structural feature, namely their ongoing production of challenging goals. It follows that whenever we do something, we have or aspire to have, if we are less than fully active, a further aim. Borrowing Nietzsche’s vocabulary, Katsafanas dubs it ‘will to power’: selecting and structuring our activities so they involve challenges we exert ourselves in meeting.

As in Velleman’s view, the upshot is that your various reasons are invariably accompanied by a further reason: choosing more rather than less challenging – but nonetheless attainable – objectives. Katsafanas takes this interest to answer the complaint he directed at authorship views of action. If you approve of an aim, you want to achieve it to the greatest extent possible; therefore, if you are active, you are committed to more rather than to less challenging activities. (However, as Anderson 2013, sec. 2.C points out: Schopenhauer did not take the fact that one is motivated by one’s drives to entail this interest or a positive assessment of either one’s striving or one’s actions. A life of endless unsatisfied striving is, he thought, a scene of nightmarish futility.)[^anderson-2013] Although this underwrites the contrast between having more and having less reason to do something, Katsafanas does not regard it as inducing commensurability of reasons across the board. He does take it to have consequences for substantive moral theory; for instance, because the struggle to overcome resistance involves suffering, that sort of suffering counts as a good thing.

## 4. The Practice View of Action

In an early, influential, and Wittgenstein-influenced paper, John Rawls introduced the notion of a *practice* as a generalization or extension of the notion of a game (Rawls 1955)[^rawls-1955]. The important feature of a practice for our purposes is that it introduces statuses which are internal to it; a practice introduces standards. It also thereby introduces reasons which are internal to it. Baseball involves statuses such as being a ’foul’ or a ’strike’; whatever you’re doing, it can’t be a (baseball) foul if you’re not playing baseball. Similarly, something counts as a ’home run’ only by virtue of the standards, given by the rules of baseball, to which running the bases has to live up. The reasons within a practice may be means-end or calculative, or they may be reasons of other kinds. (In squash, that the other player’s head is between your racquet and the ball is a reason to call a ‘let’, not because it is the best way to win; if you were to hit your opponent in the head, you would win the point. In squash, calculative reasons are modulated by gentlemanly reasons.)

Tamar Schapiro has extended Rawls’s treatment, developing it into a theory of action (Schapiro 2001; she attributes the view to Kant, but again we will not take up the historical question)[^schapiro-2001]. On her view, ‘actions’ are just moves in a completely generic practice; that is, ‘action’ is a status within the generic practice in something like the way that ‘move’ is a status within chess. Schapiro does not name the generic practice, but because it will be convenient to have a way of referring to it, let’s call it ‘Intendo’. Intendo is the game you are playing whenever you do anything at all; ‘agent’ is thus the generic role in the generic game (the analog of ‘player,’ in chess). Practices specify standards and reasons, and so ‘practical reasoning’ turns out to be a practice status as well. Intendo consequently determines what forms practical reasons can take, and so patterns of practical inference are read off of what turns out to be the theory of action.

Being an action is a status (the generic move) within Intendo; so there could be no practical reasons coming from outside the practice of Intendo. (Other, more local practices have to accommodate reasons that come from outside the practice; for instance, in chess, the object of the game is to win, but I may have personal reasons for not playing to win.) Therefore, if you can show that Intendo imposes some standard on its reasons (and to anticipate, that they have to be universalizable), then you will have shown that all reasons have to meet it. An important metaethical question about practical reasoning has to do with the modality (roughly, the force) of the family of operators that includes ‘may’, ‘must’, ‘should’, and so on, when the issue is what you have reason to do. (Call this the ‘modality of freedom’.) Schapiro’s approach provides a surprising answer to this question: freedom of the will ranges over the allowable moves in the game of Intendo.

In extending Rawls’s view, Schapiro has departed from it in some ways. Rawls thought that ‘relatively few actions of the moral life are defined by practices’ (op. cit., 32 n.), and worried about the conservatism implicit in taking ‘the social practices of [each person’s] society to provide the standard of justification for his actions’ (32). Rawls took it to be ‘essential to the notion of a practice that the rules are publicly known and understood as definitive’ (24). This cannot be true of Intendo; if reasons for action were entirely public and understood as definitive, there would not be a cottage industry of philosophers arguing about the forms of practical reasoning. And finally, the Wittgensteinian roots of Rawls’s treatment suggest that he would have been skeptical of a strategy that turned on finding the deepest common features of all practices. Given that a practice is an extension of the idea of a game, Wittgenstein famously pointed out that there is no nontrivial feature common to all games (see Section 3.4, on language games, in the entry on Ludwig Wittgenstein). However, to point out that Schapiro’s position differs from Rawls’s is of course not thereby to criticize it; rather, it is to warn against assuming too quickly that her position inherits all of its structural features.

Schapiro’s article eyes a finish line that is some version of Kantian morality, one which it is natural to see as a reformulation and smoothing out of the analogous stretch of Korsgaard’s view. (So this perhaps makes it an additional exception to the earlier observation that theorists in this subfield don’t yet engage one another.) Schapiro writes that ‘if it is right to think of universal laws on the model of practice rules, then the law of freedom can be thought of as an indeterminate practice rule, one which simply requires us to make every movement as if it were to count as a move in some possible global practice’ (108); this is a paraphrase of the Kantian demand that one act only on maxims of which one could will that they be universal laws. So, the patterns of practical reasoning are to be those acknowledged by a Kantian theory of practical rationality and its substantive moral consequences.

Schapiro’s position is motivated in the first place by the history of action theory. She identifies two older conceptions of action, a consequence-oriented view, on which actions are simply ways of producing effects in a given, natural world, and an expressivist view, on which actions function more or less as evaluative pronouncements. The practice view of action is meant to capture the truth in each of these, while avoiding their defects; it is, as it were, their Hegelian synthesis. But Schapiro is also motivated by an appreciation of the importance and value to us of a world enriched by practice statuses. It is not just that the predecessor theories are wrong about what an action is; it is that forgoing the textured world created by our practices would be a terrible mistake. Schapiro’s position is not *motivated* by anti-psychologism, yet it is nonetheless non-psychologistic. For instance, when the king is in check, then the chess player has a reason to move it out of check; you don’t look inside anyone’s head, as it were, to determine that, but rather, at the constitutive rules of chess.

As before, if the Kantian moral conclusions are to be binding, then not only must the practice conception entail the Kantian conclusions, but the practice conception of action must be nonoptional. With baseball, you can choose not to play, and you can choose to stop playing; what’s more, the rules themselves bring games to a close. But Intendo must be a game you cannot walk away from. Why can’t you simply start producing activities that are not practice-governed? We have already gestured at one answer: the importance of living in a practice-informed world. One further answer Schapiro might give is that you *can’t* do that – where the modality of the ‘can’t’ is the modality of freedom. (Since reasons are a practice status, questions having to do with practical reason do not arise for the alternative forms of activity.)

The question is especially pressing for Schapiro’s. Non-ideal theory is concerned with guidelines for action and moral thinking in a world that is not very moral at all. Schapiro 2006 takes up a traditional Kantian test case, that of the murderer at the door asking if his intended victim, to whom you have just served a cup of tea, is at home. Kantian moral theory prohibits lying, and the casuistical exercise is to provide an explanation, consistent with the moral theory, of the legitimacy of lying to him. Schapiro’s solution draws on the idea that moral reasons are statuses in the game of Intendo. If the murderer at the door is refusing to play the game, then your own moves cannot *be* responses to his moves – in this case, honesty meant as a contribution to the ‘co-legislation game’ (the game that properly played Intendo turns out to be) is simply not an option. And if proper honesty is not an available move, perhaps other moves are available to you.

The tension is this: on the one hand, you must act from the moral law because you cannot opt out of Intendo; that is why morality is binding. On the other hand, you may lie to the murderer at the door, because he *has* opted out of the game. Schapiro provides a label for what she intends as a way of splitting the difference: the murderer is *betraying* the generic practice without *opting out* of it. But the distinction marked by the label is as yet unexplained. The further development of the practice view will be shaped by the need to accommodate these apparently conflicting theoretical constraints.

## 5. Evaluation as Essential to Action

We will review two variants of the thesis that evaluation is an essential feature of action, or of a privileged kind of action, due respectively to Sarah Buss and Talbot Brewer.

Buss distinguishes between mere “movements of the human body” and full fledged intentional action by requiring, for the latter, that agents endorse their actions at the time they are initiated; the content of the endorsement must in one way or another come to allowing the agent’s reasons to be sufficient for performing the action (Buss 1999)[^buss-1999]. Anything short of that, she argues, amounts to adopting a spectatorial stance towards your own actions: if you identify a number of desires, notice that you could satisfy them by performing an action, but then don’t arrive at an evaluative belief with roughly the content that that’s good enough to go ahead, you will be doing no more than sitting back to see what happens – to see whether the desires actuate your body.

Buss then considers a Humean moral psychology on which reasons for action are drawn from two mutually exclusive classes of psychological states, desires and beliefs, where beliefs are understood to have no properly evaluative content (e.g., Smith, 1987)[^smith-1987]. Since such a moral psychology has no room for evaluative beliefs, and since intentional action requires one particular sort of evaluative belief, Humean moral psychology is incompatible with there being intentional actions. It follows that a theory of practical reasoning presupposing Humean moral psychology cannot allow for intentional action. Theories of practical reasoning that identify reasons for action with desires and beliefs so characterized are directed towards choosing actions, yet incompatible with actions being chosen. The conclusion we are invited to draw is that Humean theories of practical reasoning are incoherent. A dramatic way of putting it might be: if your will is to be free, instrumentalism must be false.

There is evident overlap between Buss’s view and Velleman’s: both pivot on the concern that one act for one’s (sufficient or good enough or best) reasons. (In Velleman, that concern appears as a desire, where in Buss, it appears as an evaluation.) But the concern is differently motivated: where Velleman is trying to halt a regress in the structural analysis of agency, Buss is trying to express the philosophical thought that action is active rather than passive. Once past the conclusion that a theory of practical rationality had better not presuppose a Humean theory of motivation, Buss is open-minded both about what one’s practical reasons might turn out to be like and about the consequences for moral theory. Worries about psychologism do not seem to figure into her view.

Turning now to Brewer, we encounter a shift of key. In the contrasts between full-fledged and lower-grade doings that we have reviewed so far, “action” was used to designate the first-class version, and often “activity” labeled its lesser relative. Now in Brewer’s terminology, *dialectical activity* is the privileged category, and actions make up the inferior contrasting class. Actions are merely structured to produce a “pre-envisioned state of affairs.” But dialectical activity is marked out by two features. First, coming to a better understanding of what the activity is counts as an essential aspect of the activity. And second, continually arriving at a deeper understanding of the good of the activity is an essential aspect of the activity (Brewer 2009, 55 and *passim*)[^brewer-2009]. For instance, one might well think that part of what it is to be in a healthy and satisfactory friendship is to be developing a progressively deeper sense of what this particular friendship is about, why and how it is rewarding, what challenges it poses, and how to navigate them. Moreover, friendship also involves developing an improving understanding of the demands, rewards, value, and significance of friendship generally. (The view is positioned as recovering insights from older ethics, with pride of place to Aristotle, but as before, we will not take up the historical question.)

The anchor for the view is not that of our previously surveyed positions, that to do is thereby to act. (And so Brewer’s view is perhaps a penumbral member of this family of positions.) Rather, firstly, mere actions typically will not make sense outside the context of a dialectical activity, and secondly, he advances but does not quite directly argue for the suggestion that dialectical activities make up everything that could count as intrinsically valuable for human beings. Anything that is worthwhile on its own – worthwhile enough to be taken seriously from conversation to philosophy – belongs in this category.

Because the proposal is likely to bring to mind specificationist claims about practical reasoning, some compare and contrast is in order. Specificationism is built around the observation that, frequently enough, one’s ends are not concrete or definite enough to launch questions about how to attain them; the content of one’s goals must be filled in and firmed up enough for that to be possible. Borrowing a low-key example from Bernard Williams (1981a, 104), if our objective is to be entertained this evening, we have to decide what it will be before we consider the steps to take. So, there must be a mode of practical thinking that does this job. (For an overview of the specificationist back and forth, see Millgram, 2008.)[^millgram-2008] But where the aim of specificationist deliberation is to arrive at a sufficiently concrete rendering of an objective, in dialectical activity, the clarification of the activity and its desirability is ongoing, not just a preliminary. You find out what the rewards of this friendship are by participating in it, and the longer you are someone’s friend, the more nuanced and deeper your appreciation of what it brings to your life becomes. Deciding on an evening’s entertainment doesn’t usually turn up as part of the fun; with friendship, however, it is grounds for complaint when one of the participants ceases paying attention to the emerging shape of the friendship. Here, deliberation is not simply means-end reasoning; although one is moved to one after another after another goal-directed action, those actions are themselves part and parcel of further comprehending just what the dialectical activity in which they arise *is*.

Adapting one of Brewer’s illustrations, two friends might take a motorcycle trip to Vegas; a few years later, we find them in the gardening aisle at Home Depot; a few years after that, doing something very different from either of those things. The relation between activities and actions as *other* views conceive them is that being friends produces an endless stream of subsidiary goals and actions. But because the participants’ conception of it continues to deepen and shift, the actions in that stream cannot be concretely characterized up front. Brewer’s distinction between high-grade and low-grade are interdependent. Dialectical activity normally produces merely end-directed action in an ongoing basis. The intelligibility normally depends on being embedded within a dialectical activity. (It makes sense that they’d be biking to Vegas, given how, at that stage of the friendship, they understand what their friendship is about.) The improving grasp of what one is doing that is central to the activity depends on feedback from the circumstances produced by the low-grade, end-directed actions; it was, perhaps, the way the Vegas trip didn’t work out that made them rethink what they were doing with each other, and start in on gardening.

On the views Brewer is positioning his own against, actions are motivated by desires, which are propositional attitudes: descriptions of states of affairs that might be realized in the future in light of which you adjust what you can and will so to make them true. (Brewer attempts to reappropriate the concept of desire for his own purposes, but I won’t spell that move out here.) However, what you are *moved* by in the course of a dialectical activity, (as opposed to ’motivated’ by) cannot be captured by a proposition. For a proposition has a definite, stable content. But what moves you in the course of dialectical activity is deepening and evolving; there is no point at which apprehending it is properly over. So no proposition could successfully demarcate what the activity is after and about.

Moreover, desires as standardly conceived treat the proposition as what is to be made true in the future. But the object of dialectical activity does not lie in the future: living your life is a dialectical activity, and you are *already* living; your life does not wait to be lived until it is over. If you are somebody’s friend, you are *already* participating in the friendship. If you are philosophizing in an appropriately frame of mind (and so, always striving better to understand what philosophy *is*), you are *already* philosophizing. So, although the many simpler subsidiary actions that are constantly being spun off by dialectical activities can often be thought of as controlled by this sort of propositional attitude, dialectical activity cannot itself be construed as desire-driven, in this standard sense of ‘desire’.

What is at stake in the disagreement over how to conceptualize these ongoing courses of action? In the example we’re using, the friends’ goals change over time; seen through the lens of rationalization-by-desires as standardly conceived, the segments of the friendship are collected into a developing trajectory retrospectively. The cost is not just that one misses seeing what unifies the activity while it is in progress; the standard view’s inability to see the activity as unified, despite the very different character of its segments, undercuts its *actual* unity. But then, one does not have *that* dialectical activity: a friendship, whose parties are jointly pressing towards a more truthful, workable, or otherwise better understanding of that friendship. Operating only on the basis of rationalization-by-desires condemns one to a life largely empty of the goods that really matter, such as friendships. Whether this is the cost of surrendering dialectical activities as Brewer describes them will depend on whether he is substantively characterizing those central human goods correctly; an opponent will presumably contest the view on those grounds.

Returning to the point of convergence with older specificationism, if the evolving conception of a dialectical activity and what makes it desirable is deliberative rather than arational, there must be reasons, and reasoning, that can modify not only one’s decisions about how to attain what one already straightforwardly wants but also one’s view of what one is after. In the jargon: over and above instrumental rationality, deliberation of ends must be possible. And that returns us to the question of the conception of reasons tied to this contrast between dialectical activity and mere actions. It shouldn’t be possible to simply spell out what practical reasoning correctly performed must amount to. Practical reasoning is itself a dialectical activity, so our grasp of what it is will develop alongside our grasp of the activity. Nonetheless, a number of upshots for practical rationality stand out in Brewer’s discussion.

First, against the often implicitly accepted model of practical reasoning as a matter of first surveying surrounding circumstances, assessing them, and then deciding what action to initiate, one largely attends to what one is already in the middle of doing, and clarifying how it has gone hitherto with an eye to a continuation. The model here is that of an author considering his half-written book, wondering how to complete his not-fully-formed train of thought – the one he is in the middle of expressing. Because understanding what one is doing does not come all at once but reveals itself as one carries out the action, there is typically no ability to survey and assess all the considerations relevant to a decision up front when anything significant is at stake, as presupposed by the competing model.

Second, deliberation amounts to asking, ‘*What* have I been doing?,’ and the characterizations driving ongoing decisions will be largely framed using thick ethical concepts. Rather than applying concepts like ‘good’ or ‘right’ to evaluatively neutral descriptions of one’s activity, they will deploy descriptions whose application depends on mobilizing one’s evaluative capacities. (Williams 1981b, and for background, see the entry on thick ethical concepts.)[^williams-1981b] Williams controversially claimed that thick ethical concepts could not be factored into strictly factual and strictly evaluative components (for an overview, see Millgram 1995)[^millgram-1995]. Brewer argues that the unity of dialectical activities over time supports Williams’s view on this point, and in addition, that our need to rely on thick ethical concepts undercuts the distinction we have used in framing this very survey, between theoretical and practical reasons – that is, respectively, reasons that figure into argument about how the facts stand, and those that figure into arguments for doing one thing or another.

Third, and finally for now, Brewer accounts for our qualms about delegating practical reasoning to others. If practical thought is, as Brewer puts it, ‘a seamless part of the activity’ of friendship, a conversation, philosophy, or just living one’s life, then handing over one’s deliberation and decisions to third parties is to forego the valuable activity itself.

Although both Buss and Brewer highlight the role of evaluation in action, for Buss the evaluation that matters is the settled output of one’s evaluating; for Brewer the evaluation that matters is the evaluat*ing*.

## 6. Prospects and Outstanding Issues

The turn to action theory is producing some of the most interesting recent work on practical reasoning. As we have seen, their shared methodology notwithstanding, these theorists disagree on just about everything else: their philosophical reasons for adopting the approach, the correct theory of practical reasoning, the form or forms of nonoptional practical inference, and the upshots for morality or ethics. However, as awareness of the shared agenda grows, we are starting to see positions that attempt to take on board elements from more than one of the theories currently in play and, in addition, take up the task of explaining why competitors are misguided.

We are also beginning to see pushback against the program as a whole. Bagnoli 2021 identifies assumptions – e.g., that actions are deliberate, purposive, and guided – that are a bad fit for improvisation, but which are taken for granted by most theorists (although some of the theorists we have canvassed gesture in this direction, for instance, Velleman and perhaps Katsafanas). Arpaly 2020 evinces disbelief that the supposed distinction between action and mere activity effects a division. The supposed markers of full-fledged action are matters of degree.

The primary source of internal disagreements in the subfield is a disagreement as to what counts as an *action*. On one view, intentional actions are characterized by their stepwise or nested internal structure; on another, by being authored or owned; on a third, by their place in a practice; on a fourth, by entailing an evaluation of one’s reasons. The norm still is to omit explanations of why competing conceptions of action are incorrect. In a famous essay that kicked off discussion of a different topic in practical reasoning, Robert Nozick observed that advocates of the alternative responses to Newcomb’s Problem were prepared to explain why they thought their own position correct, but not what was wrong with arguments for the other alternative; such a posture is unsatisfactory, and Nozick emphasized that in such debates ‘it will not do to rest content with one’s belief ... nor will it do to just repeat one of the arguments, loudly and slowly’ (Nozick 1997, 48)[^nozick-1997].

It follows that the most important item on the agenda of this research program is to produce arguments that will decide between the competing conceptions of action. One can’t hang one’s theory of practical reasoning, and one’s moral theory to boot, off an account of action that is treated as obviously false by the others in one’s discipline. We may conclude by observing that there are two modes of argument one must choose between. One might try to establish that, as a matter of metaphysical fact, actions *are* as this or that theory says; alternatively, one might argue that one *ought* to produce actions which are as the theory says; that is, the top-level argumentation might be either theoretical or practical.

The choice between the two modes of argument is tied to a further strategic choice. On the approach we are examining, theories of practical inference inherit their bindingness from the non-optionality of the associated conception of action. That entails that any such theory needs an answer to the question, ‘Given what is meant by “action”, why not produce activity of some *other* kind?’ To canvass the forms this question takes for the theories we have on the table: Why shouldn’t I look for noncalculative control structures, and manage my activity by using *them*? What’s wrong with answering the question, ‘Did you really *do* (i.e., author) that?’ with, ‘Well, no, it just kind of happened?’ What’s wrong with engaging in activities that compete with each other or frustrate themselves – behaving as though you were a market on the inside, rather than a totalitarian state? Why shouldn’t I opt out of the game of Intendo? Why bother with evaluating the reasons for my action? Why *not* detour around activities with the structure of friendship, philosophizing, and conversation? Notice that we should not assume without argument that one or another mode of action must be nonoptional; if the pressures toward one or another form of action amount to less than practical necessity, pluralism about action might be the appropriate conclusion (Millgram 2010)[^millgram-2010].

A theoretical answer would involve showing that there is nothing else that you *can* – metaphysically – do: that whatever you do (in the thinnest possible sense) is action (in the theory’s thicker and more substantive sense). A practical answer would involve showing that, given the alternatives, the suggestion that you produce actions (in the substantive sense of one’s favored theory) is, as they used to say, an offer you can’t refuse. The type of force or bindingness that one’s theory of practical reason will inherit from one’s theory of action will be determined by the choice between these two modes of argument.
