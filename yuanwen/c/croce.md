# Benedetto Croce
**First published Mon Nov 23, 2026**

*Bellamy, Richard, Kemp, Gary*

Benedetto Croce (1866–1952) was the leading Italian philosopher of the first half of the twentieth century. Best known among Anglo-American philosophers for his work on aesthetics and the philosophy of history, his views standardly get linked to those of R. G. Collingwood (see Wolheim 1968 on Collingwood 1938 and Walsh 1951 on Collingwood 1946)[^collingwood-1938][^walsh-1951]. However, Croce’s theories of art and history served a more general purpose than Collingwood’s (Croce 1946)[^croce-1946]. Not only did they form part of his Philosophy of Spirit, but also he considered this philosophy as playing a broader cultural and political role in providing a substitute for religion as a source of meaning and orientation in life, not least for his fellow Italians.

Croce’s Philosophy of Spirit sought to rework the Hegelian dialectic by conceiving the unfolding of human consciousness as resulting not from the clash of opposites but through the interaction of four ‘distinct’ modes of mental activity. He divided these modes into ‘theoretic’ and ‘practical’. The first he subdivided into the Aesthetic (Croce 1902)[^croce-1902], which involves ‘intuitions’ about ‘particular’ objects, and Logic (Croce 1909a)[^croce-1909a], which involves conceptual thinking about the relations between objects and universals. The second he subdivided into ‘economic’ action of a pragmatic character, and ethical or moral action (Croce 1909b)[^croce-1909b]. Croce considered each of these four domains as governed by a particular ‘pure’ concept: the Aesthetic by Beauty; Logic by Truth; economic action by the Useful and ethical action by the Good. He considered each mode as supporting the others. Thus, the faculty of intuition enables us to give a particular expression to the impressions to which we are subject. Only then can we use our logical powers to link these particulars to general concepts. These theoretical processes become the basis of our practical activity, whereby we can will or avoid certain instances of things related to various general concepts.

Croce considered this account of the relationship of theory to practice as avoiding both ‘sensationalism’ and ‘transcendentalism’. On the one hand, our experience of the world requires human intuition, that is not itself simply a product of empirical impulses, to be made sense of. On the other hand, that intuition and its logical elaboration, was not a priori and apart from experience but a theoretical reflection on the world that gave rise to action upon it. Meanwhile, he came to see this relationship between theory and practice as bringing together the real with the rational within an immanent process involving the development of human consciousness in history (Croce 1909a)[^croce-1909a]. Moreover, *contra* Hegel (Croce 1907)[^croce-1907], he considered the rationality of history not as the unfolding of some immanent ideal but as resulting from free human thought and action, whereby our present theoretical and practical activity contributed to posterity by developing the potential of the past (Croce 1917a, 1938)[^croce-1917a][^croce-1938].

From this perspective, Croce’s historicism can be regarded as a ‘theodicy’ in the broad sense of Michael Rosen (2022: 191)[^rosen-2022] – forming ‘part of a project of reconciliation’ to the human problems of death and suffering, and a response to the related search for meaning and moral orientation in our lives. Deeply influenced by the German idealist tradition from Kant to Hegel traced by Rosen, Croce followed these writers in seeking what Rosen (23, 145) calls ‘historical immortality’, in which posterity takes the place of heaven.

To provide such religious solace, Croce believed his philosophy needed to make sense of the political and cultural events and trends of the age, seeing them as historical manifestations of Spirit, understood as the progressive self-understanding of humanity and more particularly of the Italian people (Croce 1908a)[^croce-1908a]. As a result, Croce’s philosophy should be understood as responding to the intellectual and political challenges of his times. Certain historians of Italian political thought apart (e.g. Jacobitti 1981; Bellamy 1986, 2014: ch. 6; Galasso 2000)[^bellamy-1986][^bellamy-2014][^galasso-2000][^jacobitti-1981], Croce scholars have often rejected or ignored this broader context as irrelevant, considering his philosophy as a reflection on the human condition sub specie aeternitatis (e.g. Roberts 1987: 22; Sasso 1975)[^roberts-1987][^sasso-1975]. However, that is to adopt a paradoxically un-Crocean approach to his work.

By contrast, this entry divides the development of his philosophy into three phases that mirror his attempts to confront the successive challenges posed to it by the main contemporary political and cultural movements – notably Marxism, Fascism and what he called decadentism (Bobbio 1955, chs. 7 and 13; Bellamy 1986; 1991; 2014, ch. 6; Momigliano 1966, who notes 6 phases)[^bellamy-1986][^bobbio-1955][^momigliano-1966]. In the first phase, from 1890 to 1903, Croce was the *enfant terrible* of Italian philosophy, in revolt against Catholic theology and Platonism, on the one hand, and positivism and materialist versions of Marxism, on the other, and praising humanity’s creative powers. This phase culminated in the launch of his bi-monthly philosophical and literary journal *La Critica* and the publication of the *Aesthetic*. The second phase, from 1903 to 1923, involved the further elaboration of the Philosophy of Spirit, in the *Logic* (1909a), the *Philosophy of Practice* (1909b) and the *Theory and History of Historiography* (1917a*)*, in a historicist and idealist realist direction. This phase reflected a conservative political realism that reached its apogee in his ethical writings during and immediately after the First World War (collected in Croce 1922)[^croce-1922]. In this period, Croce came close to adopting a form of providentialism, whereby individuals should have faith that in performing the duties of their allotted station they were furthering the unfolding of Spirit in history (e.g. Croce 1922: 95–97)[^croce-1922]. The third phase, from 1924–52, coincided with his opposition to Fascism and his development of an avowedly liberal, ‘ethico-political’, view of history. In a partial return to the first phase, he now focused on the individual’s freedom to think and act as central to his absolute historicism, which he identified with a meta-political conception of liberalism. He related this view to his familial links to the Risorgimento liberal tradition which he considered himself to be continuing. His popular histories of the *Kingdom of Naples* (1925a), of *Italy* post-unification (1928a) and, above all, of *Europe in the Nineteenth Century* (1932), served to further illustrate how liberal Italy prior to the rise Fascism was the product of what he termed ‘the religion of liberty’.

## 1. Phase One: Towards a Radical Idealism

### 1.1 Early Life and Influences: Towards an Idealist Aesthetic

Born in 1866 in Pescasseroli to a family of wealthy landowners, Croce never had to pursue a professional career other than that of an independent scholar. His parents had familial ties to major figures in both the Bourbon regime of the Kingdom of Naples, with which they remained temperamentally aligned, and the pro-Savoyard opposition favouring a united Italian state. In 1883 he was buried in an earthquake in Casamicciola in which his parents and sister died, though his brother also survived. Croce reports having already lost his Catholic faith but confessed the event led to his being ‘racked by doubts concerning the purpose and meaning of life’ and to even contemplate suicide (1945a). Having suffered his own personal Lisbon Earthquake, he was unable to have faith in positivist, materialistic, sensationalistic or associationist views that treated the natural world as divinely and benevolently ordained. However, he came into contact with a quite different approach to meet his ‘urgent longing for a new and rational faith concerning life, its purposes and duties’ within the circle of scholars and politicians at the home of his uncle Silvio Spaventa in Rome, who took in both brothers (Croce 1945a: 11–16)[^croce-1945a].

Silvio was the younger brother of the prominent Hegelian philosopher Bertrando Spaventa. Often portrayed as the Italian Feuerbach, Bertrando Spaventa sought a ‘reform’ of the Hegelian dialectic that avoided what he regarded as its ‘mystical’ tendencies. Drawing on Vico, and linking Hegel’s *Phenomenology* to the *Philosophy of Right*, he transferred the attributes of the Hegelian *Geist* to the human species to see history as the progressive unfolding of human consciousness towards the attainment of moral autonomy in the ‘ethical state’ (Gallo 2024: 154- 160)[^gallo-2024]. Croce’s early collaborator Giovanni Gentile came to regard Bertrando Spaventa as inspiring his ‘actualist’ version of Hegelian idealism, with Bertrando’s related theories of ‘the circulation of Italian thought’ and the ‘ethical state’ feeding into his account of Fascism as the product of the Renaissance and Risorgimento (Gallo 2024: 160–61, 182)[^gallo-2024]. Though elements of both theories influenced Croce, he consistently distanced his own idealist philosophy from that of the elder Spaventa brother and stressed its continuity – so far as his practical philosophy was concerned – with that of Silvio Spaventa (Gallo 2024: 161, 181; Croce 1925c)[^croce-1925c][^gallo-2024]. Unlike Bertrando, Silvio did not follow an academic career (Gallo 2024: ch. 5)[^gallo-2024]. A leading protagonist of the Neapolitan uprisings of 1848, with the unification of Italy in 1860 he became a key figure in the moderate liberal Historical Right party gathered around Count Camillo Cavour and his successors. Encouraged by his brother, he conceived the new Italian state in neo-Hegelian terms as the context within which the individual came to will a rational form of freedom as embodied in the law, allowing the reconciliation of individual interests with the public interest. Like his brother, he saw the ethical state as a secular alternative to the religious state they associated with the Catholic Church, which they linked to the Bourbon monarchy and resistance to the liberal national project of the Risorgimento. In this way, the struggle against opponents of unification became one of civilization against barbarism, with crime and brigandage the product of the Catholic obscurantism and feudal oppression of the Bourbon regime.

Croce, who later edited collections of Silvio Spaventa’s political writings and parliamentary speeches (Croce 1898a, b)[^croce-1898a], came to view the Risorgimento and the subsequent liberal era in similar ethical terms. Initially, he saw the cultural programme of ‘making Italians’ as requiring the force and authority of the state – a view that led him initially to support Fascism as a source of law and order during the post-war period of industrial unrest. However, when in 1924 he broke with the fascist suppression of liberal democratic institutions and its open use of violence, he insisted that Silvio Spaventa had offered a ‘liberal’ conception of the state, committed to parliamentary government, party competition and the rule of law, and grounded in promoting the freedom of its individual members. Croce saw Silvio Spaventa’s view of the state as quite different to the abstract Hegelian account of Bertrando Spaventa that Gentile associated with the new fascist order. (Croce 1925c)[^croce-1925c].

On reaching 21 in 1886, Croce returned to Naples where henceforth he lived the life of a scholar. At first, he devoted himself to researching certain episodes and aspects of the city’s history – making lasting and pioneering contributions to the collection of Neapolitan folk songs and fables, the history of Neapolitan theatre from the Renaissance to the eighteenth century, the Spanish influence on Italian life during the Renaissance, and the study of the Neapolitan revolution of 1799, amongst other topics (Croce 1945a: 20–23)[^croce-1945a]. Many of these studies appeared in the journal *Napoli nobilissima* he established alongside other local historians and were only later collected in books related to their different themes (e.g. 1891; 1897; 1919a). Although scholarly accomplishments in their own right, these erudite writings also contained presentiments of elements of his later philosophical views, both in their method and their anti-positivist and anti-realist concern with the role of autonomous individual thought and action in the making of history and of poetry as a form of ‘pure’ aesthetic expression (Corsi 1974)[^corsi-1974]. These ideas came together in his first two philosophical essays on ‘History Subsumed under the General Concept of Art’ of 1893 and on ‘Literary Criticism: Theoretical Questions’ of 1894 (Croce 1945a: 24–25)[^croce-1945a]. These essays fed into his *Aesthetic* of 1902 and his writings on the unity of philosophy and history in the *Logic* and will be considered alongside these works.

### 1.2 Marxism: The Priority of ‘Economic’ Action

While staying with his uncle, Croce enrolled at Rome university to study jurisprudence. However, the main influence on him were the lectures on moral philosophy by Antonio Labriola, the star pupil of Bertrando Spaventa and a regular visitor to Silvio Spaventa’s household (Croce 1945a: 18–19)[^croce-1945a]. After leaving Rome, he remained in contact with Labriola, who had steadily moved from the conservative liberalism and neo-Hegelianism of the Spaventa brothers, by way of the neo-Kantian psychological doctrines of J. F. Herbart – whereby concepts were not timeless universal categories but timebound instruments for the interpretation of particular features of given historical conditions – towards a Marxist democratic socialism (Bellamy 1987 54–58; Gallo 2024: 220–231)[^bellamy-1987][^gallo-2024]. As Croce reports Labriola telling him (Croce 1906: 1121)[^croce-1906], he came to embrace socialism when he saw the Hegelian ‘ethical state’ as a utopia that could only be achieved once the harsh reality of the antagonistic interests of different classes had been overcome. He then sought to critique both positivist and especially Darwinian inspired accounts of historical materialism as involving a pre-determined view of social evolution grounded in progressive changes to the economic base arising from the inherent contradictions within pre-communist modes of production – a move approved by Friedrich Engels. Instead, in ways that drew on his earlier neo-Hegelianism, Labriola characterised Marxism as a ‘philosophy of praxis’ – a term later picked up by Antonio Gramsci in his *Prison Notebooks* (Bellamy 1987: 58–65)[^bellamy-1987].

Labriola’s writings of the period 1895–99 formed part of a key debate of the Second International on the nature of Marxism (Croce 1945a: 17–20; Gallo 2024: 220–30)[^croce-1945a][^gallo-2024]. Though Labriola initially considered Croce along with George Sorel as forming a triumvirate in his reform of Marxism – the one organising the Italian and the other the French publication of his essays – both differed from him in stressing the practical rather than strictly philosophical contribution of Marxism (Bellamy 1987: 65–71)[^bellamy-1987]. In a series of essays collected in Croce’s book (dedicated to the memory of Labriola) *Historical Materialism and Marxist Economics* (1900), Croce criticised both the Marxian account of the falling rate of profit and even Labriola’s revised account of historical materialism. Instead, he praised Marx as ‘the Machiavelli of the proletariat’ and considered him as having perceived the logical primacy of ‘economic’ action, aimed at the Useful, over ‘ethical’ action, aimed at the Good – a position approved by Vilfredo Pareto and that he later developed in his *Philosophy of the Practical*.

These debates brought Croce into contact with Giovanni Gentile. A student at the Scuola Normale in Pisa of another follower of Bertrando Spaventa – Donato Jaja, Gentile’s position was closer to Labriola’s. His book *The Philosophy of Marx* (1899 and fulsomely dedicated to Croce) combined two studies – one critical of historical materialism, the other appreciative of the ‘philosophy of praxis’, with the second containing the first Italian translation of Marx’s ‘Theses on Feuerbach’. Despite these differences, their debate over Marx gave rise to an almost daily correspondence and collaboration that lasted until 1924 and their split over Gentile’s adherence to Fascism. Yet the philosophical issue that was to divide them can be seen from the very start, with Gentile emphasising the subjective moment of reflexive consciousness as the foundation for all human experience, while Croce sought a more ‘realist’ idealism, whereby thought and action were separate moments in the rational understanding and enactment of the real within history. As Gentile noted in a second dedication to Croce of a book that ironically came to mark their philosophical rupture and foreshadow their political one – his *General Theory of Spirit as Pure Act* (1916) – theirs was an intellectual friendship based on a ‘concordia discors’.

### 1.3 The Aesthetic: As science of expression and general linguistic

The new century marked the start of Croce’s development of his own philosophical and cultural programme. Not only did he begin to develop his own philosophical system but also from 1903 he began editing the bi-monthly journal *La Critica*. Almost entirely written by Croce and Gentile, it’s regular features were Croce’s continuing of the *History of Italian Literature* (1870–71), of Francesco De Sanctis, another Neapolitan Hegelian, into the post-unification period and Gentile’s studies of Italian philosophy post 1850. Croce recalled how ‘in working on *La Critica* I felt … I’d found my role’. He described this work as ‘political in the widest sense: the work of both a scholar and citizen’, which he conceived as ‘a service that I paid most directly to Italian culture’ by championing the new idealist philosophy that he, like Gentile, saw as having shaped the unification of Italy (Croce 1945a: 32–33)[^croce-1945a]. Supported by Gentile, Croce sought both to criticise those contemporary intellectual currents, such as Catholicism and positivism, he considered not simply erroneous but also pernicious, and to proselytise his own views and those of writers he considered to share or inspire them, at least in part. As the opening editorial of *La Critica* put it, his mission was to exalt the creative power of the individual and as a consequence to critique ‘all attempts to put the world in short pants and persuade adults to return to childhood’ – attempts he associated with religious belief in a transcendent God and divinely ordained order, on the one hand, and all forms of historical determinism, on the other (Croce 1903a: 3). This aim also informed his first major philosophical work, the *Aesthetic* of 1902.

#### 1.3.1 Primacy of the Aesthetic

The *Aesthetic* was conceived as the first volume of the Philosophy of Spirit and is as much about the primacy of the aesthetic in our cognition of the world as Art *per se.* Philosophers since Kant distinguish intuitions or representations from concepts or universals. Croce both follows this tradition and departs from it radically. For an ‘intuition’ is a complete conscious manifestation just as it is, in advance of applying concepts (and all that is true a priori of them is that they have a particular character or individual physiognomy—they are not necessarily spatial or temporal, contra Kant). Croce supposes the modes of mental activity are arranged at different levels. The intellect presupposes the intuitive mode—namely, the aesthetic—but the intuitive mode does not presuppose the intellect. The intellect—issuing in particular judgements—is presupposed in turn by the practical. Consequently, all mental activity, which means the whole of reality, is founded on the aesthetic, which has no purpose, concepts or judgements of its own. Indeed, intuition precedes the distinction between reality and non-reality. It is a purely intellectual exercise that objectifies impressions that may stem from either a world that exists or an imagined world. Judgements of existence are ‘extraneous’ to the true nature of intuition and result from ‘perceptions’ of reality (Aes. 4–5).

History represents, by definition, the only all-encompassing account of reality. For our knowledge of the world is the history of our thought about the world. All this world is at the most basic level intuition or aesthetic experiences woven into a single fabric, a world-narrative: ‘historical intuition’, if it relates to the world as it exists, ‘imaginary intuition’ if presented as a possible or imaginable world (Aes. 30). However, though the subjective present is real and has duration; any attempt to determine its exact size will be arbitrary. Therefore, the only rigorous view is that the past is no less real than the present. For reasons explored below in the discussion of his *Logic* (Section 2 2.1), Croce regarded the natural sciences as impure, second-rate. Consider the concept of a space-time point (Aes. 4–5). It is not something anyone has ever met with in experience; it is an abstraction, postulated as a limit of certain operations for the convenience of a ‘theory’. As such, Croce deemed it a ‘pseudo-concept’ and did not regard the ‘empirical laws’ in which it figures as fit subjects for truth and knowledge. Its significance, like that of other pseudo-concepts, is pragmatic (Aes. 30–31).

By contrast, what Croce calls pure concepts are characterized by their possession of expressiveness, universality and concreteness, and they perform their office by a priori synthesis. Consequently, everything we perceive or imagine—every representation or intuition—possesses all three qualities: there is no possible experience that is not of something concrete, universal in the sense of being an instance of something absolutely general, and expressive, that is, admitting of verbal enunciation. Instead, empirical concepts, like heat, are concrete but not universal; mathematical concepts, like number, are universal but not concrete. Examples of pure concepts are rare, but those recognized by Croce are finality, quality and beauty and form the domain of Logic.

A critical difference between Croce’s ideas and those of Collingwood, emerges when we ask: what are the constituents of intuition? For Collingwood intuitions are built up out of sense-data. For Croce intuition is an organic whole. To analyse it into atoms is always a false abstraction: the intuition could never be re-built with such elements.

#### 1.3.2 Art and Aesthetics

One might think that Croce would follow Kant’s Transcendental Aesthetic and think of art as a narrow if profound region of experience. But Croce takes the opposite line: art is everywhere, and the difference between ordinary intuition and ‘works of art’ is only a quantitative difference (Aes. 13). The Aesthetic has ‘its real roots in human nature’ with art part of our ‘general spiritual life’ rather than ‘a sort of special function or aristocratic club’ (Aes. 14).

His point is not that every object is to some degree a work of art but rather that every *intuition* has to some degree the qualities of the intuition of a work of art. It’s just that the intuition of a work of art has them in much greater degree.

#### 1.3.3 Intuition and Expression

We now reach Croce’s notorious doctrine whereby ‘intuitive knowledge is expressive knowledge’ (Aes. 11).

##### 1.3.3.1 The Double Ideality of the Work of Art

As an idealist, Croce considered there is nothing besides the mind, so a work of art is a mental object along with everything else. But he still maintained the commonplace distinction between mental and physical things. And on this divide, the work of art was still a mental thing, making art doubly ideal (see PPH 227).

Croce regarded intuition as entailing expression. To actually intuit an aesthetic impression requires one to be able to express it. Leonardo Da Vinci’s intuition of the Last Supper required his expression of it in his painting. Croce denies that we could have some vague intuition of a painting such as Leonardo’s without actually giving expression to that vision. However, the ability to give expression to one’s intuitions, as Leonardo did, is not a mere matter of possessing the requisite technical skill. Expression like intuition is a mental activity. As Croce quotes Michelangelo as saying, "One paints, not with the hands, but with the brain." (Aes. 10). Though all humans possess the faculty of intuition and expression, we do so to a different extent. As was noted above, he saw this difference as quantitative rather than qualitative – my stick figures are by some magnitude inferior to Rembrandt’s portraits but are nonetheless artistic intuitive expressions of a kind (Aes. 11).

The mental quality of all art means spectators ‘of’ a work of art must recreate the work of art in their own minds. Given the perceived aesthetic qualities of anything vary with the states of the perceiver, in speaking of the former we are really speaking of the latter. However, that does not lead Croce to a relativistic view of art. Rather, as is explained in 1.3.6 below, judgement and criticism of a work of art require the spectator to share an echo of the intuition of the artist and be able to appreciate its expressive quality for themselves. (Aes. 120–21).

##### 1.3.3.2 The Role of Feeling

Croce dismissed the hedonistic notion of feeling with regard to art. However, he regarded feeling as necessarily part of any mental activity. On this account, we obtain a feeling of aesthetic satisfaction from a successful expression (Aes. 74–6). Yet, it is the coherence of expression rather than the feeling it arouses that is the measure of aesthetic value (Kemp 2003: 173–9)[^kemp-2003]. That applies also to the feelings a work of art might aim at arousing in its spectators. What is important aesthetically is the quality of the expression of the feeling rather than feeling *per se*.

As a result, Croce discounts attempts to distinguish the feeling aroused by the content of a work of art from its artistic form as confused (Aes. 80–81). The distinction only applies at a theoretical level, to a posited a priori synthesis (EA 39–40). At that level, the irruption of an intuition just is the emergence of a form. At the aesthetic level, there is no identification of content independently of the forms in which we meet it, and none of form independently of content. It makes no sense to speak of a work of art’s being good on form but poor on content, or vice versa.

##### 1.3.3.3 Feeling, Expression and the Commonplace

Croce’s identification of intuition and expression appears to erase the distinction between a person who looks at a bowl of fruit but cannot draw or paint it, and the person who does draw or paint it. Surely, the first intuits but cannot express it, while the second can do both? Croce addresses this objection from both sides. On one side, there is ‘the illusion or prejudice that we possess a more complete intuition of reality than we really do’ (Aes. 9). We have, most of the time, only fleeting, transitory intuitions amidst the bustle of our practical lives. ‘The world which as rule we intuit is a small thing … It consists of small expressions … it is a medley of light and colour’ (Aes. 9). On the other side, if our person is seriously focussed on the bowl of fruit, only a prejudice denies that she is thereby expressing herself— although Croce came to accept the ordinary direct perception of things, as in photography, generally lacks the ‘lyrical’ quality artists give to their works.

#### 1.3.4 Natural Expression, Beauty and Hedonic Theory

Most people regard wailing as a natural expression of pain or grief and consider expressive behaviour or gestures as being caused by the underlying emotion or feelings. But Croce joins a long line of aestheticians in attempting a sharp distinction between this phenomenon and expression in art. Whereas the latter is the subject of aesthetics, the former is a topic for the natural sciences – ‘for instance in Darwin’s enquiries into the expression of feeling in humans and in non-human animals’ (PPH 265; cf. Aes. 21, 94–7).

Speaking of such ‘psychophysical phenomena’, he writes:

> …such ‘expression’, albeit conscious, can rank as expression only by metaphorical license, when compared with the spiritual or aesthetic expression which alone expresses, that is to say gives to the feeling a theoretical form and converts into language, song, shape. It is in the difference between feeling as contemplated (poetry, in fact), and feeling as enacted or undergone, that lies the catharsis, the liberation from the affections, the calming property which has been attributed to art; and to this corresponds the aesthetic condemnation of works of art if, or in so far as, immediate feeling breaks into them or uses them as an outlet. (1929b in PPH 219).

Croce may be right to distinguish these things, but whether his official position—that expression is identical to intuition—allows him do so is another matter. He does not analyse the phenomena so as to deduce, with the help of his account of expression, the result. He simply asserts it.

Croce’s wish to divorce artistic expression from natural expression is partly driven by his horror at naturalistic theories of art. Likewise, his refusal to rank pleasure as an aim of art (Aes. 82–6). He does not deny that aesthetic pleasures (and pains) exist, but they are ‘the practical echo of aesthetic value and disvalue’ (Aes. 94). Strictly speaking, they are dealt with in the *Philosophy of the Practical*, which is orientated towards *usefulness*, and do not enter into the theory of art as defined by *beauty*. As he later noted, pleasure is much wider than the domain of art, so a definition of art as ‘what causes pleasure’ will not do (EA 11–13). Nevertheless, Croce does speak of the ‘truly aesthetic pleasure’ had in beholding the ‘aesthetic fact’ (Aes. 80). Yet, pragmatic pleasure had in beholding beauty is only contingently aroused, whereas aesthetic pleasure is always aroused by such beholding, because an intuition is an act of mind that brings the will into play.

#### 1.3.5 Externalization

Painting pictures, scraping a bow upon strings, reciting a poem are only contingently related to the work of art as an expressed intuition. Croce does not mean the painter could get by without paint; though it is not an impossibility in principle though it is factually. Rather, he means art is always driven by intuition, thereby making it possible for others to have the, or an, intuition. First, memory—albeit contingently—often requires physical work to sustain or develop the intuition. Second, physical work is necessary for the practical business of the communication of the intuition.

The process of painting is a closely interwoven operation of positive feedback between the intuitive faculty and the practical or technical capacity to manipulate the brush, mix paint and so on. Technique is ‘knowledge at the service of the practical activity directed to producing stimuli to aesthetic reproduction’ (Aes. 111). He concedes it may be:

> very difficult to perceive the frontier between expression and communication in actual fact, for the two processes usually alternate rapidly and are almost intermingled. But the distinction is ideally clear and must be strongly maintained… The technical does not enter into art, but pertains to the concept of communication. (PPH 227–8; cf. Aes. 50–1, 96–7, 103, 111–17; EA 41–7)

#### 1.3.6 Judgement, Criticism and Taste

The first task of any spectator or critic is to reproduce or realize the intuition which is the work of art. One may fail: ‘haste, vanity, want of reflexion, theoretic prejudices’ may bring it about that one finds beautiful what is not, or fail to find beautiful what is (Aes. 120). But given the distinction between practical technique and artistic activity proper, their task is identical to that of the artist:

> How could that which is produced by a given activity be judged by a different activity? The critic may be a small genius, the artist a great one … but the nature of both must remain the same. To judge Dante, we must raise ourselves to his level: … empirically we are not Dante, nor Dante we; but in that moment of contemplation and judgement, our spirit is one with that of the poet, and … we and he are one thing. (Aes. 121)

If by taste we mean the capacity to find beauty—and by genius we mean the capacity to produce beauty, then they are the same: the capacity to realize intuitions.

In Croce’s overall philosophy, the aesthetic stands alone: in having an intuition, one has succeeded entirely insofar as aesthetic value is concerned:

> …the criterion of taste is absolute, but absolute in a different way from that of the intellect, which expresses itself in ratiocination. The criterion of taste is absolute, with the intuitive absoluteness of the imagination. (Aes. 122)

Obviously, critical verdicts vary greatly. Croce believes this is largely due to variances in the ‘psychological conditions’ and the physical circumstance of spectators (Aes. 124), that can be offset by ‘historical interpretation’ (Aes. 126) The rest result from ‘haste, vanity, want of reflexion, theoretic prejudices’ (Aes. 120). Also, all that Sibley famously characterized as aesthetic concepts— not only gracefulness, delicacy and so on but also ugliness—are for Croce variations on the master-concept *beauty*.

#### 1.3.7 The Identity of Art and Language

Croce’s designation of the ‘Aesthetic’ as a ‘general linguistic’ involves several interconnected elements. Croce claims that drawing, sculpting, composing music are just as much ‘language’ as poetry, and *all* language is poetic; therefore ‘*Philosophy of language and philosophy of art are the same thing*’ (Aes. 142; author’s emphasis). That’s because language is expressive; ‘an emission of sounds which expresses nothing is not language’ (Aes. 143).

We can regard Croce as arguing: (1) Referential semantics—scarcely mentioned by Croce —necessarily involves parts of speech. (2) However:

> It is false to say that a verb or noun is expressed in definite words, truly distinguishable from others. Expression is an indivisible whole. Noun and verb do not exist in it, but are abstractions made by us, destroying the sole linguistic reality, which is the *sentence*. (Aes. 146)

If we take this quote as asserting the primacy of sentence meaning—glossing over the anti-abstraction remark, which is tantamount to a denial of syntactic compositionality—then together with (3) a denial of what in modern terms would be a distinction between semantic and expressive meaning, or perhaps in Fregean terms sense and tone, it is not obvious that the resulting picture of language would not apply equally to drawing. In that case, just as drawings cannot be translated, so linguistic translation is impossible (though for certain purposes we can translate ‘relatively’; Aes. 68).

Interestingly, Croce did *not* think of all signs as natural signs, as lightning is a sign of thunder. Rather, he thought ‘pictures, poetry and all works of art’ as conventional—as ‘*historically* *conditioned’* (Aes. 125; authors emphasis). Here, Croce was inspired by Giambattista Vico (1668–1744). Croce considered Vico the first to recognise the aesthetic as a self-sufficient and non-conceptual mode of knowledge, viewing all language as substantially poetry (Aes. 220–34). Vico’s only serious mistake was his belief in a historical period when all language was poetry; thereby confusing concrete history with ‘ideal history’ (Aes. 232).

## 2 Phase Two: Towards a Realist Idealism

### 2.1 From Liberal Italy to the Rise of Fascism

The *Aesthetic* was received enthusiastically by the young Turks of Italian philosophy gathered around the Florentine journal *Leonardo*. As Giuseppe Prezzolini (1903)[^prezzolini-1903] put it in an article entitled ‘L’uomo-Dio’ (‘The Man-God’):

> Art, from the narrow-minded, vulgar and almost primitive criterion of the exact reproduction of things, increasingly affirms itself as a personal vision; and the nascent conscience of the ideal transformation of these objects forms within the artist the ever stronger desire for unreal worlds and fantastic creations – whence the aesthetic, beyond an absolute relativism, comes to place the autonomy of the imagination as its foundation and to throw away as narrow habits and useless classifications the old rhetoric and styles. So says the recent philosophical study of B. Croce.

Initially, Croce encouraged such interpretations. Yet, he soon found himself caught between what he regarded as extreme forms of ‘transcendentalism’, on the one hand, and of ‘sensationalism’, on the other. Croce associated transcendentalism with any theory of knowledge involving rational a priori presuppositions of our experience and thereby lying outside the world as we conceive and act upon it. He identified this position with the traditional, Platonic, idea of the philosopher, as one who attains to the ideal forms of knowledge by withdrawing from the world. He considered such views as essentially theistic. However, he now found himself criticised by the pragmatist Papini (1905)[^papini-1905] for adopting just such a perspective himself in the first edition of his *Logic.* Yet, in responding to this critique, he also wanted to avoid what he saw as the parallel dangers of sensationalism, which he associated with forms of positivism or materialism that he regarded as involving a form of theodicy. He considered this deistic tendency as true not only of socialist and Marxist but also of nationalist theories of history.

While he respected the attempt of those transcendental and sensationalist theories that sought a substitute for the traditional religious basis for giving meaning and value to our lives, he regarded as nihilistic both those versions of sensationalism, such as the ‘decadent’ poetry of Gabrielle D’Annunzio, that seemed to revel in sensualism for its own sake, and of transcendentalism, such as the radical idealism of Gentile, that identified reality and morality with the individual’s ‘pure act of thought’, itself simply an expression of the will. To paraphrase Kant, the one was conceptually ‘blind’ and the other empirically ‘empty’, with their respective lack of grounding in reason or reality feeding the cultural malaise leading to Fascism (Croce 1928a: 246)[^croce-1928a].

By contrast, his Philosophy of Spirit aimed to bring the rational and the real together in a way that escaped these extremes while offering a humanist response to the need for a religion. Croce’s solution involved a conception of historicism that avoided the panlogism he considered marred Hegel’s similar attempt (Croce 1907)[^croce-1907]. Despite their differences, he acknowledged Gentile’s role in pushing him in this direction (Croce Logica: ‘Postilla’ Part 2, ch. 4, 210–211; 1945: 49). Croce had noted how a critical review of a book edited by Wilhelm Windelband that Gentile had submitted to *La Critica* (Gentile 1907)[^gentile-1907] implied a similar criticism of his views to Papini’s. As he wrote to Gentile, he shared Windelband’s position (Croce 1981, Letter 287, 14 December 1906)[^croce-1906][^croce-1981], whereby ‘[historical] reconstruction is only made possible by the [logical] precedence of a strictly theoretical-philosophical moment’ (Croce 1981, Letter 291, 18 December 1906)[^croce-1906][^croce-1981]. Responding, Gentile admonished Croce for considering that to write a history of philosophy involved one already having a concept of what counted as philosophy so that one could ‘discern, in the as yet indistinct mass of facts, the “philosophical” facts and write their “history”’(Gentile 1974, Letter 371, 29 December 1906)[^gentile-1974]. Gentile argued that risked committing the very error Croce (1907)[^croce-1907] had identified in Hegel: that of subordinating empirical history to a pre-conceived conceptual scheme related to a metaphysical ideal history. Gentile contended that if one understood philosophy and the history of philosophy to be one and the same ‘in the Hegelian sense that ideal and real, idea and history, are the same’, as they both did, then history cannot but be the history of thought. As a result, the philosopher’s conception of reality was the product of his or her thought, which was in its turn a product of its engagement with past thought. Yet, as he had noted in his review, that did not mean that there were permanent problems or conceptions of philosophy – rather philosophy was continually in a process of being rethought so that ‘the eternal is in time; indeed, it is of time’ (Gentile 1907)[^gentile-1907]. By contrast, Croce insisted that ideal and real, though related, must be kept distinct (Croce 1981: Letters 294 31 December 1906 and 295 5 January 1907)[^croce-1906][^croce-1907][^croce-1981].

Caught between the pragmatist realism of Papini and the subjectivist idealism of Gentile, Croce sought to develop an idealist realism of a historicist character in a revised edition of the *Logic as the Science of the Pure Concept* that also informed both his *Philosophy of Practice: Economic and Ethical* and his *Theory and History of Historiography*. Summarising his position, he observed how he had come to regard philosophy as arising within and contributing to the historical process. However, it did so not in a pure and abstract ideal form but through its continuous and reflective engagement with the real world of facts. Indeed, ‘the perfection of a philosophy’, he now claimed, resides … in its having overcome the provisional form of an abstract “theory”, and in thinking the philosophy of particular facts, narrating history, history that is thought’. (Croce 1945a: 51)[^croce-1945a]

Croce regarded this identification of philosophy with history as ‘setting me free from scepticism toward myself and toward man’s power of reaching the truth’ (Croce 1945a: 49)[^croce-1945a]. Instead of the philosopher seeking to grasp an eternal, universal and transcendent truth, philosophising became part of the unfolding of truth in history. As a result, the historicist approach ‘substitutes for the despairing sense of struggling in vain to remove the veil from an object that always flies and hides, the consciousness of always possessing a wealth that always increases and … the heroic image of a humanity that ascends *a claritate in claritatem*’ (Croce 1945a: 49–50)[^croce-1945a]. Consequently, his own philosophy had not ended with the completion of his ‘system’ but continued to develop via a series of on-going ‘systemisations’.

Croce saw his philosophy as answering not only his own spiritual yearnings but also those of ‘all the contemporary world’(1908b:38) who were likewise searching for a religion, capable of fulfilling ‘the need for orientation within reality and life’ (1908b:37). To meet that need, a philosophy had to present new ideas, be ‘systematic’, and ‘up to date’, offering an understanding of the present and a path to the future by having ‘the capacity to dominate and resolve all the problems that the human spirit has put to itself up until now; that is to dominate and resolve them better than any system of the past’ (1908a: 9–10). He considered his historicism as meeting these criteria by providing the individual with an understanding of how their present thought and action connected past and future. Philosophy and history linked theory and practice, the ideal and the real, through individuals addressing the concrete problems of the present and considering their actions as part of an ongoing historical process.

Appointed a life Senator in 1910, he now emphasised this broader social and political role of his philosophy and called on his fellow Italians to adopt this historicist faith to guide their role as citizens. Yet, Croce interpreted this faith in a conservative manner, as attuning oneself to existing traditional patterns of life, whereby individuals felt ‘part of a great whole, obedient to and cooperating within it, and deriving their worth through the work they perform within it’ (Croce 1911a: 184)[^croce-1911a]. He bemoaned the fact that such ‘good individualism’, which affirmed itself through such social and historical bonds, had given way to a ‘bad individualism’, that affirmed itself through breaking such bonds. As a result, there had been a collapse of a sense of social unity among citizens, so that ‘the great words that express this unity: King, Country, City, Nation, Church, Humanity have become cold and rhetorical’ (Croce 1911a: 184)[^croce-1911a]. He admonished his contemporaries for seeking ‘to create a new world’ with utopian programmes or ideals, rather than continuing ‘to work upon the old, which continually renews itself’ (Croce 1911a: 184)[^croce-1911a] Moreover, such a historicist faith provided not just a social philosophy but also offered individuals the spiritual solace basic to all religious feeling of ‘an insurance against the losses of this life, taken on the capital of another’(1911a:188–89).

Croce had opposed Italy entering the First World War, later describing it as ‘a kind of war of “historical materialism” and “philosophical irrationalism”’ (Croce 1928a: 283)[^croce-1928a]. However, once Italy had entered the war, he accepted this decision as reflecting the inner rationality of history to which Italians must adapt. He now portrayed the war as ‘a fight to which the German people, well aware that life is the right and duty of the strongest and most capable, have invited the other peoples, because the trial of the facts reveals who is best prepared or best disposed to give the imprint to the new historical epoch’ (Croce 1928b [1915] 69)[^croce-1928b]. Meanwhile, he sought to reconcile those called on to fight to their fate by regarding themselves as having been asked:

> To participate in the mystical passion of the making of Reality, and therefore in the perpetual struggle, which from the daily conflict extends to the armed struggle or war … [the individual] cannot arrogate to himself to change the divine laws of the world but must only defend the cause of the people of which he forms a part, and maintain to the utmost the post which his particular conditions have assigned him, with faith that from his work, loyally and rigorously fulfilled, will come the greatest good. (Croce 1928b [1916] 134)[^croce-1928b]

As we shall see, this argument reflects that of his *Philosophy of Practice*, in which he maintained that individuals are bound to serve the Useful by tackling those problems that arise in daily life in the most effective way they can, leaving the morality or Goodness of their actions to the judgement of history. Yet, given Croce considered history as inherently rational and moral, the possibility of a conflict between the Useful and the Good seemed precluded. As he remarked, ‘for the state one can sacrifice … even the health of one’s soul, but never morality, because of the contradiction involved which prevents it’ – that contradiction stemming from the rationality of the real (Croce 1912a)[^croce-1912a].

Though Gentile, who very publicly joined the Fascist Party on 31 May 1923, was to describe Croce as ‘a loyal fascist without the black shirt’ (Gentile 1925a)[^gentile-1925a], Croce never believed in fascism. Indeed, he regarded it as exhibiting aspects of his two bugbears: a ‘decadent irrationalism’ in the case of its futurist supporters, Gentile’s actualism and the glorification of violence; and positivist utopianism (the ‘Masonic mentality’) in the form of a totalitarian ‘programme’ that offered itself as the end of history in realising the neo-Hegelian ‘ethical state’ as described by Bertrando Spaventa and other Risorgimento activists (1928a: 266–67). Nevertheless, like many conservative liberals, he tolerated Fascist violence as a means, so he believed, to avoid the ‘anarchy’ of the post-war period and restore ‘a more severe liberal regime, in the context of a stronger state’ (Croce 1923)[^croce-1923] – and continued to do so even after the assassination by Fascist thugs of the socialist deputy Giacomo Matteotti in June 1924, when he supported the vote of confidence in Benito Mussolini in the Senate.

Both during the First World War and the rise of Fascism, Croce came close to identifying morality with *realpolitik* (Croce 1916a)[^croce-1916a]. In this endeavour, he followed Hegel in rejecting what he saw as the Kantian project of deriving moral duties from a set of ‘abstract’ universal principles (“Moralität’) and locating them instead in the ethical order (“Sittlichkeit”) characteristic of the ‘Volkgeist’ of particular peoples, in which conflict and its overcoming prove necessary for the unfolding of ‘Spirit’(Rosen 2022: 178–79, Croce 1916b)[^croce-1916b][^rosen-2022]. On this Hegelian account, the ‘power’ (“Gewalt”) of the state reflects that ‘of reason actualising itself as will’ (Hegel quoted in Rosen 2022: 182 – compare Croce 1916b)[^croce-1916b][^rosen-2022]. Though this process operates through individuals, they must be seen simply as moments of the realisation of *Geist* and have faith in its providential operation. The soldier thereby finds historical immortality through his sacrifice for his country (Croce 1916c)[^croce-1916c]. Yet, Croce disputed Gentile’s identification of Fascist corporatism with the Hegelian ethical state. For this same conceptual apparatus is also compatible, as in Herder, with a defence of diversity and individuality – whereby Spirit develops dialectically through the presence of difference and disagreements. It was this latter version that opposition to Fascism led him to develop.

By 1925, it had become clear even to Croce that Mussolini’s Fascist regime was built on coercion and violence and the imposition of a totalitarian ideology of an anti-pluralist and anti-liberal character. He had already split with Gentile over his politics in his last ever letter to him of 24 October 2024, and soon afterwards went openly into opposition to Fascism as the drafter of The Manifesto of Antifascist Intellectuals – published on 1 May 1925 as a ‘protest against’ Gentile’s Manifesto of Fascist Intellectuals of 29 March of that year (Croce 1925b)[^croce-1925b]. Yet, as we shall see, this shift from passive supporter to active opponent of Fascism required significant changes to his philosophical position.

### 2.2 The Logic

Croce’s *Logic* had three main aims (Logica: 389–90). The first was to distinguish pseudo- from pure concepts. He considered both formal logic, including mathematics, and scientific reasoning to involve pseudo-concepts. The second and third involved developing what he considered the key arguments of Kant’s *Critique of Pure Reason* and Hegel’s *Science of Logic* within his own account of the unfolding of Spirit (or human consciousness). He considered that this involved not only a dialectical unity of opposites, as in Hegel, but also the retention of the distinct moments of its theoretical and practical aspects and the associated ‘pure concepts’ of the Beautiful, True, Useful and the Good. He regarded this unity of distinctions as reflecting the different levels of our consciousness of reality. This view also involved the identification of philosophy with history, which he regarded as overcoming certain tensions in Kant’s metaphysics. The unity of philosophy and history, of the ideal and the real, made possible and manifested itself in the synthetic *a priori* character of ‘individual judgements’, which he regarded as central to all judgements of fact or value. For it is only through reflection on the history of thought itself that we can clarify the pure concepts that make our experience of, and reasoning about, the world possible. Each of these claims is explored below.

#### 2.2.1 Pseudo- and Pure Concepts

Croce considered pseudo-concepts to be forms of ‘practical’ rather than ‘theoretical’ knowledge. He identified two main types: ‘empirical’ and ‘abstract’ (Logica: 29–30). The first, typical of the natural sciences, involved what he called judgements of ‘classification’; the second, typical of mathematics, involved judgements of ‘enumeration’ (Logica: 114–125, 159). Neither natural science nor mathematics are truly logical in character because neither genuinely represents the process of the real or of thought. The formation of pseudo concepts may have theoretical elements but they belong to the practical rather than cognitive realm proper. They provide ‘rules of thumb’ that aid certain practical endeavours by either classifying various empirical phenomenon, in the manner of the natural sciences, or providing certain formal connections between abstract entities, as in mathematics.

Croce denied either of these categories of pseudo concept were truly logical in providing an understanding of the nature of reasoning itself. Empirical classifications are useful ways of organising various factual entities but they do not illuminate their nature or value. Meanwhile, abstract enumerative formulations are empty of empirical representative content and so they too fail as a means for grasping reality. That quality belongs to the realm of the two authentically theoretical forms of thought: that based on intuition (aesthetics) and that based on the pure concepts of the distinct modes of human cognition (logic truly considered). The latter was divided in its turn into ‘judgements of definition’ and ‘individual judgment’ (Logica: 127–39).

#### 2.2.2 Judgements of Definition and Individual Judgement

Croce’s distinction between ‘judgements of definition’ and ‘individual judgment’ was intended as a challenge to the Kantian distinctions between analytical and synthetic, and a priori and a posteriori propositions. Pseudo concepts appear to preserve the Kantian distinctions, but Croce regarded that as a mistake. Both empirical and abstract pseudo concepts offer definitions of a synthetic *a posteriori* and analytical *a priori* character respectively, but in Croce’s view these definitions are arbitrary and, as a result, so are the individual judgements regarding particular facts that they may offer. However, Croce considered that in the case of pure concepts all ‘judgements of definition’, including those propositions Kant regarded as analytical *a priori* as in ‘all bachelors are unmarried’, were at the same time synthetic *a posteriori* ‘individual judgments’ that ‘represent’ experience (Logica: 89).

On Croce’s account ‘individual judgements’ necessarily presupposed ‘judgements of definition’ and vice versa. Croce denied that there could be truths of form or of reason that were not also truths of fact. He contended that true definitions arose in concrete circumstances as responses to a question posed by a given problem to a particular person. As a result, all definitions carry within them a representative element stemming from an individual judgement about a given situation or issue, which they in turn inform. The pure concept may be universal but gets defined in multifarious ways. As he put it:

> It is sometimes said by way of simplification, that the same question has been put by very many men, in various countries and at various times. But in so saying one makes precisely a simplification, or better an abstraction. In reality, every question differs from every other question, and every definition, though it may sound the same and be bounded by certain definite words, which seem to remain unchanged and constant, differs in reality from every other, because the words, even when they seem to be materially the same, are in effect different, according to the spiritual differences of those who pronounce them. (Logica: 134)

Indeed, he remarked ‘the question formulated by an individual in a given moment, is not that formulated by the same individual a moment after’ (Logica: 134).

Croce contended that the nature of human consciousness was necessarily a unity of both ‘judgements of definition’ and ‘individual judgement’ which characterised the unfolding of the dialectic of the distinct ‘pure’ concepts of Spirit through the thought and actions of millions of individuals in history. He considered this process to have provided the metaphysical worldview required for synthetic *a priori* propositions to be possible, whereby in Hegel’s phrase ‘the real is rational and the rational is real’ (Logica: 143–48).

#### 2.2.3 The Unity of Philosophy and History

As Croce observed in a long note added to the revised second edition of the *Logica* of 1909 (Logica: 210–211), the arguments relating to the unity of philosophy and history marked a departure from his earlier writing on the topic – and particularly the essay on ‘History Subsumed under the General Concept of Art’ of 1893. In that early essay, he had drawn on the contemporary German debate between Windelband and Wilhelm Dilthey on the nature of the human sciences to counter an article entitled “Is History a Science?’ (1801) by the Italian positivist historian Pasquali Villari. Croce agreed with Windelband that history was ‘idiographic’ or ‘picture-making’, rather than ‘nomothetic’ or ‘law-making’. It ‘represented’ past events in their individuality rather than elaborating general laws of human behaviour. However, he also agreed with Dilthey that history involved human ‘life expressions’ that required an empathetic reconstruction on the part of the historian. He considered both the representative and the empathetic nature of history as involving the intuitive quality he associated with the Aesthetic. In the *Logica*, he still considered intuition to be the historian’s way into making sense of the past and the means whereby she could go beyond the mere collection and classification of facts, which he considered a mere preliminary to history proper. Yet, he had still to fully historicise his conception of philosophy. Prompted by Gentile, the *Logic* attempted to make this move.

Croce now contended that in constructing a historical narrative, the historian moved from intuition to conceptual or logical thinking and hence to philosophy. Both the past acts of human agents in history and the writing of history in the present involved the unity of ‘judgements of definition’ and ‘individual judgment’ described in the last section. Indeed, that unity followed from the unity of philosophy and history in his account of synthetic a priori propositions as by their nature historical. On his account, there was no difference between ‘judgements of fact’, such as ‘Peter is a man’ and ‘judgements of value’ such as ‘Peter is good’ (Logica: 89, 100). Both the concepts of maleness and goodness were historical products of past meetings with various Peters and Pauls and modifiable by future encounters. However, if what we mean by a ‘man’ or as ‘good’ is in part constituted by Peter’s existence, Peter’s conception of himself as a man or as good was equally given by a historically conditioned notion of man and good. A unity exists between the language and concepts we use and the practices we engage in, which develop and change through the course of history. Philosophy became in this way a matter of translating the concepts of the past into those of the present (Logica: 199, 202).

As a result, thinking and acting in the present necessarily entailed writing the history of the present. As Gentile had remarked, all history is ‘the history of thought’ because all history deals with the actions of human agents which involve their thought processes. Consequently, all history entailed philosophy – that is conceptual thinking by its creators. By the same token, Gentile and Croce also considered all philosophy as history, because how we thought now was the product of past thinking. As the histories of aesthetics, logic, the practical and historiography that Croce appended to each volume of his Philosophy of Spirit were meant to show, we only made progress in our thinking on a topic to the extent that we sought to fill the lacunas and unravel the contradictions of the theories that had preceded it. For the past posed the problems and questions that we confronted in the present, with our answers being the material of the future (Logica: 204–5).

Nevertheless, Croce still considered it important to unite the rational ideal with the empirical real. How else could a historical narrative be distinguished from a historical novel or painting? The latter could be largely fictional; the former was grounded in facts – albeit facts as perceived by individual agents at the time. He feared Gentile could not distinguish the two.

### 2.3 The Philosophy of Practice

Croce’s political stance during this period tended to prioritise realism over pure idealism – which he thought risked becoming solipsistic. This conviction also informed his *Philosophy of Practice*, which sought to offer a philosophical basis for his political and moral views. At its heart, was the distinction between economic and ethical willing, the one centred on the Useful and the other on the Good. Croce first made this distinction in his writings on Marxism (Croce 1900)[^croce-1900], which he considered as grounded in the economic and came to identify with a form of political realism in which utilitarian ends justify unscrupulous means to achieve them.

Croce regarded political moralism as misplaced. In part, that reflected his belief that conceptions of the Good, as with other philosophical concepts, vary over time. It makes no sense to criticise the Spanish Inquisition for intolerance, say: they simply possessed a different mind-set to ours that justified their actions at the time. That we would condemn such views now is different to treating them as wrong then. In this respect, moral judgements are historical judgements. By the same token, it is similarly mistaken to reify a given ethical conception of the Good society – as he believed Gentile had done in defending the Fascist corporate state – and impose that view as if it was universally true for all people now and in the future. All we can do as individuals is to pursue what seems rational for us to do from the perspective of the Useful and consider that it will prove moral in the fulness of time. Though all individuals may collaborate in ‘the plot of history’, it ‘is not the work, nor can be in the intentions, of any one of them in particular’. No one can judge history ‘practically, because it always transcends particular points of view, which alone make possible the application of practical judgement.’ For Croce, the ‘judgement of history’ can only be given retrospectively as ‘’the very fact of its existence’, so that ‘its rationality is its reality’. This ‘rationality’ suggests that ‘a Providence’ governs history, but it is not ‘the work of a transcendent intelligence’. Rather, it operates and ‘becomes actual within individuals and works not above or outside of them, but with them.’ Indeed, without an ‘intimate persuasion’ of the rationality of history ‘who would find within themselves the strength to live’? (Croce 1909b: 174–75)[^croce-1909b].

Clearly an implied teleology undergirds Croce’s historicism, though one that can never be pronounced on as leading to some defined end. As with his description of the First World War as a ‘life struggle, for the survival and prosperity of the best type [of social group]’(Croce 1916c 158–59)[^croce-1916c], he came close to describing the historical process in almost social Darwinian terms as a process of ‘continual struggles’ that had ‘effected the passage from animal life to human life … and creates ever higher ways of life’ (Croce 1909b: 175)[^croce-1909b]. Yet, such arguments could seem to be closer to naturalism than idealism and possibly led him to consider Fascist violence as a tactic in the life struggle with socialism and Marxism, from which it had emerged the victor.

### 2.4 Theory and History of Historiography

The final, initially unplanned, volume of Croce’s Philosophy of Spirit developed the *Logic’*s thesis of the unity of philosophy and history yet attracted from R. G. Collingwood (1921)[^collingwood-1921] precisely this criticism of descending into naturalism. Croce neatly summarised his views via a distinction between ‘history’ and ‘chronicle’, on the one hand, and the argument that ‘all history is contemporary history’, on the other. In Croce’s theory these two propositions hang together. However, as he made even clearer in his *History as Thought and as Action* (Croce 1938)[^croce-1938], his theory was not simply a methodology for the study and writing of history (though Gardiner 1961 treats it as such)[^gardiner-1961]. He considered all our understanding and activity involved this historical methodology – indeed, it expressed the true nature of the philosophical enterprise itself.

By ‘chronicle’ Croce meant the mere collection of documents and their classification or enumeration via the deployment of certain pseudo concepts. However, he considered chronicle a ‘dead’ past. History came ‘alive’ through being relived in the present and represented in the form of a narrative (Croce 1917a: 3–4, 10–11)[^croce-1917a] So conceived, ‘all true history is contemporary history’ (Croce 1917a: 4)[^croce-1917a]. Criticizing the dictum of the French positivist historian Hippolyte Taine that ‘After the collection of facts, comes the search for causes’, Croce contended that there could be no ‘brute or disconnected’ historical ‘facts’ apart from the explanations that brought them into being. And these explanations could not take the form of a set of social or behavioural laws but must derive from ‘re-living’ them from the point of view of the historical actors involved by adopting their conceptual scheme (Croce 1917a: 54–55)[^croce-1917a]. As he put it in a famous passage (quoted approvingly by Collingwood 1993: 199)[^collingwood-1993]:

> Do you wish to understand the true history of a Neolithic Ligurian or Sicilian? Try, if you can, to become a Neolithic Ligurian or Sicilian in your mind. If you cannot do that, or do not care to, content yourself with describing and arranging in series the skulls, implements and drawings which have been found belonging to these peoples. (Croce 1917a: 119)[^croce-1917a].

As Collingwood noted, this argument related to Croce’s views on the unity of philosophy and history, whereby the universal concepts and definitions of the former always were conjoined with the individual judgements of the latter, which related them to the understanding of specific facts and actions. Without possessing such conceptual knowledge from the inside of the individual actors who made history, one could only classify and enumerate the external objects they had left behind with the aid of pseudo concepts.

This argument raises three issues: first, might there not be conceptual schemes so different to our own – or so incompletely documented – as to be inaccessible to us – so that the life of the Neolithic Ligurian, for example, might forever remain a mystery? Second, does that mean – as Martin Hollis (1968: 243)[^hollis-1968] criticised Peter Winch and E. Evans-Pritchard for apparently believing – that if a particular society believes in witchcraft, then witches exist in that world? And, finally, does this approach entail a distinction between the humanities and the natural sciences.

The first two issues relate to the problems posed by relativism. In his critique of such views, Hollis contended that for a conceptual scheme to be accessible to us there must be certain common modes of rational reasoning that can serve as a ‘bridgehead’ between the historian and those in the past whose thought we are attempting to make sense of (Hollis 1968: 246)[^hollis-1968]. That might allow us to understand why a given people believe in witches, but also to criticise that belief as mistaken – that the evidence they adduce in its support is unfounded or misinterpreted. However, Croce rejected the idea of there being either common pan-historical norms of human reasoning or fundamental problems that all human societies might need to address. Nevertheless, he did not endorse relativism either, since he considered all human thought and action as part of the unfolding of a universal human consciousness or Spirit constantly individualising itself within history. He denied this was a transcendental entity because it operated immanently, through the particular actions of individuals. Finally, our consciousness of the natural world could be similarly assimilated to Spirit. As he continued the passage quoted above:

> Do you wish to understand the true history of a blade of grass? Try to become a blade of grass; and; if you cannot do it, satisfy yourself with analysing its parts, and even arranging them in a sort of ideal or fanciful history. (Croce 1917a: 119)[^croce-1917a]

Collingwood baulked at this further development – arguing he doubted a blade of grass possessed ‘thought’ (Collingwood 1993: 200)[^collingwood-1993]. As he had remarked in a review of the book: ‘there are two Croce’s, the realist, dualist, empiricist, or naturalist … and the idealist, whose life is a warfare upon transcendence and naturalism in all their forms’ (Collingwood 1921)[^collingwood-1921]. In truth, Croce sought to unite the two in ways that rendered the real and the rational one and the same. In the period 1906–23, that union was achieved through faith in the Providential character of history – albeit in a Vichean more than a Hegelian sense. Yet, in seeking to criticise Fascism as a dead end he was forced, as we shall see, to develop a more critical stance to the course of real history.

Meanwhile, Croce drew a more general conclusion regarding the nature of philosophy from his ‘absolute historicism’. In an appendix on ‘Philosophy and Methodology’ added to the Italian edition of the book, he suggested that his theory involved a deflation of the ‘traditional’ view of philosophy as ‘metaphysic’, that sought transcendent, a priori solutions to supposedly permanent and universal problems. Croce countered that there are no general or fundamental problems of philosophy, capable of a definitive solution. As a result, philosophers had lost their status as possessing a special ‘enlightened’ insight into the noumena or true nature of reality lying behind the mere appearances and experiences of the phenomenal world. Instead, philosophy was the historical methodology of thinking about the particular problems that all scholars encounter in their various fields of expertise, and indeed that all individuals employ in their everyday activities. Consequently, ‘every man (within his sphere, however august or wide it may seem) is a philosopher, and every philosopher is a man, inextricably linked to the conditions of human life, which cannot be transcended by any means’ (Croce 1917a: 144)[^croce-1917a]. Indeed, he provocatively considered that no special place existed for the study of philosophy *per se*, suggesting that ‘a powerful improvement of philosophical culture’ would be achieved if the ‘general philosopher’ the ‘*purus* *philosophus*’ disappeared from the academy and instead ‘all scholars of human affairs, jurists, economists, ethicists, literature specialists, in sum all scholars of historical matters, were to become self-conscious and disciplined philosophers’ (Croce 1917a: 145)[^croce-1917a].

### 2.5 Later Developments of the Aesthetic

This period saw Croce’s studies of *Goethe* (1919b); *Ariosto, Shakespeare and Corneille* (1920), and *Dante* (1921) producing a revision of his aesthetic theories that partly reflected working on these great poets. Downplaying the earlier rejection of gradations of expression and emphasis on beauty, he now viewed successful art as constituting a ‘lyrical’ or even a ‘cosmic’ intuition suffused with universal human feeling. (EA 30) Indeed, he claimed art was in a sense ‘universal’ and concerned with the ‘totality’ of human experience:

> In every utterance, every fanciful [imaginative] creation, of the poet, there lies the whole of human destiny, all human hope, illusions, griefs, joys, human grandeurs and miseries, the whole drama of reality perpetually evolving and growing out of itself in suffering and joy. (Croce 1917b, PPH 262)[^croce-1917b]

Given the war one can understand his desire to assert the importance of art for humanity. But in the fascist period that was to become also a moral imperative. Croce had considered art as serving no ethical purpose – it’s only value was beauty (1902 and 1913). But in Croce 1929 he considers the moral sensibility, seen as the capacity to perceive and feel moral emotions, as a necessary condition of the artist:

> The foundation of all poetry is therefore the human personality, and since the human personality fulfils itself morally, the foundation of all poetry is the moral conscience. (PPH 221)

Indeed, Fascism led him to appreciate the importance of the capacity to *act* morally even more.

## 3. Phase Three: Anti-Fascism and The Religion of Liberty

### 3.1 Liberalism as ‘Metapolitical’

1924 has been seen as marking a significant turning point in Croce’s political and philosophical views (Galasso 2000: ch. 13; Garosci 1966)[^galasso-2000][^garosci-1966]. This change coincided with, and was motivated by, his shift to open criticism of the Fascist regime, of which he became the leading intellectual opponent within Italy. Although he acquired that role in drafting the ‘Protest’ against Gentile’s Manifesto of Fascist Intellectuals in 1925, he had already published a set of essays on ‘Politics in a Nutshell’ in 1924, that can be seen as a critique both of the neo-Machiavellian view of the state as the repository of ‘force’ or ‘power’ and the Hegelian ‘ethical state’, both of which had been deployed by Gentile – and, to some degree, by Croce himself – to defend the Fascist seizure of power in 1922.

Croce accused Gentile of equating the ethical state with a ‘governmental’ conception of morality, noting that:

> the moral life embraces the men of government and their adversaries, conservatives and revolutionaries, and perhaps these last more than the others, because they more than the others open up the paths to the future and bring about the advancement of human societies. (Croce 1945b (1924a): 232).

Liberalism shared this insight, recognising:

> the necessity of leaving, as much as possible, free play to the spontaneous and inventive forces of individuals and social groups, because only from these forces can one expect mental, moral and economic progress, and it is only from their free play that the road that history must pursue can be drawn (Croce 1925d: 126)[^croce-1925d].

He contended that strictly speaking this conception of liberalism was ‘metapolitical’ and even a religion (Croce 1945b (1927): 284–5). It reflected the role of individual liberty and the operation of the dialectic in the thought and actions of individuals throughout human history (1939 PPH: 712), and as such encompassed ‘all the moral virtues … which place the end or purpose of conduct in respect for the person of others, in the good of the universal, in the enrichment of spiritual life, in the unceasing betterment of the world (1939 PPH: 709–10). Indeed, he defended it as encompassing the moral core and historical import of Christianity (Croce 1942)[^croce-1942]. While he associated liberalism with a broadly liberal democratic political system, such as that of Italy pre the First World War, he rejected attempts by fellow liberals to link it to either free market capitalism, as Luigi Einaudi did (Croce 1928c)[^croce-1928c], or a socialist welfare state, as De Ruggiero and others inspired by the New Liberalism of T. H. Green and L. T. Hobhouse proposed (Croce 1943a)[^croce-1943a]. In his view, ‘concrete liberal institutions are the *ad hoc* products of political genius inspired by liberty, or what comes to the same, of liberal genius equipped with political prudence’ (1939 PPH: 720). Pragmatism in pursuit of liberty thereby became his political ideal, qualities he identified with Cavour and Giolitti, the heroes respectively of his *History of Europe in the Nineteenth Century* (1932) and the *History of Italy from 1871–1915* (1928).

### 3.2 History as the History of Liberty

Croce’s embrace of an explicitly liberal politics required commensurate changes to his philosophies of practice and history and their underlining account of human agency. Hitherto, he had conceived practical action as ‘economic’ and aimed at the ‘Useful’. Individuals should act in the most instrumentally effective way in the given historical conditions, with the moral value of their actions a matter of faith in the inner rationality of human history. That had led to a ‘realist’ account of politics as ‘realpolitik’ and focussed on ‘reasons of state’. However, he now adopted an ‘ethical-political’ view of history, that included:

> Not only the state, the government of the state and the expansion of the state, but also what is outside the state, whether it cooperates with it or tries to modify it, overthrow it and replace it: namely, the formation of moral institutions, in the broadest sense of the word, including religious institutions and revolutionary sects, including customs, fancies and myths that are practical in tendency and content. (Croce 1924b: 338)[^croce-1924b].

The primacy accorded to the ‘ethical’ element reflected the emphasis he now placed on individual freedom of thought and action and the resulting plurality of competing visions of the future within a liberal society. Yet, this pluralism raised the issue of whether historical circumstances placed any constraint on human agency.

His *History as Thought and as Action* (1938) addressed this issue. All action, he continued to believe, arose out of a historically conditioned ‘need’ or ‘problem’ in one of the four dimensions of theoretical and practical endeavor. Whatever the problem, a response required knowledge of the appropriate ‘real situation’ in which we find ourselves, and hence of ‘the process of reality as it has hitherto developed’. Such knowledge ‘is thus called historical’ and applied to all fields of human endeavour (Croce 1938: 4)[^croce-1938]. Thus, a scientific question involved an understanding of the history of science and the adequacy or otherwise of current theories in explaining a given natural phenomenon, while a practical question rested on an understanding of the historical circumstances in which one found oneself and the applicability of prevailing norms in addressing it. All present action thereby rested on historical thought.

As ever, Croce sought a path between the rational and the empirical that avoided both the radical idealism of Gentile’s ‘actualism’ and the causal necessity of historical materialism. Though ‘we are products of the past and live immersed in a past that envelops us on every side’, he argued we advance ‘through thought, which does not break the link with the past but grows upwards out of it in an ideal growth, and converts it into knowledge’ (Croce 1938: 31)[^croce-1938]. Historical knowledge thereby becomes the premiss for new action even in ‘our daily behaviour’, when ‘we determine what we had best do’(Croce 1938: 31)[^croce-1938]. In this way, ‘historical thought grasps the past to turn it to use, and transfigures it into its object, and thus the composition of history is our liberation from history’ (Croce 1938: 31)[^croce-1938]. History thereby becomes ‘the history of liberty’ – not, he insisted, understood as the progressive realisation of a particular ideal of liberty, but as the product of the free thought and action of individuals as they assess their circumstances in thought in order to transform them through action (Croce 1938: 46)[^croce-1938]. As he was to put it, thought and action became manifestations of a vital spark (‘vitalità’) within the human spirit (Croce 1947)[^croce-1947].

Yet, he allowed there could be many theories of our historical situation. Repeating his earlier dictum that all history is “contemporary history”, he remarked that was not only because historians must ‘relive’ it in the present but also because they do so in response to a ‘present need’ (Croce 1938: 5)[^croce-1938]. As a result, ‘the present condition of my soul … constitutes the document, the living and internal document, of historical judgement’ (Croce 1938: 6)[^croce-1938]. Does that make all history subjective? To a degree it does. Croce openly admitted his *History of Europe in the Nineteenth Century* was a peon to the liberal ideal, with its hope that its restoration might lead to a ‘Europe union’ able to end ‘nationalist competition … and the whole psychology that clings’ to it not a ‘prediction’ but rather an indication ‘of the paths the moral conscience and observations of the present offer to those who agree with the narrative given in this history’ (Croce 1932: 358–9)[^croce-1932]. Yet, his insistence that all history is the history of thought also allowed him to condemn racial histories as based not on biological fact but the ungrounded prejudices of the historian (Croce 1938: 301–05)[^croce-1938], and to regard periods of authoritarian rule – be they the absolute monarchies of the counter-reformation (Croce 1929a)[^croce-1929a] or the fascist regime that he regarded as their modern equivalent – as historical dead-ends due to their suppression of free thought and action (Croce 1930; 1943b; 1944: 9)[^croce-1930].

## 4. Conclusion

Croce’s philosophy has tended to be treated as involving discreet contributions to the study of aesthetics and history. His philosophical writings in these areas have generally been studied independently not only from each other and but also from his own writings as a literary critic and a historian. Meanwhile, his writings on ethics and politics are largely ignored by philosophers outside Italy. These too have been treated as discrete contributions to the politics of the day, and mostly explored by political historians of Italy.

However, Croce regarded his writings as forming a unity that he intended to fulfil a broader cultural purpose. His philosophy of history should not be understood as providing methodological guidance to historians on how to write history, any more than his philosophy of aesthetics offers advice to artists, authors or critics. Rather, it aims to serve as a more general methodology underlying how we can attain knowledge of the world and act to address the particular theoretical or practical problems that arise for us in whatever activity we may be engaged.

Applying Croce’s philosophy to himself, as he was wont to do (Croce 1945a)[^croce-1945a], the development of his thought cannot be understood as an entirely internal evolution – an attempt to overcome certain logical incoherencies or deficiencies in his argument. It was also stimulated by and sought to address external challenges – not least the First World War, with its implications for the Germanic project of collective reconciliation within the moral life of the state that had inspired his own, and the rise of Marxism and Fascism as rival historical and political visions. This led him to give primacy to the personal dimension of his philosophy, as a way individuals could ascribe meaning to existence through their understanding and engagement with the world through history. From this perspective, the authority of the state rested on its capacity to give free play to such individual activity to the whole of humanity – these became the only justifiable ‘reasons of state’ – and indeed of religious authority. Yet, as Michael Rosen has recently argued (Rosen 2022: 200, that leaves unanswered Theodor Adorno’s observation regarding the metaphysical significance of Auschwitz ‘in making a real Hell from human evil’. As Rosen remarks, if the Lisbon earthquake destroyed the religious theodicy of a world created ‘by an omnipresent, omniscient, benevolent God’ for the ‘promotion of human well-being’, Auschwitz challenges ‘the humanistic conception of history as a project of collective self-realisation’ by rendering ‘convictions about collective human progress unsustainable.’

Croce considered Fascism a parenthesis in Italian history (1944: 9). It’s harder to treat the horror of Auschwitz as a mere historical anomaly. The nearest Croce came to responding to this dilemma was in an essay of 1949 involving an imagined conversation between a Neapolitan philosopher (a fictional version of himself) and Hegel. Discussing the Hegelian dictum whereby ‘that which is real is rational and that which is rational is real’, which lay at the heart of his historicist faith, Croce confessed how:

> the irrefutable truth of that aphorism sometimes seems to waver in those who feel the very real and terrifying presence of the evil they are fighting; and therefore it must be added that the duality of the rational and the real, abolished by historical thought, is established and constantly re-established and held firm by the practical and moral consciousness, which through it defines the terms of itself (the *Sein* and the *Sollen*), which is not theoretical truth but practical and moral action. Thus, all those who fear some unknown disappearance of moral consciousness from the world, and an alignment of evil with good, and the replacement of moral judgment and action by the brutality of fact, should be reassured. (Croce 1949: 6)[^croce-1949].

Here he seems to acknowledge that a universal humanism finds a fragile basis in faith in the rationality of the real as manifested in the development of reason and the moral conscience through history. Ultimately, our faith must lie with ‘morally good’ individuals having the courage and capacity to act well even in dark times.
