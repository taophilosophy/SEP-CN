# Medieval Theories of Future Contingents
**First published Thu Jun 15, 2006; substantive revision Wed Apr 9, 2025**

*Frost, Gloria, Knuuttila, Simo*

The philosophical debate concerning the truth-value of singular statements about future contingents derives from Chapter 9 of Aristotle’s treatise *De interpretatione* (*Peri hermeneias*). In Chapters 7–8, Aristotle deals with the contradictory pairs of assertoric statements which divide truth and falsity so that one is true and the other is false. In Chapter 9 he raises the question of whether this holds for all assertoric statements or whether there might be an exception concerning statements about singular future events or states of affairs which are neither necessary nor impossible and hence may take place, or may not. Aristotle’s famous example is tomorrow’s sea battle. Is the prediction that there will be a sea battle tomorrow necessarily true, if it is true, and does its truth entail that the sea battle is inevitable? After a discussion of deterministic arguments and the absurdity of fatalism (18a34–19a22), Aristotle gives his ambiguous reply in 19a23–39. Boethius’s (c. 480–524) Latin translation of *De interpretatione* gave rise to a long commentary tradition through the Middle Ages, beginning from the 11th century. Boethius’s own two commentaries were very influential as well and provided information about other ancient commentaries (Magee 2010)[^magee-2010]. Chapter 9 was also dealt with in theological commentaries on Peter Lombard’s *Sentences* in the context of discussing God’s knowledge of contingents (I.38) and in separate questions (Isaac 1953; Lohr 1967–1974; Craig 1988; Marenbon 2000; Braakhuis & Kneepkens 2003a; Knuuttila 2010)[^braakhuis-2003a][^craig-1988][^isaac-1953][^knuuttila-2010][^lohr-1967][^lohr-1967-2][^lohr-1974][^marenbon-2000]. Al-Fārābī’s (c. 870–950) commentary on *De interpretatione* was influential in medieval Arabic philosophy; Ibn Rushd (Averroes) (1126–1198) wrote another Arabic commentary. (For future contingents in medieval Arabic thought, see Marmura 1985; Adamson 2006; Kaukua 2024.)[^adamson-2006][^kaukua-2024][^marmura-1985]

## 1. From Aristotle to Boethius

### 1.1 Aristotle on Future Contingents: Competing Interpretations in Antiquity and Contemporary Scholarship

The discussion about future contingents in modern philosophical logic was revived by J. Łukasiewicz’s works on three-valued logic (1957, 1967). He thought that in order to avoid fatalistic consequences, one should admit that the principle of bivalence (for any proposition *p*, either *p* is true or *p* is false) does not hold good for future contingent propositions. Arguing that this was realized by Aristotle in Chapter 9 of *De interpretatione*, Łukasiewicz attempted to formalize Aristotle’s position by introducing a third truth-value (neither true nor false) for future contingent propositions and giving three-valued truth-tables for truth-functional basic connectives. Many authors have followed Łukasiewicz in taking Aristotle to restrict the principle of bivalence, though they have not ascribed to Aristotle the details of Łukasiewicz’s system, which is problematic in many respects. In arguing for the deterministic consequences of unrestricted bivalence, Łukasiewicz himself assumed, and took Aristotle to assume, that the truth of a proposition entails the necessity of its truth. Logical determinism, therefore, is to be avoided by qualifying the truth-values of future contingent propositions. The critics of this view do not see any entailment relation between truth and necessity, some of them maintaining that the illusion of logical determinism has its roots in a tacit oscillation between a temporal and atemporal predication of truth. The temporalized reading may be associated with diachronic determination, certainty, necessity and other time-dependent qualifications. Mixing these with non-deterministic atemporal predications yields the wrong idea of fatalism (von Wright 1984: 52–67)[^wright-1984]. It is argued that even the temporalized predication of truth carries no deterministic implications. (Sorabji 1980: 96–103; Sorabji’s example is that “even if it has already been true that I am going to swim, I still retain the power to make it to have been false”.)[^sorabji-1980]

The majority of Aristotelian scholars believe that the argument in *De interpretatione* 9 aims at avoiding fatalism by restricting bivalence for future contingent predictions (Frede 1970, 1985; Sorabji 1980; Craig 1988; Gaskin 1995; Crivelli 2004; Weidemann 2012)[^craig-1988][^crivelli-2004][^frede-1970][^frede-1985][^gaskin-1995][^sorabji-1980][^weidemann-2012]. The second interpretation insists that Aristotle’s point is not to deny the truth-value of these statements, but their being true in the same way as other statements are true. The difference is that while the truth of other statements is necessary, the truth of future contingent predictions is not yet similarly determined. This interpretation is associated with Aristotle’s remark that not everything which is actual and therefore necessary is necessary without qualification (19a23). This is taken to mean that the necessity of an event at a certain time does not imply that it was inevitable. Aristotle speaks about singular future possibilities which may be realized or remain unrealized and which may or may not cease to be antecedent possibilities (see *De interpretatione* 19a13–17, *Nicomachean Ethics* III.5, 1114a17–19, *Metaphysics* VI.3). In the same way predictions may begin to be necessarily true or necessarily false. (For various versions of this approach, see Anscombe 1956; Rescher 1963; Hintikka 1973; Fine 1984; Judson 1988.)[^anscombe-1956][^fine-1984][^hintikka-1973][^judson-1988][^rescher-1963]

The former interpretation, which denies bivalence for future contingent statements, is called the ‘traditional’ or ‘standard’ interpretation. Some Stoics read Aristotle in this way, as Boethius reports in his second commentary on Aristotle’s *De interpretatione*:

> Now some people – the Stoics among them – thought that Aristotle says that future contingents are neither true nor false. For they interpreted his saying that nothing [of that sort] is disposed more to being than to not being as meaning that it makes no difference whether they are thought false or true; for they considered them to be neither true nor false [in Aristotle’s view], but falsely. (*In Perihermeneias* II, 208.1–4, trans. by N. Kretzmann.)

On the basis of a note in Simplicius’s commentary on Aristotle’s *Categories* (407.6–13), it is argued that some Aristotelians also qualified the principle of bivalence. (For this and other ancient deviations from the principle, see Sorabji 1980: 92–93.)[^sorabji-1980] On the other hand, future contingent propositions were treated as true or false in Stoic logic. Some hold that the Stoics took the universally valid principle of bivalence to imply the predetermination of all future events. (See Cicero, *De fato*, 20–21; Bobzien 1998: 59–86.)[^bobzien-1998] (For an argument that the Stoics avoided determinism despite their application of bivalence to future contingent propositions, see de Harven 2016.)[^harven-2016]

Boethius regarded the Stoic interpretation of Aristotle’s view as mistaken, his interpretation being based on the terms ‘definitely’ true or false and ‘indefinitely’ true or false. These same terms were also used in Ammonius’s commentary (c. 435/445–517/526). Since this work was not known to Boethius, both authors seem to have known some earlier Greek discussions in which these qualifications were introduced. (Ammonius’s Greek commentary on *De interpretatione* 9 is translated by D. Blank and Boethius’s Latin commentaries by N. Kretzmann in the same volume, with interpretative essays by R. Sorabji, N. Kretzmann and M. Mignucci in 1998.)[^kretzmann-1998][^mignucci-1998]

### 1.2 Boethius on Future Contingents and God’s Knowledge of Contingents

The contemporary reconstructions of what Ammonius and Boethius meant can be divided into two groups. According to one interpretation, Ammonius and Boethius ascribe to Aristotle the view that the predictions of future contingent events and their denials differ from other contradictory pairs, because truth and falsity are not definitely distributed between them and the propositions are consequently neither definitely true nor definitely false. In answering the Stoic criticism, Boethius might have thought that future contingent propositions have the disjunctive property of being true-or-false, which would mean something other than simply lacking a truth value. (For various interpretations of how Boethius restricted bivalence, see Frede 1985; Craig 1988; Gaskin 1995; Kretzmann 1998.)[^craig-1988][^frede-1985][^gaskin-1995][^kretzmann-1998] Another interpretation holds that Boethian future contingents are not definitely true or false because their truth-makers are not yet determined, but are true or false in an indeterminate way. No qualification of the principle of bivalence is involved. True statements are either definitely true or simply (indefinitely) true or false (Mignucci 1988, 1998; 2020; Seel 2000; Beets 2003 for Boethius’s first commentary)[^beets-2003][^mignucci-1988][^mignucci-1998][^seel-2000]. According to both interpretations, Ammonius and Boethius assumed that the definite truth of predictions directly implies determinism and that Aristotle denied the definite truth of all future contingent propositions; it is less clear how they understood the indefinite truth of these (Sorabji 1998a)[^sorabji-1998a]. Boethius’s formulations often suggest that future contingent propositions are true-or-false. An additional reason for not treating them as simply true or false is his view of the necessity of the present. In commenting on Aristotle’s remark that “ what is necessarily is when it is” (*De interpretatione* 19a23), Boethius explains that even though present things are temporally or conditionally necessary, they may be generically or antecedently contingent (*In Perihermeneias* I, 121.20–122.4; II, 241.7–243.28). He apparently thinks that the truth of future contingent statements implies that things cannot be otherwise, for the assumed actuality of the relevant future truth-makers means that alternative diachronic prospectives are rendered temporally impossible.

In his longer commentary on *De interpretatione*, Boethius says of God that “he knows future events as happening contingently and not necessarily so that he does not ignore the possibility that something else might take place” (*In Perihermeneias* II, 226.9–12). Boethius also stresses that since the truth of future contingents is not accessible to humans, predictions of the form “A sea battle will take place tomorrow” are false in the sense that they assert the truth of something that cannot be known (*In Perihermeneias* II, 211.26–213.18). In his later treatise *Consolatio philosophiae* Boethius states that “I do not think that anybody would say that those things which are happening now were not going to happen before they happened” (V. 4.19) In this treatise, Boethius argues that God is atemporal and has the totality of changing history present to him. God’s knowledge is thus not foreknowledge since it is not temporally located, but the predictions of future contingents are true or false from the point of view of God’s eternal knowledge of the things referred to (V.6, 25–32; see also Augustine, *City of God* 11.21). According to Boethius, it is necessary that if God knows that *p*, then *p*. This ‘conditional necessity’ does not imply the ‘simple necessity’ of *p* (*Consolatio philosophiae* V, 6.27–30). Some medieval authors read this distinction in Boethius and Augustine as a modal placement solution to the problem of foreknowledge and free will (Matthews 2004)[^matthews-2004]. Thomas Aquinas (1224/5–1274), for example, refers to Boethius’s distinction using the terms *necessitas consequentiae* and *necessitas consequentis* (*Summa contra gentiles* I.67; *De veritate* I.23, ad 13). For Boethius, see Evans 2004; Marenbon 2005; Sharples 2009; Marenbon 2013.

Boethius’s remarks on modalities have led some authors to analyze them by using models derived from modern temporal or tense logic with branching time theory developed by Prior, Burgess, Thomason and others. In applying these to Boethius, Bornholdt states that the models shed light on Boethius’s approach even though they were not intended by Boethius himself (2017: 49; see also Martin 2004: 53–63)[^martin-2004]. Bornholdt extends his formal analysis to later medieval discussions as well, calling it the Boethian model. For similar non-historical reconstructions of time logic in some late medieval theories of future contingents, see the entry on future contingents.

## 2. Medieval Interpretations of Aristotle

### 2.1 Peter Abelard and an anonymous 12th century commentator

In light of the influence of Aristotle’s works in the Middle Ages, figures throughout the period offered interpretations of Aristotle’s views on the truth-value of future contingent propositions. Like with present day scholars, there were varying views about how to read Aristotle’s position. Mignucci’s account of how Ammonius and Boethius understood the distinction between definite and indefinite truth is roughly speaking the same as the explanation of these terms in Peter Abelard’s (1079–1142) interpretation of *De interpretatione* 9. Abelard believed that Aristotle assumed that future contingent statements are true or false, though not determinately true or false before the actuality of the things to which they refer (*Dialectica* 213.17–20). Abelard used the notions of ‘determinate/indeterminate’ instead of the Boethian ‘definite/indefinite’, as most medieval authors did. In this interpretation, he retained the principle of bivalence for all assertoric statements, but rejected the universal application of the stronger principle that every assertoric statement is determinately true or determinately false. According to Abelard, the notions of ‘determinate’ or ‘indeterminate’ apply primarily to the truth-makers of assertions and secondarily to assertions themselves:

> But as the obtaining of future contingent states of affairs is indeterminate, similarly the propositions which enunciate these are said to be indeterminately true or false, for those which are true are indeterminately true and those which are false are indeterminately false, in acccordance with the indeterminate obtaining of what is predicted. *Dialectica* 211.30–32)

Abelard was interested in the question of whether the present truth of statements about future contingent events are themselves determinate; i. e., whether “The sentence ‘Socrates will eat tomorrow’ is true”, if true, is determinately true. Denying this, he restricted the principle that all past and present true propositions are determinately true. Abelard also distinguished between determinateness and certainty. Future contingent propositions may be certain if they are revealed by angels, for example, but they are not knowable of themselves and not determinately true. (See *Glossae super Peri hermeneias* IX.19–42; *Dialectica* 211.32–212.23; Lewis 1987; Binini 2020, 2022: 276–294.)[^binini-2020][^binini-2022][^lewis-1987]

The ‘traditional’ view of truth and bivalence in Aristotle is put forward in an anonymous early 12th-century commentary on *De interpretatione*, edited by M. Dal Pra, which is often, and probably mistakenly, attributed to Abelard. Applying Boethius’s distinction between definitely and indefinitely true statements, the author argues that future contingent statements are merely disjunctively true or false (*tantum sub disjunctione*); 100.13–19; 112.8–113.3). They are true-or-false.

### 2.2 13th and 14th century commentators

While Abelard thought that the universal validity of the principle of bivalence was an Aristotelian view, 13th-century commentators argued for the traditional interpretation. Albert the Great (1206–1280) and Thomas Aquinas took Aristotle to be arguing in *De interpretatione* 9 that if every contradictory pair divides truth and falsity in a determinate way and the members are consequently determinately true or false, all things must determinately be or not be. They thought that Aristotle then refuted the consequent by referring to various contingent things. It follows that the antecedent cannot be true and future contingent propositions are not determinately true or determinately false. They are true-or-false under disjunction (Albert the Great, *Liber Perihermeneias*, I.5.4–6, 418–422; Thomas Aquinas, *In libros Perihermeneias*, I.13, 170–172; I.15, 202–203). The same interpretation of Aristotle is found in Arabic commentators Abū Nasr al-Fārābī and Averroes. (See *Al-Farabi’s* *Commentary and Short Treatise on Aristotle’s* De interpretatione, trans. F. Zimmermann, lxviii, 75–76, 78–79, 91–92, 244–245, and Averroes’s *Middle Commentary on Aristotle’s* De interpretatione, 82va.) Al-Fārābī’s own view was that future contingents are either true or false (94–96; Marmura 1985; Adamson 2006)[^adamson-2006][^marmura-1985].

The traditional interpretation of Aristotle’s view was put forward by many late 13th and 14th century Latin commentators such as John Duns Scotus (1265/6–1308), *Quaestiones in libros Perihermenias Aristotelis*, I.7–8 (179–181), trans. Buckner and Zupko; Walter Burley (ca. 1275–1344), *Commentarius in librum Perihermeneias Aristotelis*, 92, 95–96; Peter Auriol (ca. 1280–1322), *Scriptum in I Sententiarum* I.38.3, 817–828; William Ockham (ca. 1287–1347), *Expositio in Librum Perihermenias Aristotelis* I.6.15; Gregory of Rimini (ca. 1300–1358), *Lectura super I Sententiarum* I.38.1.1 (238–243); Peter of Ailly (1351–1420), *Quaestiones super libros Sententiarum* I.11.1A). In his *Supercommentary on Averroes’s Commentary on Aristotle’s* De interpretatione, the Jewish philosopher Levi ben Gerson (Gersonides, 1288–1344) states that positing a truth value for future contingents leads to absurdity (83r; Rudavsky 1985: 166). However, John Buridan (ca. 1300–1361) read Aristotle in the same way as Abelard. All assertoric statements are true or false though those about future contingents are not determinately true or false (*Questiones longe super librum Perihermeneias* I.10).

Since theologians usually thought that divine omniscience and prophecy presupposed unrestricted bivalence, the discussion of future contingents was divided into historical constructions of Aristotle’s view, mostly in accordance with the traditional interpretation, and the systematic discussions in theology that mostly refuted this for doctrinal reasons. Later examples of the traditional interpretation of Aristotle’s view include George of Brussels (d. 1510), *Logica magistri Georgii inserto textu Bricoti*, 51rv; Iodocus Trutfetter (1460–1519), *Summulae totius logicae* II.1 (LL3v–4v); *Veteris artis…Perihermeneiasque expositio* (g1r); see also Baudry 1989 (ed.), *La querelle des futures contingents (Louvain 1465–1475). Textes inédits*, 206, which Schabel 1995–6: 401–404 shows was authored by Guillaume Baudin (late 15th cen.). (For medieval interpretations, see Weidemann 1994; Gaskin 1995; Knuuttila 2010.)[^gaskin-1995][^knuuttila-2010][^weidemann-1994]

## 3. Medieval Developments from 1000–1200

### 3.1 Distinctions Between Types of Modalities and Modal Statements

In discussing statements such as “What is known by God necessarily takes place”, Anselm of Canterbury (1033–1109) distinguished between the antecedent and consequent necessity of things which are known. The latter is the necessity of a thing’s actuality which is caused by the actuality of the thing itself. The former is the necessity by which a thing exists because of an external cause (*Cur Deus homo* 2.17, 125.6–126.2). Neither antecedent knowledge nor antecedent truth has a compelling effect on things. In explaining Mary’s believing the truth of a prophetic statement concerning the death of Christ, Anselm writes:

> Therefore, since her faith was true faith, it was necessary that things would be as she believed. But if you are once again disturbed by my saying “It was necessary…”, remember that the truth of the virgin’s faith was not the cause of his dying freely but that her faith was true faith because this was going to happen. (*Cur Deus homo* 2.17, 124.27–125.3)

Anselm thought that future contingent statements based on divine revelation were true without deterministic implications. A similar view was held by most medieval theologians when they addressed divine omniscience and prophesy. For Anselm’s view, see Knuuttila 2004.

William of Champeaux (c. 1070–1122) dealt with an argument against the compatibility between contingency and divine omniscience which was discussed in Augustine’s *City of God* (V.9). In response to Cicero’s *De fato* and *De divinatione*, Augustine refuted the claim that if events can happen in any way other than God has foreseen, then God can be in error. William stated that the antecedent of the argument is true but the consequent is false and therefore the consequence is not valid (Lottin 1959: 195; Marenbon 1997: 226–227)[^lottin-1959][^marenbon-1997]. Peter Abelard, in discussing the same example, applied the systematic division between modal statements *de sensu* or in the compounded sense and modal statements *de re* or in the divided sense. (For Abelard’s modal terminology, see *Glossae super Peri hermeneias* XII.3–46. Later authors used the expression *de dicto* instead of *de sensu.*) Abelard’s analysis of Cicero’s argument was often repeated in medieval theology, since it was put forward in slightly modified form in Peter Lombard’s *Sententiae* (c. 1157), which became the standard medieval introduction to theology. Abelard states that when the proposition “A thing can be otherwise than God knows it to be” is read as a modal proposition *de sensu* (It is possible that a thing is otherwise than God knows it to be), the antecedent is false and the possibility of God’s error as a consequent would not be justified even if the consequence were valid. When the antecedent is read *de re* (A thing that is now somehow could be otherwise than God knows it to be), the proposition is true, but the consequent about God’s error is false, for if things were otherwise, God would know them as they would be. This shows that the consequence is not well formulated. Following Abelard, Peter Lombard formulated the same view in stating that “Things cannot be other than as God foreknows them” is true in the compounded sense and false in the divided sense. The truth of the compounded sense saves God’s infallibility and the falsity of the divided sense expresses God’s freedom and the metaphysical contingency of the future (Peter Lombard, *Sententiae* I.38.2; Peter Abelard, *Glossae super Peri hermeneias* IX.86–99; *Dialectica*, 217.27–219.24). It is assumed here that when a temporally definite statement is true, its denial may be possibly true.

### 3.2 The Distinction Between Propositions and Their Content

Early medieval logicians could deal with singular future statements as statement types with truth-values changing in accordance with the time of assertion, as in Aristotle and Hellenistic philosophy. (For the prevalence of temporally indefinite sentences in ancient philosophy, see Hintikka 1973: Ch. 4; Bobzien 1998: 66–67, 100–101, 109–111.)[^bobzien-1998][^hintikka-1973] In the theological context of timeless divine omniscience, it was more natural to regard these statements as temporally definite. Abelard calls statements propositions (*propositio*) and what is asserted by a statement its *dictum.* He considered that a statement is true or false when its *dictum* is true or false. In dealing with prophesy, Abelard suggested that the *dictum* about a singular event is first expressed by a future tense proposition and then by present and past tense propositions. (For the nature of the *dictum*, contrasted with the ‘facts’ and ‘propositions’ of twentieth-century philosophical jargon, see Marenbon 1997: 202–209)[^marenbon-1997].

The view of a fixed propositional content was developed further by 12th-century authors who were later called *nominales.* One of the theses of this group was “What is once true is always true”. (See Iwakuma and Ebbesen 1992: 196, 199–201, 205–206 for the relevant texts. For the history of the principle, see also Marenbon 1992: 58–61 and Ebbesen 1992: 73–74.)[^ebbesen-1992][^iwakuma-1992][^marenbon-1992] This thesis was employed in the discussions of how the beliefs of Abraham and others who lived before the coming of Christ and believed various things about him were the same as the beliefs of those who live after His coming. The reason for asking this was that the previous beliefs were formulated in future tense statements and the latter in present or past tense statements. According to the *nominales*, one could regard as basic a temporally definite propositional content the meaning of which is expressed by various tensed expressions depending on when they are uttered. While tensed statements about temporally definite singular events have a changing truth-value, the corresponding non-tensed *dicta* are unchangingly true or false (Nuchelmans 1973: 177–189; for some later examples, see also Nuchelmans 1980; Lewis 1995; Goris 2001)[^goris-2001][^lewis-1995][^nuchelmans-1973][^nuchelmans-1980]. Peter of Poitiers (c. 1130–1205), one of the authors taking this approach, argued that while the singular statements about contingent things are immutably true or false because of God’s eternal choice, their unchanging truth-value could be otherwise. God does not know contingent things through tensed statements, since their truth-value is changeable. If God’s knowledge is described by using tensed statements, analogously to the articles of faith before and after the coming of Christ, one should read them so that they signify the same (Peter of Poitiers, *Sententiae* I.7, 133–143; I.12.199–223; I.14.328–253). This became a well-known position since it was also employed in Peter Lombard’s *Sententiae* (I.39.1; I.41.3).

### 3.3 Rejection of the Necessity of the Present

The theological formulations by Peter Abelard, Peter Lombard and Peter of Poitiers discussed above exemplify 12th-century deviations from the Aristotelian thesis “What is necessarily is when it is”. This was traditionally understood as implying the principle of the necessity of the present, which was not questioned in ancient modal theories (Knuuttila 1993: Ch. 1)[^knuuttila-1993]. Since God’s knowledge about contingent things was regarded as unchangeable, the contingency of this knowledge (in the sense that it could be different) also implied the denial of the Aristotelian equation of immutability with necessity, a denial regarded as an explicit doctrine of the *nominales* (Iwakuma and Ebbesen 1992: 194)[^ebbesen-1992][^iwakuma-1992]. The new modal paradigm, which is more or less consciously applied in these discussions of God’s will, power, and knowledge, could be characterized as the model of simultaneous or synchronic alternatives. There are three main examples of it in early medieval philosophy.

Abelard assumes that at a given instant of time, what is actual is necessary as no longer avoidable, but he argues that unrealized alternatives are possible at the same time in the sense that they could have happened at that time. Some of the alternatives of a singular being are real counterfactual alternatives. These are unrealizable because of some previous change in the conditions of the subject, and some are merely imaginable alternatives, such as Socrates’s being a bishop, which never had a real basis in things. While often employing traditional ideas about necessity and possibility, Abelard also developed ideas pertaining to simultaneous alternatives. (See Martin 2003; Knuuttila 2008: 537–538; Binini 2022: 255–261.)[^binini-2022][^knuuttila-2008][^martin-2003]

Gilbert of Poitiers (1085/90–1154) stressed the idea that natural regularities which are called natural necessities are not absolute, since they are chosen by God and can be overridden by divine power. This had become a widespread theological view in Gilbert’s time. Gilbert explained it in the light of the Augustinian view of God’s acting by choice between alternative providential options and omnipotence as an executive power. There is an interesting formulation of Plato’s ‘Platonitas’ in Gilbert. This is said to include all of what Plato was, is and will be as well as what he could be but never is. Even though Gilbert does not explain why there is a modal element in the individual concept, it was probably needed in order to speak about Plato in alternative possible histories or, as in Abelard, about Socrates as a bishop. Gilbert seems to have been the first to formulate an individual concept in this way (Knuuttila 1993, 75–82; Marenbon 2007: 158–159)[^knuuttila-1993][^marenbon-2007].

A third context of the systematic interest in simultaneous alternatives was the new 12th-century theory that declarative singular statements or their contents should be primarily treated as temporally definite and as having an unchanging truth-value. All propositional contents pertaining to contingent things have a truth-value on the basis of God’s eternal choice. These truth-values would be otherwise if the providential design of the world were different in relevant respects. Robert Grosseteste (1168/75–1253) taught that the opposites of actualized contingent things are no longer realizable possibilities, though they are possible alternatives in the sense that they could have been included in God’s eternal providential choice. Actual history is an explication of one of the divine alternatives with respect to which things are primarily called necessary, possible or impossible. Necessities and possibilities at this basic level are called modalities ‘from eternity and without beginning’. Mathematical truths exemplify these ‘simple’ necessities. In addition, there are necessities and impossibilities which have a beginning and which are eternal contingencies in the sense that they depend on God’s free choice (*De libero arbitrio* 168.26–170.33; 178.28–29). The contingency of the divine acts of knowledge and will is associated with an atemporal causal priority between the powers and their acts (178.24–26). Grosseteste’s views are compared with those of Duns Scotus in Lewis (1996)[^lewis-1996].

This was an innovative theory for dealing with future contingent statements as omnitemporally true or omnitemporally false or, if these were tensed, as antecedently true or antecedently false. The truth-values of statements about contingent things, though temporally immutable from the beginning of the world and also immutably known by God, were metaphysically contingent. The consequence from knowledge or truth to necessity, whether causal or semantic, was denied. The contingency of the created order was based on God’s eternal and and free providential choice that involved the acts of the created will as free. Anselm formulated this traditional view, following Augustine, as follows: “It is both necessary that God foreknows what will come to be and that God foreknows that something will freely come to be” (*De concordia praescientiae et praedestinationis et gratiae Dei cum libero arbitrio*, 1.1; cf. Augustine, *On Free Choice of the Will*, 3.3.8). Anselm argued that that predictions are true or false and their truth can be understood as the correspondence to what will happen, although what will contingently happen is not known to human beings without supernatural help. This was the standard view of the possibility of prophesy. (See the above quotation from Anselm’s *Cur Deus homo*; Peter Abelard, *Glossae super Peri hermeneias*, IX.69–85; Anonymous, *Summa Duacensis*, 129–130.)

## 4. Later Medieval Views on Future Contingents and Divine Foreknowledge

Later medieval figures discussed future contingents in several contexts, such as in commentaries on Aristotle’s *Perihermeneias* and in questions about God’s foreknowledge and prophesies about future contingent realities. Later medieval figures were well acquainted with the conception of God’s eternal and timeless knowledge in Augustine and Boethius and this view continued to play a role in later discussions. As we will see below, some figures, such as Thomas Aquinas maintain that future contingent realities are eternally present to God, while others, such as John Duns Scotus, reject this view and develop alternative theories to explain God’s knowledge of contingents.

### 4.1 Thomas Aquinas

Aquinas discusses the truth-values of future contingents in his commentary on Aristotle’s *Perihermeneias*. He assumes that if future propositions have truth-values, then the realities which they are about must be determined by their antecedent causes (*In libros Perihermeneias* I.13). Aquinas’s position on this matter depends on his views about the connection between truth and being. In Aquinas’s view, propositions about the present are true in virtue of conforming to a presently existing reality. Since future realities do not yet exist, Aquinas believes that propositions about the future can only be true in virtue of conforming to a presently existing cause that will deterministically produce the corresponding future reality. Thus, future propositions about necessary events, such as eclipses, can be true in virtue of their presently existing causes. However, future contingent events cannot be known through their causes since, given their contingent nature, their causes may or may not produce them. Like Boethius, Aquinas regards future contingent propositions as disjunctively true or false. Human beings can only make conjectures about which contingents will be realized in the future based on the various degrees of probabilities corresponding to certain types of causes (*In libros Perihermeneias* I.13; Osborne 2008, 227–232)[^osborne-2008].

To explain how God has certain knowledge of future contingents, Aquinas claims that all things are timelessly present to God, who has direct knowledge of them and their temporal order, though none of them is past or future with respect to His cognition (*Scriptum super libros Sententiarum* I.38.1.4–5; *Summa contra gentiles* I.67; *Summa theologiae* I.14.13). Accordingly, those realities which are future from the perspective of human beings are present to God’s eternal vision and thus, known by God with certainty. Aquinas thinks that when humans prophecy it is due to God impressing his knowledge on the human intellect (*Summa theologiae* II–2, 171.3, 6). For various interpretations of Aquinas’s views on divine foreknowledge, see Wippel 1985; Craig 1988: 99–126; Goris 1996; Stump 2003; Marenbon 2005; Knuuttila 2010.

Medieval critics found the idea that each instant of time is present to God’s non-temporal eternal vision problematic because of the transitivity and symmetry of the relation of simultaneity. (See John Duns Scotus, *Lectura* I.39.1–5, 23, 28, 87, and further references in Hoenen 1993: 169–70.)[^hoenen-1993] It would seem to follow that if the past, present and future are present to God’s eternal vision, then each moment of time is also present to every other moment of time and the past and future exist in the same way as the present (Shanley 1997 addresses this objection from Aquinas’s perspective)[^shanley-1997]. Contemporary scholars of Aquinas continue to debate about whether Aquinas believed that the future exists *now* in God’s sight before it is actualized in time (See Costa 2019 and 2022; Gili 2020)[^costa-2019][^costa-2022][^gili-2020].

Aquinas addresses the question of whether God’s certain knowledge of contingent realities destroys their contingency. One might think that if God infallibly knows that something is the case, then it cannot really be otherwise. Aquinas utilizes Boethius’s distinction between conditional or suppositional necessity (*necessitas ex suppositione*) and absolute or simple necessity to address this worry. (Aquinas also refers to this distinction between necessity of consequence vs. necessity of the consequent.) While it is true that a thing must be the case on the supposition that God knows it to be the case, the thing in question may nevertheless be the effect of causes that operate contingently (*Scriptum super libros Sententiarum* I.38.1.5, *Summa contra gentiles* I.67, *Summa theologiae* I, 14.13, *De veritate* 2.12). Aquinas uses an analogy to show that necessity of supposition does not imply absolute necessity: “Necessarily, if I see Socrates sitting, then he is sitting” because otherwise I would not be able to see him sitting. Yet, this does not imply that he cannot do otherwise. Similarly, “Necessarily, if God knows *x*, then *x*”, but this does not imply that *x* is itself absolutely necessary.

Scholars have noted a key difference between Aquinas’s approach to God’s eternal knowledge of contingents and Boethius’s approach. While Boethius does not emphasize God’s causal production of contingent realities, Aquinas is careful to note that God’s knowledge is the cause of the created realities which he knows (Shanley 1997)[^shanley-1997]. Aquinas states that God’s knowledge relates to all creatures similar to how a craftsman’s knowledge relates to the craft he produces (*Summa Theologiae* I.14.8). Thus, in Aquinas’s view, God is not a passive spectator taking in the eternal vision of temporal realities. Rather, all created temporal realities are eternally present to God because God eternally causes them.

Aquinas offers an account of how God’s infallible causation of created realities is compatible with their contingency. In his view, created effects are not caused by God alone, but by God and created secondary causes cooperating. He explains that created effects are necessary or contingent based on whether they are caused by a necessarily or a contingently operating created secondary cause. The divine will itself transcends the order of necessary and contingent causes and God ensures that created effects are necessarily or contingently caused by willing that they have certain types of created secondary causes (*In libros Perihermeneias* I.14; *Summa Theologiae* I.19.8). He writes that “Not only that which God wills happens, but it also happens in the mode in which God wills it, as necessarily or contingently … According to how it disposes some things to happen in this way or that way, it adapts causes for them in the mode which it disposes (De veritate 3.5).”

Some argue that Aquinas was in fact committed to “providential determinism” (Porro 2013: 121–127; Posti 2017: 82)[^porro-2013][^posti-2017], while others disagree (Torrijos-Castrillejo 2020; Silva 2022; Kopf 2023)[^kopf-2023][^silva-2022][^torrijos-castrillejo-2020]. Another difficulty that has been raised for Aquinas’s account regards how his view of God’s essential simplicity and immutability can be compatible with the view that God’s eternal choice could be otherwise. If God’s eternal choice cannot be otherwise, then it would seem that none of the effects that are produced by it can be otherwise. For responses to this worry, see Grant forthcoming.

### 4.2 John Duns Scotus

Scotus’s views on future contingents are embedded in his broader, innovative modal theory. In the medieval Augustinian tradition metaphysical possibilities were thought to be based on the divine essence and represented the ways in which it could be imitated by created things. According to Scotus, however, when God as an omniscient being knows all possibilities, he does not know them by turning first to his essence. Possibilities can be known in themselves; in fact, they would be what they are even if there were no God. Scotus calls the propositional formulations of pure possibilities logical possibilities (*possibile* *logicum*). These express things and states of affairs to which it is not repugnant to be. Possibilities as such have no kind of existence of their own, but are real in the sense that they form the precondition for everything that is or can be. Scotus takes it as an obvious fact that there are contingent states of affairs which could have not been at that very moment of time at which they are (*Ordinatio* I.2.1.1–2, 86). The idea of “synchronic contingency,” which holds that *not-p* remains possible at the very moment when a contingent *p* obtains, differed from the traditional view of the necessity of the present and played an important role in Scotus’s metaphysics. (On Scotus’s modal theory, see Vos *et al.* 1994; Knuuttila 1996; Normore 1996, 2003; Honnefelder 2005.)[^honnefelder-2005][^knuuttila-1996][^normore-1996][^normore-2003][^vos-1994] Scotus thought that human freedom requires “real potencies” for alternatives at the same instant of time (Dumont 1995)[^dumont-1995].

Regarding divine knowledge, Scotus rejects that God knows contingents on account of their presence in eternity (*Ordinatio* I.38–39.1–5, 10). In his view, it would denigrate the divine intellect to make God dependent upon created objects for knowledge. Furthermore, as mentioned above, Scotus thinks the “eternity solution” destroys the successive or flowing nature of temporal events by implying that all temporal events exist at once. Under the influence of Henry of Ghent, Scotus develops an alternative theory of God’s knowledge of contingents, though he eventually qualified his acceptance of it. (See Hoenen 1993: 177–179; Söder 1999: 177–183; Schabel 2000: 43–46.)[^hoenen-1993][^schabel-2000][^söder-1999]

A central element in Scotus’s theory of God’s knowledge of contingents is the complex (*complexio*). The complex is an object of divine thought and volition that is composed of a subject and predicate term. Presumably, the terms that compose complexes do not denote objects outside of God’s mind since nothing exists outside of God’s mind before an act of his will. Scotus claims that prior to the activity of God’s will, contingent complexes do not have truth-values. God knows these complexes as neither true nor false. These neutral propositions obtain their truth-value as a result of the divine will’s joining or dividing the terms that compose them. For example, when God joins “stars” and “even in number,” he causes there to be a connection between terms that are not naturally connected. Following the activity of God’s will, the proposition ‘The stars are even in number’ becomes true and the proposition ‘The stars are not even in number’ becomes false. Once the divine will makes its choice of which terms to join together, the divine intellect immediately knows as true or false these propositions that were previously known as neutral (*Reportatio* IA.38.1–2, 37; *Lectura* I.39.1–5, 62; *Ordinatio* I.38–39.1–5, 23–24). Scotus’s account of God’s knowledge as mediated by propositions serves as a way to reconcile God’s knowledge of every contingent fact with the successive nature of time. While contingent existents are not always actual, the terms that represent them are eternally present to God’s mind. By knowing the way in which these terms are composed by his will, God can know what is, will be, or was the case at any time in the world, although the realities that correspond to these propositions may or may not have actual existence.

Regarding the logical implications of Scotus’s theory, Beck 1998 argues that Scotus’s theory of the neutral proposition does not entail that he denied the principle of bi-valence since propositions are only neutral prior to creation and there is never a time in the actual world when a proposition lacks a truth-value. It is clear that Scotus does not think that the definite truth-values of propositions about future events entails determinism, since he believes that many future events are due to free causes which he conceives of as choosing between alternatives without being determined. (See Vos et al. 1994; Söder 1999; for some discussions of Scotus’s view of the free acts of the will, see Dumont 2001; Normore 2003: 141–145; Langston 2010.)[^dumont-2001][^langston-2010][^normore-2003][^söder-1999][^vos-1994] Scotus, however, does not attempt to reconcile God’s knowledge of contingents with human freedom.

Scotus believes that although the truth-values of future contingent propositions are determinate, they could be otherwise because God’s eternal choice could be otherwise (*Ordinatio* I.38.2, 426). Scotus believes that for a choice to be contingent, the agent must have a real capacity for opposite alternatives prior to choosing one of them. Scotus famously attempted to explain the contingency of God’s eternal choice by distinguishing between instants of nature, which represent natural, rather than temporal, priority and posteriority. Scotus distinguishes instants of nature in God’s mind to account for how God can encounter opposite alternatives prior to choosing one of them even though his choice occurs in an eternal instant that lacks all temporal duration.

These ideas are not found in Scotus’s early questions on Aristotle’s *De interpretatione* that mostly follow Boethius’s views on future contingents, adding some more elaborated analyses of the structure of future-tense propositions: they may signify that it is true now that something will be or signify that something will be (*Opus* 2.1.7–9). See the translations and commentary in Buckner and Zupko 2014. Thomas Wylton (ca. 1288–1304), an early critic of Scotus’s ideas, put forward some propositional distinctions similar to those in Scotus’s questions on *De interpretatione*. See the edition and comments in Schabel 2011b; Bornholdt 2017: 116–120.

Scotus’s theory of God’s knowledge of contingents was adopted by many Franciscan thinkers after him (Schabel 2007: 308)[^schabel-2007]. Yet, some prominent thinkers were critical of his views, including William of Ockham and Peter Auriol (Schabel 2007; Adams 1987: 1130–1137)[^adams-1987][^schabel-2007]. One serious objection raised against Scotus’s view was that God would not be able to know contingents about human free choices by knowing the determinations of his own will since God does not determine free human choices. The problem was especially difficult in the case of human sins since it was standard belief that although God permits sin, he does not actively will it. Scotus himself eventually recognized these difficulties with his position. In his *Reportatio Parisiensis* I.46.2, he states that there are many contingent propositions known by God that are not chosen by his will such as those about creaturely sins (see Frost 2010)[^frost-2010]. Scotus never explains how God knows such propositions.

### 4.3 William of Ockham

Ockham did not accept Scotus’s denial of the necessity of the present (Normore 1982: 371)[^normore-1982]. He thought that real capacities for opposites are diachronic (*Ordinatio* d. 38, 581). Yet, he holds that some aspects of the future, especially human choices, are contingent. Ockham thinks that, for both philosophical and theological reasons, future contingent propositions must be either true or false. As a matter of faith, he believes that God must know the future. On philosophical grounds, he argues that propositions cannot come to be true as time goes on. Rather, there must be as many truths as there are at one time as at another (On these arguments, see Wengert 1987). Like Scotus, Ockham believes that God knows the future by knowing propositions about the future, rather than knowing future realities in themselves in virtue of their presence in eternity. However, as mentioned above, Ockham rejects Scotus’s view that God knows the truth-values of contingent propositions by knowing the determinations of His own will. Ockham never offers an explanation of how God derives knowledge of the truth-values of future contingent propositions. He writes:

It must be held beyond question that God knows with certainty all future contingents—*i.e.* He knows with certainty which part of a contradiction is true and which false… It is difficult, however, to see how He knows this [with certainty] since one part [of the contradiction] is no more determined to truth than the other… (*Predestination, God’s Foreknowledge, Future Contingents*, q. 2, a. 4, trans. Adams and Kretzmann 1983: 48)[^adams-1983]

Though he had no theory of how God derives knowledge of the future, his discussion of the issue of how God’s knowledge of future contingents can be maintained without implying that the future is determined has been influential in contemporary discussions of God’s foreknowledge. While Scotus preferred to distinguish God’s eternal knowledge and choice from the temporal order sharply (*Lectura* I.39.1–2, n. 28, 85; *Ordinatio* I.40, n. 8), Ockham thought that they can be treated as temporally past. This led him to ask how God’s foreknowledge as something past and hence seemingly necessary is compatible with the contingency of future things. Ockham’s answer was that even though God’s foreknowledge is past, its content is future, and the past truth of future contingent propositions in God’s immutable but contingent knowledge does not fall under the necessity of the past (*Ordinatio* d. 38, 387–8; *Tractatus de praedestinatione* 515–516. For Ockham’s view, see Adams and Kretzmann 1983; Normore 1982)[^adams-1983][^normore-1982]. For example, although it was true in the past that ‘Socrates will sit at future time *t*,’ since this proposition is about the future, it is not the case that it is necessarily true as other truths about the past (*e.g.* ‘Socrates was sitting yesterday’). Though the view is today associated with Ockham, several thinkers around 1200 similarly accepted that past truths that are about the future do not fall under necessity of the past (Marenbon 2005, 105–106; Wciórka 2018 and 2020)[^marenbon-2005][^wciórka-2018][^wciórka-2020]. The view is also defended by Bonaventure (1221–1271) and criticized by Aquinas (Schabel 2000: 37 and 227)[^schabel-2000]. Many later thinkers likewise held that propositions about the future are not subject to necessity of the past. (See Robert Holkot (ca. 1290–1349), *Sent.* II.2 in *Seeing the Future Clearly*, 127, 145–146; Gregory of Rimini, *Lectura* *super I Sententiarum* I.38.2.3 (302–303); Pierre d’Ailly, *Quaestiones super libros Sententiarum* I.11.3S; Gaskin 1997; On Holkot’s development of the view see also the entry on Robert Holkot.) The “Ockhamist” view of divine foreknowledge continues to be defended today in philosophical theology (Zagzebski 1991: 66–97; See also the entry on foreknowledge and free will)[^zagzebski-1991]. While contemporary philosophers have developed the theory in various ways, Ockham himself did not articulate a metaphysical theory to support his claims regarding future contingent propositions (Wengert 1987).

Walter Chatton (ca. 1290–1343) was a contemporary critic of Ockham’s views about future contingents and divine knowledge. He also addressed the theories of Scotus, Wylton, and particularly Peter Auriol, his main source of inspiration, but mostly avoided formulating his own position. For an English translation with a commentary on his treatises on future contingents, see Bornholdt 2017, and 2019 [Other Internet Resources]; see also the entry on Walter Chatton.

### 4.4 Peter Auriol

While many later figures held that future contingents must have truth-values in order to secure God’s knowledge of the future, Peter Auriol is noteworthy for rejecting this view. Auriol equates immutability with necessity. Accordingly, he thinks that if future propositions were immutably true or false, they would be necessarily so. To save the contingency of the future and particularly free action, he rejects that the principle of bivalence applies to future propositions and develops a three-valued logic. Auriol claims that God is aware of the future in an “indistant” way that does not imply that future contingent propositions should be treated as true or false. Auriol’s indistant knowledge abstracts from temporality and simultaneity and is allegedly compatible with God’s necessary immutability and human free will (Schabel 2000: 67–123; see also the entry on Peter Auriol)[^schabel-2000].

Regarding related developments, English 14th-century authors often commented on the terminological distinctions between determinate and indeterminate truth and falsity. Walter Burley, Richard of Campsall (ca.1280-ca.1350), Arnold of Strelley (d. ca. 1347), and Robert Holkot preferred to call true propositions about future contingent things as indeterminately or contingently true. Some commentators have argued that speaking about this special mode of being true or false was an attempt to modify bivalence by formulating an idea of many-valued logic, see Normore 1982, 1993; Gelber 2004: 224–250.

Auriol’s position inspired much critical discussion until the early sixteenth century. Gregory of Rimini, under the influence of Francis of Marchia (born ca. 1290), developed a detailed defense of the universal applicability of the principle of bivalence in response to Auriol’s views see the entry on (Gregory of Rimini). Aspects of Auriol’s view came to be regarded as heretical when a Papal commission made a decision about the theses of Peter de Rivo, who defended a view similar to that of Auriol. Some of de Rivo’s statements were officially condemned in 1474 by Pope Sixtus IV (Baudry 1989 (ed.), *La querelle des futurs contingents (Louvain 1465–1475). Textes inédits*; Schabel 1995–96, 2000: 315–336, 2003a).

### 4.5 Francis of Marchia

Francis of Marchia was especially influential on subsequent medieval thinkers. Marchia argues that there must be some ground in reality for the truth of future contingent propositions and God’s knowledge of the future. Marchia locates that ground in the causes of future contingent realities. He argues that there must be some determination in the cause of a contingent reality toward producing its effect otherwise the cause would be indifferent between causing or not causing its effect and nothing would occur. Marchia distinguishes between two types of determination and two corresponding types of indetermination, *determinatio* and *indeterminatio de inesse* and *de possibili*. *Determinatio de inesse* applies to causes and effects and is the ground for foreknowledge. However, it is compatible with *indeterminatio de possibili* which preserves contingency. (On Marchia’s views see Marchia 1999, 2000; Schabel 2000; See also the entry on Francis of Marchia.)[^schabel-2000]

### 4.6 Thomas Bradwardine

Thomas Bradwardine’s (ca. 1290–1349) views on future contingents were also particularly influential. In his *De Causa Dei*, Bradwardine defends the view that the future, like all of temporal history, is necessary relative to God’s antecedent will. Bradwardine’s defense of the view that God predestines everything made him famous both in his own time and in the fifteenth and sixteenth century (Normore 1982: 374–376)[^normore-1982]. In his *De futuris contingentibus*, which is a question from his *Sentences Lectura*, Bradwardine complements the view that everything is necessary relative to God’s will by applying the distinction between God’s absolute and ordained power. Though contingents are determined by God’s ordained power, they could have been otherwise absolutely speaking since God could have chosen otherwise by his absolute power (Rossiter 2017)[^rossiter-2017]. Bradwardine’s views were criticized by his contemporaries Thomas Buckingham (ca. 1300 – ca. 1345) and Richard Kilvington (ca. 1302–1361) (See Genest 1992; Buckingham 1987; Kilvington 2022)[^genest-1992]. Bradwardine’s views were favorably received by others. Just four years after *De causa Dei* was published, Pierre Ceffons (1320–1380) used Bradwardine as a means to defend an extreme form of necessitarianism (See Schabel 2020)[^schabel-2020].

### 4.7 Luis de Molina and Middle Knowledge

Those who rejected that God determines the truth-values of future contingents about free human choices struggled to provide an explanation of how God knows the future acts of free causes. Luis de Molina (1535–1600) developed an influential solution to this problem. Molina claims that in addition to God’s necessary knowledge of all possibilities and his “free” knowledge of his own choices regarding which possibilities to create, God has a third type of knowledge called ‘middle knowledge’ (*scientia* *media*). Through his middle knowledge, God knows prior to creating the world what any possible creature would freely do in any possible situation (Freddoso 1988)[^freddoso-1988]. These counterfactuals about how creatures would freely act are referred to as “middle knowledge” because like God’s free knowledge these truths are contingent, yet like God’s necessary knowledge, these truths are not determined by God’s will. Molina’s ‘middle knowledge’ theory was actively debated in the sixteenth and seventeenth century and continues to be debated today in contemporary philosophy of religion. For an overview of the sixteenth century *De auxiliis* controversy regarding middle knowledge see Matava 2020. For contemporary defenses of middle knowledge, see Flint 1998 and Dekker 2000.

## 5. Later Medieval Discussions of Future Contingents and the Problem of Prophesy

During the decades following Ockham and Auriol the debate about future contingents focused increasingly on the issue of prophecy (Gelber 2004: 201)[^gelber-2004]. Thinkers struggled with how to reconcile the contingent truth of future propositions revealed by prophets with the inability of prophecies to go unfulfilled. It would seem that if prophesies cannot fail to be realized, then the truths revealed by the prophets must be necessarily true.

One solution that Ockham presents is the view that prophecies are actually hidden conditional statements. For example, the prophecy that ‘Peter will be saved’ has a hidden antecedent of ‘If he repents.’ Thus, even if it comes to pass in the future that Peter is not saved, the conditional proposition that was prophesied (*i.e.* ‘If Peter repents, then he will be saved’) nevertheless remains true. One critique this solution faces is that it makes prophecies trivial since the conditional proposition is true regardless of whether the conseuqnet that is uttered to by the propher does or does not come to pass. (For exposition and analysis of Ockham’s views on prophecy, see Soler 2024.)[^soler-2024]

Auriol held that propositions revealed by prophets were neither true nor false since no future contingent propositions have truth-values. Even if what a prophet states about the future does not come to pass, Auriol denies that this makes the prophet a false prophet. In his view, the criteria for being a true prophet is not the truth of one’s statements, but rather one’s intention to express divine understanding about the things prophesied about (Gelber 2004: 205–206; Schabel 2000: 67–132)[^gelber-2004][^schabel-2000]

Though Ockham and Auriol addressed the issue of prophecy, Richard FitzRalph’s (1299–1360) contributions marked a turning point in scholastic discussions of the topic. He is the first known figure to devote a separate question in his *Sentences* commentary exclusively to the issue the revelation of future contingents. Furthermore, he gave more attention to the question of God’s revelation of contingents than to the question of God’s foreknowledge of contingents which is where earlier figures typically addressed issues of the contingency of the future (Genest 1991: 240)[^genest-1991]. A sign of the growing complexity of the issue of prophesy and contingency in his time is that he devoted 20,000 words to the topic, which is more than double the length of Ockham’s discussion (Genest 2023: 276)[^genest-2023].

FitzRalph maintains that claims about the future, unless they are necessary, cannot be certain. Accordingly, revelation about future contingents, including free actions, cannot be certain. He does not deny that claims about future contingents may not come to pass. Rather, his focus is on dissociating the contingency of revelation from immorality (Leff 1963: 45–46)[^leff-1963]. He admits that God could mislead creatures by revealing what is not yet certain, but he emphasizes that this does not imply that God would do this since his actions are governed by his goodness (Leff 1963: 49–50)[^leff-1963]. He claims that even Christ could prophesy falsely, yet it would not count as lying or deception since this would require the intention to deceive which is impossible for Christ to have (Genest 2023: 289–290)[^genest-2023]. See also the entry on Richard FitzRalph.

By contrast, Robert Holcot argued that not only could God deceive, but scripture gives evidence that God has in fact deceived humans on many occasions, including when Abraham was made to believe that he should and would kill Isaac (Gelber 2004: 215–216; Incandela 1994)[^gelber-2004][^incandela-1994]. Holcot wanted to safeguard the contingency of the future not only to uphold human freedom, but also to uphold God’s freedom to do otherwise. Adam Wodeham (1295–1358) similarly argues that if a prophesy had to be fulfilled once it is uttered, then God would make himself impotent. He believes that God, by his absolute power, is able falsify past pronouncements made by prophets as long as the realities they regard are still future (In Sent. III, d.14, q.4, (Paris, 1512), fol 125ra; On Wodeham’s views see Kennedy 1990; Kennedy 1984 attributes similar views on prophecy to Thomas Buckingham). The view that Christ could err regarding future contingents was censured in 1347 when John of Mirecourt’s views were examined (Stegmüller 1933)[^stegmüller-1933].

For further texts and studies on late medieval controversies related to foreknowledge, freedom and future contingents, see Genest 1992; Schabel 2003a, 2003b, 2007, 2011a, 2011b; Schabel, Friedman, and Balcoyiannopoulou 2001; Rossini and Schabel 2005; Thakkar 2005, 2010.
