Retributive Justice

First published Wed Jun 18, 2014; substantive revision Tue Nov 24, 2026

Retributive justice is best understood as the form of justice committed to the following three principles:

  1. that those who culpably commit certain kinds of wrongful acts, paradigmatically serious crimes, morally deserve to suffer proportionate punishment;
  2. that it is morally right or intrinsically good—without reference to any further goods that might arise—for some (legitimate) punisher to impose the punishment they deserve; and
  3. that punishment must not be imposed in the absence of desert or exceed what would be proportional to the wrongful action.

While retributivism was for a long period of time disregarded as “a barbaric remnant of superstitious thought that more rational forms of society would sweep away” (Bennett 2014: 4446), recent work on retributivism has gained much support in the philosophy of punishment, making retributivist views among the dominant candidates for a positive justification of punishment. Still, critics continue to voice the charges of barbarism (Tadros 2011) and moral repugnancy (Scanlon 2008), and many features of retributivism—especially the notions of desert, proportionality, the normative status of suffering, and the ultimate justification for retribution—remain contested. Though theories of retributive justice have ancient and medieval roots, this entry focuses on contemporary conceptions of retributive justice.

1. Introducing Retributive Theory

1.1 On Punishment

Retributivism offers a justification of punishment. While the definition of punishment is itself a matter of controversy, punishment generally refers to the deliberate or intentional imposition of burdens or suffering on wrongdoers for an act or omission that the punisher believes to be (a culpable) wrong. (The entry on legal punishment discusses this concept in depth.) This definition includes both punishment as it is imposed by the state in the public sphere for the commission of crimes and punitive responses to wrongdoing in other spheres, including the private and the institutional. Still, the challenge of justifying state punishment is the most pressing, as the forms of punishment imposed by the state generally involve sanctions that are different in kind from and more onerous than those imposed outside the political sphere, paradigmatically involving the deprivation of basic human rights (to life, liberty, and property, among others).

Punishment is often analyzed in terms of two constituent parts: censure (or condemnation) and sanction (or hard treatment, that is, treatment that involves a hardship imposed on the person being punished, or at the very least the withdrawal of a benefit to which they would otherwise be entitled).

1.2 Basic Strengths and Weaknesses

1.2.1 Intuitive Appeal

Many share the intuition that those who commit wrongful acts, especially serious crimes, should be punished even if punishing them would produce no other good (see Darley & Robinson 2002). Consider, for example, being the sentencing judge for an offender who has been convicted of rape. How should you sentence him? You may have the intuition that he should not be “let off easy”. If so, you might think that this reflects the importance of incapacitating him, deterring him and others from future criminal acts, or publicly condemning the crime. But now suppose that he has since suffered an illness that has left him physically incapacitated so that he cannot rape or commit other violent crimes again. Moreover, he has enough money to support himself without resorting to criminal activities. You therefore have no need to take steps to prevent him from committing future crimes. Still, others might be emboldened if you don’t punish him. So suppose further that you could “sentence” him to spend his days on a tropical island of his choosing, where he can relax and enjoy himself, as long as he regularly reports to an island prison to be videoed in prison garb, footage of which is made publicly available, ensuring thereby that others harbor the false belief that he is suffering for his crime. If you believe that this could be done with no prospect of detection, should you choose such a sentence? Many share the intuition that this would be unjust. They believe that the wrongdoing should not go unpunished, even if punishment would achieve no other downstream good (see, e.g., Moore 1997: 98–101).

Not only is retributivism in this way intuitively appealing, the primary alternative, consequentialist theories of punishment that focus on deterrence, incapacitation and rehabilitation, seem to confront a deep problem: They have difficulty accommodating a core retributivist commitment, namely that these goals cannot justify imposing punishment on those who have done no wrong. They also have trouble explaining the discomfort most have with imposing disproportionately large punishments on those who have committed only minor wrongs.

Both of these sources of retributivism’s appeal have clear limits. The positive intuition can be challenged with the claim that it is merely the reflection of a morally dubious psychological propensity to feel an excess of what Nietzsche, in the Genealogy of Morals (1887), called ressentiment:

a witches’ brew [of] resentment, fear, anger, cowardice, hostility, aggression, cruelty, sadism, envy, jealousy, guilt, self-loathing, hypocrisy and self-deception. (Moore 1997: 120)

And the argument that retributivism better justifies punishment than consequentialist alternatives presupposes that punishment is justifiable (for criticism of this premise, see Golash 2005; Boonin 2008), and that there are no alternatives that are better than both (for three alternatives, see Quinn 1985; Tadros 2011; Lacey & Pickard 2015a).

To respond to these challenges, retributive justice must ultimately be justified in a larger moral context that shows that it is plausibly grounded in, or at least connected to, other, deeply held moral principles. Only in this way should its intuitive appeal be regarded, in reflective equilibrium, as morally sound. For a discussion of the prospects for deeper justification, see Section 3.

1.2.2 Primary Objections

While retributivism has intuitive appeal, it has also been subject to serious criticisms. Some of the major objections directed at retributivism include the following:

  • To deserve punishment one must have acted under conditions of freedom that are necessary for morally responsible action. But, many argue, this is an illusion because humans do not possess the sort of freedom required for moral responsibility of the kind envisioned by retributive punishment; and so the retributive idea fails. (See, e.g., G. Strawson 1986; Pereboom 2001; N. Levy 2011; Caruso 2021; for more on this challenge generally see the entry on Skepticism About Moral Responsibility.)
  • Even if people have the sort of freedom required to be responsible, the idea that they could “deserve” punishment rests on a morally objectionable claim, that human suffering could ever be intrinsically good. But this claim is “morally repugnant” (Scanlon 2013: 102), and so should be rejected.
  • Even if we accept that imposing suffering on the deserving can have morally positive valence, this is insufficient to warrant punishment, for establishing that something is good does not suffice to show that anyone, let alone the state, has the right to give another person what they deserve (see, e.g., Dolinko 1991; Golash 2005: 80).
  • Finally, even if people can deserve punishment for their wrongdoing, and even if there are people who have the right to punish those who deserve it, our epistemic access to the facts that make them responsible (and culpable for particular actions) is highly limited, undermining the possibility of finding punishments that are justifiable in individual cases (see, e.g., Smilansky 2000; Kelly 2018).

These objections only begin to unpack the challenges faced by any attempt to move from the intuitive idea that “culpable wrongdoing should not go unpunished” to a sustained, defensible account of retributive justice. Whether retributivism is vulnerable to these objections and how devastating they are depends on the specific retributive theory endorsed. See section 3 for Varieties of Retributivism. (For a discussion of objections to which many forms of retributivism are not vulnerable, see Dahan Katz 2023a; 2023c.)

2. What Retributivism is Not

Before laying out different varieties of retributive theory, it is important to clear some ground about what retributivism is not, since the term “retributivism” has been used so liberally that many ideas which are of little relevance to the justificatory theories to be discussed here are confused with it (see Cottingham 1979; Moore 1988).

2.1 Sublimated Vengeance

Critics often associate retributivism with vengeance, viewing the former as a laundered, institutionalized form of the latter. But the difference between the two has been marked as far back as ancient Greece. Aeschylus’s Oresteia traces the move from angry, personal vengeance and retaliation to legal justice, vindicating the genealogical connection between the two, while highlighting their deep differences.

Offering a philosophical analysis of the distinctions between retribution and vengeance, Robert Nozick draws five distinctions between them, including that revenge is personal whereas retribution is not, and that

[r]evenge involves a particular emotional tone, pleasure in the suffering of another, while retribution either need involve no emotional tone, or involves another one, namely, pleasure at justice being done. (Nozick 1981: 367)

These imply that even if no one wanted to take revenge on a wrongdoer, there could still be a retributive reason to punish her (Moore 1997: 89; for a skeptical take on these distinctions, see Fassin 2018: 36).

These distinctions do not imply that the desire for revenge plays no motivational role leading people to value retributive justice. But even if they share a motivational root, retributivism, as George Fletcher has written (2000: 417), “is not to be identified with vengeance or revenge, any more than love is to be identified with lust”. The question of their normative evaluation should thus be kept distinct.

2.2 Lex Talionis

Lex talionis is Latin for the law of retaliation. It connects to the original retributive notion of paying back a debt, and it specifies that the debt is to be paid back in kind. It is reflected in the Biblical injunction (which some Biblical scholars warn should be taken symbolically, not literally) to take “an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth” (Exodus 21: 23–25; Leviticus 24: 17–20). Kant also endorses this notion of punishment. Invoking the principle of equality for punishment, Kant writes: “whatever undeserved evil you inflict upon another within the people, that you inflict upon yourself” (1797 [1991: 141]). Thus, he who steals “deprives himself (by the principle of retribution) of security in any property…. [and if] he has committed murder he must die” (1797 [1991: 142]).

Lex talionis provides a principle of proportionality (for more on lex talionis as a measure of proportional punishment, see the supplementary document Challenges to the Notion of Retributive Proportionality). But while retributive justice includes a commitment to punishment that is proportional to the crime, it cannot be reduced to a measure of proportionality (Moore 1997: 88; Husak 2019); nor are most contemporary retributivists attracted to lex talionis even in the limited sense of a potential principle of proportionality. Still, it is worth noting that while lex talionis is often rejected on grounds of its severity and barbarity, historically the principle was introduced to bar the imposition of harm that exceeded the harm inflicted in the original wrong, and thus was in fact a mitigating principle of justice rather than one intended to ensure harsh punishment (Augustine, Answer to Faustus, Book 19, par. 25).

2.3 Negative Retributivism

What has been called “negative” (Mackie 1982), “minimalist” (Golding 1975), or “weak” (Hart 1968: ch. 1) retributivism is the view that guilt, desert, or something like it, acts as a side constraint on permissible punishment (Duff 2001: 11). Guilt or desert provides, on this view, only a necessary condition for meting out punishment. Theorists who take this position typically assume that the justification of punishment must appeal to consequentialist considerations. Moreover, it cannot appeal to the value of giving people the punishment they deserve, as desert provides neither a sufficient condition nor even a positive reason to punish (see also Mabbott 1939; Quinton 1954). Rather, it must appeal to goods such as deterrence, rehabilitation or incapacitation.

What are often referred to as “mixed” or “hybrid” theories of punishment, which appeal to different normative accounts to piece together a comprehensive theory of punishment, often include reference to a negative retributivist constraint on punishment. For example, H. L. A. Hart famously distinguished between two distinct questions for a normative theory of punishment: the question of punishment’s general justifying aim and the question of distribution, asserting that the prevention-based goods produced by punishment justify the institution of punishment, while “retributivist” restrictions, limiting punishment to the “guilty”, are relevant to its just distribution (Hart 1968, grounding these limits on fairness; see similarly Rawls 1955, for whom such constraints are generated by adopting a form of rule-consequentialism).

Negative retributivism runs into a conceptual dilemma. It is often confusingly framed as the view that it is impermissible to punish a wrongdoer more than they deserve (Hart 1968: 236–237; Duff 2001: 12; Lippke 2015: 58). If desert provides a limit to punishment, then it must be deserved up to that limit. But the notion of desert implies that there is a reason to give people what they deserve (Berman 2024: 46). Negative retributivism, however, rejects the contention that desert provides a reason to punish, thereby generating a tension within its own conceptual framework.

This contradiction can be avoided by reading the negative limit in terms of forfeiture rather than desert. But the shift to forfeiture has its own costs. It is not clear whether theories that do not appeal to desert can make sense of the proportionality restrictions that are central to retributivism (Duff 2001: 14–16). Indeed, proportionality limits without desert may be quite different from the limits implicit in the notion of deserved punishment (see Walen 2020; 2026: ch. 15.1). Thus, to avoid the contradiction, theorists should acknowledge that what they seek is not negative “retributivism”, but some other side-constrained form of consequentialism.

3. Varieties of Retributivism

Retributivism offers a positive justification of a practice that includes the intentional censuring and sanctioning of others in response to their wrongdoing. On what grounds can such practice be morally justified?

3.1 Traditional Retributive Ideas

Traditional versions of retribution appeal to a variety of ideas, often religiously grounded, to articulate a normative justification of punishment. Beyond the above-mentioned idea of “payback” (see section 2.2), the idea of an all-knowing Deity who perfectly rewards the righteous and punishes the vicious has a major place in the history of Judeo-Christian retributivist ideas. Other prominent retributivist ideas relate to notions of balance or atonement, where wrongdoing, or sin, is seen as creating a moral imbalance, as leaving a “stain”, or as “contaminating”, in ways that must be corrected, cleaned, or atoned for, often to restore the moral balance or to realign one with God or with the Good. (These ideas need not rely on an omnipotent deity, and might rather be linked to ideas of cosmic order or balance, as in the Greek thought of Heraclitus (Kahn 1981: 207) or Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics, Book V).)

Kant and Hegel are perhaps the most prominent philosophers of the modern era associated with traditional conceptions of retributive justice (though some commentators reject the interpretation of Kant as a (pure) retributivist; see, e.g., Byrd 1989; Hill 1999; Brooks 2003). Kant is perhaps best known for the contentions that punishment can only be imposed on grounds of the commission of a crime (Kant 1797 [1991: 140]), and that when one wills a “punishable” act penal justice requires the offender’s punishment, in accordance with the “principle of equality”, and essentially in proportion to their “inner viciousness” (142). Hegel, for his part, defends punishment as a restoration of right. He proposes that punishment is “necessarily connected” to crime, “as the will which is null and void in itself, accordingly contains within itself its own nullification, and this appears in the form of punishment” (Hegel 1821: §101). Per Hegel’s view, punishment both exposes that crime cannot negate right, and is a reaffirmation of the right (and of the system of law), and so is an act of justice. Distinctively, for Hegel, punishment is also understood to be a right of the offender (Hegel 1821: §100).

These ideas have had a powerful impact on contemporary retributivism, though the systems of thought associated with these retributivist views have been met with sufficient resistance to have prompted major shifts in conceptions of the normative basis of a defensible retributivist theory of punishment. For example, fair-play theories (section 3.3), might be seen as attempts to reinterpret the idea of moral balance in terms of the fair balance of burdens of self-restraint restored through punishment, while Hamptonian retributivism (section 3.4.1) attempts to offer a contemporary conception of annulment by proposing that punishment counters or negates the message of victim-inferiority conveyed in crime.

3.2 Intrinsic Goodness Retributivism

While early intimations of retributive revival predate the 1970s (see A. C. Ewing 1929; Mabbott 1939; Mundle 1954), the reemergence of retributivist theories of punishment can be attributed to fair-play versions of retributivism (Morris 1968; Murphy 1973) and the defense of intrinsic-goodness retributivism defended by Michael Moore (1988; 1997). Moore’s conception of retribution has arguably taken hold as “the” retributive view (especially among critics), and this section therefore begins by explicating his conception of “what retributivism is”.

On Moore’s view, “what is distinctively retributivist” “is the view that the guilty receiving their just deserts is an intrinsic good” (Moore 1993: 19) and “that the moral desert of an offender is a sufficient reason to punish him” (Moore 1997: 88).

Retributivism is a very straightforward theory of punishment: Moral culpability (“desert”) is … both a sufficient as well as a necessary condition of liability to punitive sanctions… For a retributivist, the moral culpability of an offender also gives society the duty to punish. Retributivism, in other words, is truly a theory of justice such that, if it is true, we have an obligation to set up institutions so that retribution is achieved. (Moore 1988: 181–182)

Moore makes an important contribution in emphasizing that desert provides what he calls a sufficient reason both to set up institutions of punishment and to perform individual acts of punishment. “As a theory of a kind of justice, it obligates us to seek retribution through the punishment of the guilty” (Moore 1997: 154).

On the standard reading, per Moore’s view, these deontic implications are grounded in an axiological claim: that the punishment of the deserving is intrinsically good. Mitchell Berman (2011) points out that there are really two ways to read the axiological claim: that punishment is itself intrinsically good (Moore 1997: 159) or, as he reads Moore’s claim, that it is intrinsically good that a wrongdoer suffer on account of, and in proportion to, their culpable wrongdoing, making punishment itself instrumentally good for achieving this end. On an alternative reading, Moore adopts both the axiological claim and the deontic claims, and these are parallel manifestations of his retributivism, rather than the latter being grounded in the former. Punishing the deserving is simply “an intrinsic value that we each categorically are enjoined to realize in our actions” (Moore 1997: 163).

Either way one reads Moore, it is important to recognize that his conception of the duty to punish is not absolute. “[R]etributivists are not monomaniacal about the achieving of retributive justice”, as he puts it (Moore 1997: 186). He acknowledges that “the small good of punishing minor wrongs could be outweighed by other goods achieved by not punishing such minor wrongs” (Moore 2014: 200); that the law may not punish the exercise of constitutionally protected liberties; and that the intrinsic value of punishing the guilty has to be weighed against the costs of punishment. He accepts that the instrumental benefits of punishment can help offset those costs (Moore 2009: 43–44), and thus can be relevant to the overall justification of punishment. His point is that these instrumental benefits do not help to justify acts of punishment as such, and can only help defray the costs. Only the good of giving offenders their just deserts can provide punishment’s justification qua punishment.

Methodologically, Moore offers a distinct way of justifying his proposed view, offering a “coherentist approach” rather than arguing for retributivism by appealing to deeper principles of justice. He aims to show that retributivism “best accounts for those of our more particular judgments that we also believe to be true” (Moore 1988: 103). His justificatory strategy involves defending two claims: first, most people intuitively think that it is important to punish wrongdoers with proportional hard treatment, even if no other good would thereby be brought about. Second, there is no reason to doubt that these intuitions are reliable.

It seems clear that the vast majority of people share the retributive intuition that makes up the first prong of Moore’s argument (Moore 1997: 101). Consider again the example of the offender convicted of rape mentioned in section 1.2.1. The intuition is widely shared that he should be punished even if doing so is expected to produce no consequentialist good distinct from him getting the punishment he deserves. The weakness of this strategy is in prong two. One might suspect that retributive intuitions are merely the reflection of emotions, such as a thirst for vengeance, that are morally dubious. They may be deeply grounded in our species as part of our evolutionary history, but that fact by itself might be thought insufficient to consider them morally reliable—compare other deeply ingrained emotional impulses, such as tribalism, that are morally problematic (Bloom 2013).

Moreover, there are positive reasons to worry that the emotional response grounding retributive intuitions is a morally misplaced reaction. Foremost among these is the argument that we do not really have free will (see section 4.3). If that is correct, then blame and punishment might both be misplaced responses to wrongdoing. Rather, sympathy for wrongdoers as products of their biology and environment might seem to call for pity and compassion and, where necessary, incapacitation, but not blame or punishment (for a contrary view, see K. Levy 2014).

Moore (1997: 145) has an interesting response to objections to the second prong of his argument. He turns to the first-person point of view. He imagines that he has committed some horrible violent crime, and then says that he hopes his response “would be that I would feel guilty unto death”. As a result, he hopes that he would welcome punishment for having committed such a crime. He then turns the table and says that one should resist the “elitist and condescending” temptation to withhold that judgment from others (1997: 148).

This is a rhetorically powerful move, but it is nonetheless open to at least four objections, all of which have been discussed in the literature. First, it presupposes that one can infer the propriety of the third-person reaction of blame and punishment from the first-person reaction of guilt and self-punishment. But there are things a person should do to herself that others should not feel equally free to do to her (Duff 2007: 383; Zaibert 2018: 94–95). Second, it may reflect only the imagination of a person who has committed no such serious crimes, rather than the insight of a person who knows what it is like to have committed a serious crime and then tried to come to terms with herself. Third, it equates the propriety of feeling guilt with the propriety of adding punishment to that feeling. But one might ask: why is the feeling of guilt not enough? (See Husak 2016: 59–60; see also section 3.7.) Fourth, one can question whether even the reaction of feeling guilt is a morally sound one. Might it not be a sort of sickness, as Nietzsche (1887 [2006: 60]) put it, “bad conscience, … the will to self-violation”?

Methodology aside, the conception of retributivism as including if not resting on the axiological claim that the suffering of the deserving is intrinsically good, has been the focal point for many a friend and foe of retributivism. In fact, in many contemporary anti-retributivist writings, it is this conception of retributivism that is being criticized rather than retributivism writ large (see Dahan Katz 2023a). Consider Victor Tadros’s objection to retributivism as “barbaric” (Tadros 2011: 63) or T. M. Scanlon’s view of retributivism as “morally repugnant” (Scanlon 2013: 102). Critics appear to object to the claim that human suffering could ever be “intrinsically good”, though this claim is not common to all retributivist views. Indeed, since Moore’s articulation of retributivism, many of his claims have come under scrutiny by other retributivists. Moreover, while the claim that deserved suffering is intrinsically good is endorsed by many contemporary retributivists (Alexander and Ferzan 2018; Zaibert 2018), others offer thoroughly deontic conceptions of retributivsm whereby imposing deserved punishment is (pro tanto) morally required, without endorsing the axiological claim that deserved suffering is intrinsically good (see Markel 2011; Dahan Katz 2021; see also section 4.2.2).

3.3 Fair Play Theories

While Moorean retributivism aims to vindicate retributive intuitions, fair-play theories point to an underlying principle of punishment’s justification: fair play. As developed by Herbert Morris (1968) and soon after taken up by Jeffrie Murphy (1973), this framework justifies punishment as responding to crime by restoring a fair balance of benefits and burdens in a system of social cooperation.

The argument starts with the thought that it is to our mutual benefit to live in society, and that to be in society, we have to accept certain limits on our behavior. It then continues:

If a person fails to exercise self-restraint … he renounces a burden which others have voluntarily assumed and thus gains an advantage which others, who have restrained themselves, do not possess. (Morris 1968: 33)

It concludes with the thought that to restore fairness, this advantage should be erased by “exacting the debt” (1968: 34). This is done with hard treatment that effectively strips away the illegitimate advantage gained by wrongdoing.

Though influential, this view has been the target of powerful and persistent objections. First, it is unclear that offenders always or even typically have advantages that others have forsaken. Suppose someone murders another in a moment of anger, triggered by a minor offense. Does he get the “advantage” of getting to engage in violence? What if most people feel they can express their anger sufficiently in such situations by expressing it in words, or have no interest in actually lashing out violently or in other non-restrained ways? If people do not, at least not upon reflection, wish to be violent, then the murderer may not be renouncing a mutually-borne burden or have taken unfair advantage of others (see, e.g., Duff 2008: 278).

Even if there is some sense in which the offender gains an advantage over others, such as the advantage of being free to use violence, what would then be the proper measure of bringing the offender back in line? To see the problem, compare how “far ahead” such a murderer is likely to get to how “far ahead” someone might get by economic fraud. Fraud may produce a much greater advantage, but we normally think that violence is the greater crime and so warrants greater punishment (Burgh 1982). (Davis 1993 challenges this framing of the advantage gained, suggesting the right test is the value a crime would find at an auction of licenses to commit crimes; Shafer-Landau (1996: 303) rejects this solution as equally implausible.)

More problematically yet, it seems to be fundamentally missing the point to say that what is wrong about, for example, murder or rape is that the offender is free riding. What is wrong is that the offender is violating life and sexual autonomy (Hampton 1988: 116–117; Braithwaite & Pettit 1992: 158–159; Murphy 2007: 13–14).

An alternative interpretation of Morris’s idea is that the relevant benefit is the opportunity to live in a relatively secure state, and the wrong is not the gaining of an extra benefit but the failure to accept the burdens that, collectively, make that benefit possible. Punishment then removes the benefit that wrongdoers cannot fairly lay claim to, having shirked the burden that was their duty to carry (see Westen 2016). This interpretation avoids the first of the problems outlined above. But it still has difficulty accounting for the thought that a crime such as murder is not fundamentally about free riding. Moreover, it has difficulty accounting for proportional punishment in a plausible way. Presumably, the measure of a proportional punishment would be something like the following: the greater the shirking of one’s duty to accept the burdens of self-restraint, the larger one’s punishment should be. But how do we measure the degree of shirking? By the harm one causes or risks causing, by the benefit one receives, or by the degree to which respecting the burden shirked would have been burdensome? Only the first (if properly complemented by mens rea considerations) corresponds with a standard retributive notion of punishment, but this alternative reading seems to point to one of the latter two meanings as the measure of unjust gain (see also Dagger 2008, developing proportionality as an internal feature of fairness).

Finally, there is the worry that fair-play views can only justify punishment in states that meet background conditions of a fair distribution of burdens and benefits in society; but given the social injustices that pervade real-world states, punishment will not in fact be justified. Indeed, one of the first proponents of fair-play retributivism, Jeffrie Murphy, offered a Marxist critique of society, concluding that absent radical reform in social relations actual states have no right to punish (Murphy 1973; but see Duus-Otterström 2017).

(For further attempts to develop a fair-play view, see Dimock 1997; Markel 2011; Duus-Otterström 2017; and Dagger 2018.)

3.4 Expressive and Communicative Views

The past few decades have seen a growth of support in the symbolic or expressive side of interpersonal and legal practices, and punishment theory has been no exception. As a descriptive matter, Joel Feinberg’s “The Expressive Function of Punishment” (1965) placed the centrality of expressed condemnation at the forefront of analyses of punishment, distinguishing it from other, non-punitive harm-inflicting practices. But his commentary prompted many to explore the expressive capacities of punishment as a source of its normative justification, giving rise to a renewal of interest in expressivist justifications of punishment (often referred to as “denunciation” in earlier texts). On such views, the message conveyed through punishment has the capacity (at least in part) to justify the practice (see, e.g., Primoratz 1989; Hampton 1988; Narayan 1993; Bennett 2008; Wringe 2016). A later outgrowth of expressivist theories, communicative theories, allege that rather than one-way expression, the two-way dialogue between the punisher and the wrongdoer is central to the justification of punishment. Expressive and communicativist views might be fully instrumentalist or they might take non-instrumentalist forms, some rejecting retributive principles and others merging with them to provide expressivist strains of retributivism. The following sections review the most prominent strains associated with retributivism.

3.4.1 Expressive Retributivism: Jean Hampton on Vindicating Victims

Jean Hampton tries to improve upon the unfair advantage theory by focusing on the idea that what wrongdoers—at least those who attack victims—do is an affront ,not just to the community as a whole, but to their victims in particular. Her view is that punishment must somehow annul the affront to the victims. As she puts it:

If I have value equal to that of my assailant, then that must be made manifest after I have been victimized. By victimizing me, the wrongdoer has declared himself elevated with respect to me, acting as a superior who is permitted to use me for his purposes. A false moral claim has been made… The retributivist demands that the false claim be corrected. The lord must be humbled to show that he isn’t the lord of the victim. … [R]etributive punishment is the defeat of the wrongdoer at the hands of the victim (either directly or indirectly through an agent of the victim’s, e.g., the state) that symbolizes the correct relative value of wrongdoer and victim. (Hampton 1988: 125–126)

Punishment is thus justified as an emphatic repudiation of the message of the victim’s lesser worth, and a reaffirmation of the victim’s equal moral worth.

This theory too raises serious concerns. First, why think that a wrongful act seriously challenges the equal moral standing of anyone (Dolinko 1991: 551)? It may imply that wrongdoers think of themselves as above either the law in general or their victim in particular. But if so, they are simply mistaken. Unless there is a danger that others will believe they are right, it is not clear why there is a pressing need to correct them.

Second, even if such a message is offensive in a way that calls for correction, why isn’t the solution simply to reaffirm the moral status of the victim, to censure the wrongdoer, and perhaps to require the wrongdoer to make compensation (Matravers 2000: 78)? One might answer that “actions speak louder than words”. Perhaps some baseline punishment may then be necessary to show that we really mean it when we say that he was mistaken (see also Primoratz 1989: 200; Kleinig 1991: 417–418). But why would we ever need to go beyond that baseline? (See further Hanna 2008.) Why should the punishment be in proportion with the gravity of the wrong, to show that “we mean it”?

Third, the message of equality through turning the tables seems peculiar. If the victim, with the help of others, gets to take her turn being lord, it is not clear how that sends the message of equality, rather than simply the message that this particular wrongdoer lost in the competition to be “lord” (Dolinko 1991: 553).

Fourth, Hampton seems to have fallen into a trap that also was a problem for Morris, namely substituting one wrong for another. The wrong of being raped is not the message that the rapist sends; it is the rape (Dolinko 1991: 551–552).

(For some of Hampton’s replies to her critics, see Hampton 1992.)

3.4.2 Communicative Theories

Antony Duff (2001; 2011; 2024) offers a communicative theory according to which punishment is necessary to communicate censure for wrongdoing. As Duff puts it, “a communicative account of punishment … justifies penal hard treatment as part of the morally communicative process itself” (Duff 2001: 88). On Duff’s view, punishment communicates “the censure that the crime deserves. It is in that sense retributive: it is justified as a response to the wrong for which it is imposed and must be appropriate in its character and severity to that wrong. But such communication is not purely backward-looking” (Duff 2012: 78). More specifically, according to Duff:

Punishment … aims at the goals of repentance, reform, and reconciliation. These goals are to be pursued by a communicative process of imposing penitential burdens on offenders. (Duff 2001: 107).

(In his most recent discussion, he also embraces the goal of deterrence, writing that “We must recognize that penal communication will always have a deterrent aspect, but that aspect can still be no more than a secondary feature” (Duff 2024: 105).)

Penal hard treatment can help reinforce censure by ensuring that offenders take the duty of repentence seriously, that they engage in reform efforts, and that they engage in behaviors that formally count as a public apology.

One might worry that condemning wrongdoers is antithetical to the sort of community that Duff strives to uphold. As Nicola Lacey and Hanna Pickard (2015a) put it, stigmatizing offenders with condemnation alienates them from society (and they are likely alienated already) and undermines their potential to see themselves as eventually redeemed. To this worry, Duff may be able to respond that the form of condemnation he has in mind is nothing more than treating wrongdoers as responsible for their criminal acts.

But this then leads to a second worry, namely that Duff’s theory cannot account for hard treatment. As Duff raises the issue:

Censure can … be communicated by “hard treatment” punishments … by imprisonment, by compulsory community service, by fines and the like, which are burdensome independently of their censorial meaning…: but why should we choose such methods of communication, rather than methods that do not involve hard treatment? (Duff 2013: §6)

One answer he gives is that:

[P]enal hard treatment [is] an essential aspect of the enterprise of moral communication itself. Punishment, on this view, should aim not merely to communicate censure to the offender, but to persuade the offender to recognize and repent the wrong he has done, … and to make apologetic reparation to those whom he wronged. [The] hard treatment aspects [of his punishment], the burden it imposes on him, should serve both to assist the process of repentance and reform, by focusing his attention on his crime and its implications, and as a way of making the apologetic reparation that he owes. (Duff 2013: §6)

A third worry, as Duff is well aware, is that it is not clear why “hard treatment [is] a necessary aspect of a communicative enterprise” (2013, emphasis added). Indeed, Lacey and Pickard (2015a) suggest that hard treatment actually interferes with the communicative enterprise.

Finally, it is not clear that anything resembling proportional punishment can be justified by Duff’s communicative account. That is, even if it does justify some hard treatment, it is unclear why the amount of hard treatment should vary as the wrong becomes more grave or as it is performed more culpably (see Walen 2026, ch. 7.1.2).

These worries might be addressed by giving up on the centrality of expression and communication, since messages can be conveyed without hard treatment, and turning instead to the normative requirement to react to wrongdoing in ways that are constitutive rather than expressive of morally correct attitudes (see Dahan Katz 2021; and section 3.5), though this move comes with its own challenges.

3.5 Dissociative Retributivism

Dissociative retributivism defends the view that the duty to punish is justified by secondary duties we have to respond and react to primary forms of wrongdoing in ways that constitute adequate dissociation from culpable wrongdoing, or more precisely, from the devaluative conception of others inherent in relevant culpable wrongs (Dahan Katz 2021).

Christopher Bennett defends an expressivist dissociative account. Bennett argues that dissociation is what is required to properly “mark” or “honor” wrongdoing, offering a theory of dissociative punishment as expressive action (Bennett 2008). On Bennett’s view, we owe it to wrongdoers to condemn their actions and, in the state context, to impose penitential amends in order to appropriately symbolize the condemnation necessary for dissociation. On Bennett’s view, punishment is an institutionalized “apology ritual” that symbolically enacts the burdens that dissociate the punisher from the wrong and impose a ritual of self-dissociation, which can and should be expected from the wrongdoer. Interestingly, Bennett takes his conception of dissociation to obviate the need to rely on desert, and emphatically rejects desert as the basis of the justification of punishment, though he takes his view to be retributivist (Bennett 2024).

Peter Chau (2017) offers a two-fold critique of Bennett’s expressive account: first, for Bennett’s argument to succeed, it must be the case that punishment is the only symbolically adequate response to wrongdoing; but less severe or non-punitive responses can also “mark” wrongdoing. Second, even if punishment were symbolically necessary, this is insufficient to justify its permissibility; further argument is necessary to explain why symbolic considerations can justify the imposition of burdens that would otherwise violate individual rights.

Leora Dahan Katz has proposed a dissociative theory according to which what wrongdoers deserve is precisely to be treated in ways that allow others who are in relevant relations with them to adequately dissociate from wrongdoers’ culpable wrongdoing. On her view, where an agent is in an appropriate relationship with the wrongdoer, to fail to respond negatively to what the wrongdoer has done is to commit a secondary wrong, one which fails with respect to the very moral grounds violated by the wrongdoer’s primary wrong. She argues:

The responsive action of continuing the relations as before without rejecting the devaluative wrong constitutes an act of abiding by or standing acquiescent in the face of the culpable wrong (absent justifying conditions). That is, the pre-existing relations render the relevant wrongdoings of others morally charged situations that trigger the non-relational duty we have to respect the moral worth of others, and call for engagement with the wrongdoer to reject the devaluative wrong if one is not, by virtue of the relations, to acquiesce. Much like standing by as someone drowns has moral significance and is constitutive of inadequately regarding others (absent justification or excuse), standing by in the face of relevant wrongdoing and continuing the relations without pause or modification constitutes a structurally similar (though lesser) failure. (Dahan Katz 2021: 594)

While her view, like expressivist and communicativist theories, calls for appropriate responses to wrongdoing, Dahan Katz explicitly rejects their expressivist commitments as ‘missing the main event’. To illustrate this, she invites us to consider a case in which a responding agent jumps in to save the drowning victim. On Dahan Katz’s analysis:

Surely such action has expressive value. But this is not why we rescue, nor does it capture what is important about rescuing. What’s important is not that we express that human life has value, or that we send a message to this effect, but that we respect human life through the lifesaving act—that we perform that action that is consistent with respecting human life (which, if we fail to do, constitutes a wrongful omission). Similarly, while appropriate punishment often does and should make expressive statements, it is first and foremost a performative act which effects the dissociation required if one is to act consistent with the moral worth of persons. It is this performative function, rather than the expressive function, that justifies punishment. (Dahan Katz 2021: 604–605)

On this view, while dissociative responses will naturally have expressive value, expression is not what justifies punishment. Rather, substantive compliance with moral norms is what can require and thus justify punishment.

One concern that the view raises is why, even if dissociation is called for, this should require punishment, and what the parameters are for determining when and whether harsh punishments are in order. It is clear that on Dahan Katz’s view the question of what is required for dissociation is context-sensitive, depending on traditional determinants of desert such as the gravity of the wrong and culpability of the wrongdoer, but also on facts about the relations between the parties to punishment and the forms of response available within the relations. Dahan Katz argues that in some instances, negative affective responses or censure may be sufficient, while in others meaningful dissociation may require the imposition of further burdens, deprivations, suffering, or hardships. On her view, censure and sanction are simply options along this spectrum, while there is no ‘natural’ form dissociation ought to take. Dahan Katz emphasizes that not all modes of dissociation will be justified: punishers and punishment practices will be subject to independent deontic constraints, as well as a further ‘duty to mitigate retributive practices’ (2021: 607–609). Yet where imposing harmful and even very significant burdens on wrongdoers meets these constraints and is necessary to adequately dissociate from the wrongdoer’s devaluative action, punishment will be justifiably imposed, and pro tanto required.

This, however, gives rise to an important challenge: to articulate under what conditions such harmful burdens might be called for. In the context of criminal punishment in particular, it requires specifying the relational features that render the deprivation of rights an appropriate form for dissociation to take, an aspect of punishment’s justification left underspecified by the account.

3.6 Blame-Based Theories

Recent decades have seen an explosion of interest in the norms and practices of blame. (See entry on blame.) And it makes sense to think about the practice of criminal punishment through this lens since criminal law, as Andrew Simester notes, is “a blaming institution” (2021: 493).

Blame is a responsive practice that, when expressed, takes the form of a negative, disapproving response, directed at an agent, for their wrongdoing or other failure (e.g. character flaw, poor quality of will, or defective exercise of agency). In modern, liberal jurisdictions, the criminal law at least purports to mete out blame, in the form of censure, only for criminal acts or omissions, not character flaws or thought crimes (but see Mendlow 2020). It can be expressed without (additional) hard treatment, but it also easily pairs with additional hard treatment in the form of punishment.

Attention to blame rose significantly following the publication of P. F. Strawson’s seminal “Freedom and Resentment” (1962), in which he highlighted the significance of our reactive attitudes—that is, of our emotion-laden responses such as resentment and indignation, (forms of blame), as well as gratitude, hurt feelings and other attitudes, that involve implicit claims about how people may and may not treat each other—within the content of interpersonal interactions and practices of holding one another responsible. Strawson takes these to be “internally” justifiable as interpersonal practices even if traditional “external” requirements for blame and punishment (such as free will) are not met, while highlighting the significance of the emotive side of our responsibility practices.

Prior to Strawson, emotions had generally been appealed to as a basis for debunking retributive theory, with the claim that it is grounded in irrational, indefensible emotions (Nietzsche 1887). Strawson’s invitation to rethink the role and normative force of the moral emotions, especially in the context of practices of holding one another responsible, generated new-found interest in the normative defensibility of the moral emotions associated with retribution, blame and punishment, and the action tendencies associated with such attitudes. Many retributive theorists have been substantially influenced by Strawsonian thought about reactive attitudes and their potential for illuminating our punitive accountability practices (see, e.g., Murphy and Hampton 1988; Bennett 2002).

Notably, while Strawson himself seems to have understood punishment to be on a continuum with blaming reactions, along the spectrum of negative reactions which have deep import for our lives and which are broadly retributively justified, much of the Strawsonian blame literature has tried to draw a sharp line between practices of blame and their punitive counterpart (see, e.g., Scanlon 2008; but see Dahan Katz 2023b for a defense of the inherent punitivity of blame).

Christopher Bennett offers a clear example of a Strawsonian retributivist account (Bennett 2008). On his view, reactive attitudes like resentment, indignation, guilt and apology are responses to failures to live up to the standards of regard and respect that govern relationships of mutual accountability, while punishment serves as an institutionalized extension of those same practices (see section 3.5).

Jeffrie Murphy (1988) offers a fairness-based justification of punishment drawing on the claim that resentment is essentially connected to self-respect (see section 3.3). He argues that resentment functions as an emotional protest against conduct that expresses contempt for one’s standing as a moral equal. Because self-respect is partly constituted through social relations, wrongdoing is experienced not merely as the infliction of harm but as a challenge to one’s moral status within a community of persons. Retributive emotions therefore have a normative significance that goes beyond personal vengeance. In this respect, Murphy’s 1988 account echoes Strawson’s treatment of resentment as a constitutive feature of the participant stance, according to which holding others responsible is inseparable from the expectations of regard and respect that structure ordinary interpersonal relationships.

3.7 Guilt-Based Theories

Another line of thought focuses on guilt, the first-person counterpart of blame. One way of taking guilt to be significant is to argue that it is a form of suffering that can be more easily defended than the unqualified thought that culpable wrongdoers deserve to suffer. Some have therefore taken the goodness or fittingness of the feeling of guilt to ground reason for others to react in ways intended to induce that feeling (Clarke 2013; Carlsson 2022; but see Nelkin 2019 who argues against non-conditional reasons to induce the feeling of guilt in others). Michael McKenna pursues this idea in the context of punishment, arguing for what he terms a “modest” form of retributivism whereby what a culpable wrongdoer deserves is to be subject to “conditions that one who is guilty would take to be adequate expressions of their having internalized guilt” (2024: 96). On his view, this grounds a justification of a qualified system of punishment which aims for criminals “to experience a fitting kind and degree of guilt for their wrongdoing” (2024: 89; see also Bennett 2002; Duff 2003 (on the painful feelings of guilt and remorse and the relation between these feelings and justified punishment)).

Alec Walen adopts a different kind of guilt-based view of punishment, according to which the core purpose of punishment is to collect a culpable wrongdoer’s guilt-debt. He rejects the idea that censure and punishment should be aimed at trying to make people feel guilt. He argues that they are not well-designed to that end; nor could their efficacy be substantially improved while also respecting people’s autonomy (Walen 2026: 125). Moreover, on his view, the essence of punishment is not inflicting suffering. Rather, it is reinforcing censure in the way that Duff discusses (see section 3.4.2) and collecting what can be collected of the guilt-debt of culpable wrongdoers while also respecting their dignity.

Guilt-debt can be collected by forcing culpable wrongdoers to engage in rehabilitative treatment aimed at addressing some of the root causes of their wrongful behavior. Reform can also be achieved, as Duff (2001) points out, by providing an occasion for sustained reflection on why they behaved wrongly. Hard treatment can also help ensure that the wrongdoers do not compound their offense by manifesting contentment about the choice to engage in wrongdoing. But the core of the collection of guilt-debt is achieved by extracting material compensation for the community (in the form of fines or public service) or by forcing convicted wrongdoers to provide for general deterrence of lawbreaking by enduring hard treatment. Proportionality—primarily in an ordinal sense, but also in a loose cardinal sense (Walen 2026: ch. 8)—is to be achieved by matching the magnitude of culpable wrongdoing with the magnitude of the debt extracted through material amends or the imposition of deterring forms of hard treatment.

Walen argues that punishment as guilt-debt collection is deserved because the state has a pro tanto duty to censure and punish the culpable commission of serious public wrongs. This duty is grounded in the state having, on the one hand, a special interest in upholding just laws that protect rights that the public has an interest in protecting or that serve other important public interests, and, on the other hand, a commitment to promoting reconciliation between culpable wrongdoers and the political community by collecting what can be collected of the former’s guilt-debt (Walen 2026: ch. 3.7).

There are at least two objections one might raise to Walen’s account. If one thinks that what it means to deserve punishment is to deserve suffering or that one’s life go less well, then his view will seem to miss the point. But his view is far from unique in rejecting the traditional focus on inducing suffering (see sections 3.2 and 4.2.1). It might also be objected that it is odd to claim that deterrence—traditionally a central feature of utilitarian theories of punishment—is core to a retributive theory of punishment. But though this is an unusual interpretation of retributive justice in contemporary theory, it harkens back to the traditional retributive idea of “payback” (see sections 2.2 and 3.1), i.e., of wrongdoers paying their debt to society.

This account of punishment, and in particular the idea of using punishment to deter future wrongdoing, is structurally similar to Victor Tadros’s duty view of punishment (Tadros 2011: ch. 12). But Tadros’s position is grounded in the private duty that wrongdoers have to their victims. His argument that this debt can be transferred to the state is strained since there are reasons to doubt that victims have a duty to allow the state to socialize what is owed to them (see Ferzan 2013); moreover, his view seems to have no purchase on victimless crimes. Walen’s account of the state’s duty to punish is grounded directly in the idea of a debt owed to the public for a culpable public wrong. The idea could then be extended, by analogy, to other social contexts ranging from professional associations to families (see Duff 2018: 80–91).

4. Desert

Desert has been analyzed as a three-way relationship between the person who deserves something, what they deserve, and that in virtue of which they deserve it. These can usefully be cast, respectively, as the desert subject, the desert object, and the desert basis (Feinberg 1970; Berman 2011: 437). A fourth dimension should also be noted: the person or persons who can appropriately give, or have a duty to give, the desert subject what they deserve: the desert agent. They raise a distinct set of issues, which are addressed in sections 4.3 and 4.4.

Desert is a favoring concept, meaning that to say that something is deserved is implicitly to say that something speaks normatively in favor of distributing what is deserved, i.e., that it contributes value to states of affairs in which the agent gets what they deserve, or that it provides reasons for responsive treatments (Berman 2024: 46).

Before discussing the three parts of desert, it is important to address the idea that desert is fundamentally a pre-institutional notion. As Joel Feinberg wrote:

desert is a moral concept in the sense that it is logically prior to and independent of public institutions and their rules. (Feinberg 1970: 87)

Accordingly, one challenge theorists of retributive justice often take themselves to face is to show how the criminal justice system can be, at least in part, justified by claims that wrongdoers deserve punishment in a pre-institutional sense. But it is also important to recognize that most theorists accord legal institutions a significant role in determining when and what punishment is deserved. That is because most theorists accept one or more of the following propositions: the law can make actions wrong that were not wrong before (mala prohibita); only violations of the law can justly be punished by the state (the legality restriction, often captured by the ban on ex post facto punishments); and legal schedules of punishment can have a role in determining what punishments people deserve (see Walen 2026: ch. 8.2).

4.1 The Desert Subject

Retributivism presents no special puzzles about who is the desert subject: the wrongdoer. What may be particularly problematic for retributivism is the claim that some persons (children or those with severe mental disorders) or entities (states or corporations) can or cannot deserve punishment. The fundamental issues are twofold: first, can the subject be responsible for wrongdoing? Second, does the subject have the cognitive (or information-processing) ability to appreciate the punishment as conveying condemnation for a wrong done, rather than seeing it simply as hard treatment? Some may lack both abilities (e.g., small children), while others may lack one ability but not the other (e.g., those with a transient mental disorder). (For contrasting views about punishing artificial persons, such as states or corporations, see French 1979; Narveson 2002; Tadros 2011; List and Pettit 2011.)

4.2 The Desert Object

What is it that wrongdoers deserve? A simple answer that has been used up to this point—to suffer their just punishment—glosses over a point that has been the source of much resistance to retributivism.

4.2.1 Suffering

Many, both defenders and critics of retributivism alike, presume that what wrongdoers deserve is “to suffer” (see e.g., Davis 1972; Alexander 2018; Zaibert 2018). Coupled with the prominent axiological view (see section 3.2), this results in the claim that “the suffering of the deserving is intrinsically good”. But many have puzzled over how this can be the case. As Hart put it, retributive justice “appears to be a mysterious piece of moral alchemy in which the combination of the two evils of moral wickedness and suffering are transmuted into good” (1968: 234–235).

A central retributivist response has been to argue that it is neither absurd nor barbaric to think that the normative valence of suffering might sometimes be positive (see e.g. Moore 1997; Zaibert 2006; Alexander & Ferzan (with Morse) 2009; Alexander 2018). But even if granted, a host of questions arise, such as whether suffering is itself valuable or if it is only the suffering of punishment that matters, and whether the suffering should be understood in terms of objective deprivations or subjective suffering.

More importantly, the ascription of intrinsic goodness to suffering has led many (e.g., Scanlon 2013) to reject retributivism, despite its intuitive appeal, on grounds that retributivism is necessarily “in conflict with a basic concern that [some] hold dear – that the lives of all people… go well” (Tadros 2013: 252). Articulating a deep skepticism about the moral credentials of retributive theory, Tadros continues: “Few claims need more defence than the claim that the suffering of other human beings is impersonally valuable” (harking back to the Benthamite anti-retributivist contention that “All punishment is mischief; all punishment is in itself evil” (Bentham 1789 [1986: 97])).

4.2.2 Other Desert Objects

This criticism can be escaped if retributivists are not committed to suffering per se being the desert object. Other desert objects that have been defended include the humbling of one’s will (Fingarette 1977), that one’s life go less well (Berman 2013), and the loss of liberty (Markel and Flanders 2010). Jeffrie Murphy has proposed that the retributive desert object is in fact suffering, but that suffering is not intrinsically related to pain, but should rather be identified with the term’s original meaning: “to endure something that is not within the control of one’s own will” (2016: 33). As explained in section 3.7, recent inquiries not primarily focused on the question of punishment develop the idea that what wrongdoers deserve is to feel guilty (Clarke 2013; see also Portmore 2024). This is on grounds that the feeling of guilt is an appropriate expression of moral concern (and related to caring about others or moral value).

A final desert object that should be considered is that what one deserves is not, once again, suffering per se, but rather punishment, in the sense of a burden imposed by another and in virtue of one’s wrongdoing. Bringing this desert object into focus reflects the idea that being subject to a proportionate quantity of pain goes nowhere towards realizing retributive value or justice. Rather the desert object must be imposed as a reaction to culpable wrongdoing by a properly situated desert agent who is permitted or pro tanto required to so respond (presumably, for the right reasons) (see Berman 2011; Dahan Katz 2023c; Walen 2026: ch. 5.1). If appropriate responses to wrongdoers are what is intrinsically good or right, retributivism is no longer vulnerable to the commonly deployed objection that it valorizes human suffering, even if we accept that imposing suffering, harm, or burdens are constitutive parts of appropriate responses.

4.3 The Desert Basis

The desert basis for retributive punishment is generally understood to be culpable wrongdoing. Wrongdoing presumes that the action in question violates a relevant norm or is impermissible. In purely moral or private practices of punishment this might refer to pre-institutional moral wrongs. In the case of state or criminal punishment, the wrongs that are subject to punishment are generally understood more restrictively, partly in virtue of the nature of the forms which criminal punishment takes (often involving deprivations of basic rights to liberty, property and even life) and partly in virtue of the restricted scope of political authority.

An important dimension of debate is whether all moral wrongs are at least presumptively a proper basis for state punishment (Moore 1997: 35–37), or only a subset of moral wrongs. One prominent way to delimit the relevant wrongs is to say that only public wrongs – that is, wrongs which the public is properly interested – may be the basis for state punishment (Duff 2018: 75–87; Hoskins and Duff 2024: §2). (For a criticism of Duff’s view of public wrongs, see Tadros 2016: 120–130; for a view that proposes that in addition to this civic-parochial constraint on criminalization, there is also a universal-procedural constraint that limits criminalization to “moral wrongdoing that significantly undermines the autonomy of others and is not necessary for democratic procedures to work appropriately”, see Bracaccini 2025.) On some views, it is not moral, but rather political forms of wrongdoing that warrant state punishment (see, e.g., Thorburn, who argues that criminal wrongs are those “concerned with attempts to usurp either the private jurisdiction of another person or the public jurisdiction of the people as a whole”, rather than a specified subset of moral wrongdoing (2011: 41)).

Furthermore, the mere violation of a norm or commission of an impermissible act is insufficient for retributive punishment, as one might act under conditions which render the commission non-culpable. Culpability here involves a number of conditions that must be met for an agent to deserve punishment. First, the agent in question must qualify as a responsible agent, generally requiring that they have adequate cognitive capacities as well as volitional control. The traditional view that “infancy” and “insanity” are inconsistent with just retributive punishment relies on those conditions being incompatible with responsible agency (see Simester 2021: ch. 17.5). Second, culpability requires a particular connection between general agential capacities and a particular wrongful act. To see this, consider an agent who takes another’s computer. If that agent honestly and reasonably believed the computer to be his (mistake), or was forced to take it by a villain who threatened to break his child’s legs (duress), the agent will not have acted culpably. While the conditions of culpability are a matter of serious controversy, one way of articulating what is required for culpability is that the agent not only fails to act in accordance with relevant reasons (which goes to the question of wrongdoing), but that they fail to engage with reasons in a morally appropriate way (see Edwards & Simester 2019: 55–57), e.g., by disregarding or undervaluing relevant reasons. Culpability can be understood to capture a subjective failure, which must attach to the objective failure of wrongdoing for an agent to deserve punishment. In criminal law, the culpability requirement often translates into mens rea requirements as well as doctrines of excuse.

A pair of substantive doctrinal questions about the limits of culpability have received much attention of late. One question is whether negligent acts suffice as a desert basis: for criticism of punishing negligent acts, see Alexander, Ferzan, & Morse 2009: ch. 3; for a defense of punishing negligent acts, see Stark 2016: chs. 7 & 8; Simester 2021: 248–251 & ch. 12. Another question is whether the resultant harms generated by wrongdoing matter to the desert basis: for criticism of the view that luck matters see, e.g., Alexander, Ferzan, & Morse 2009: ch. 5; for the contrary view, that luck can matter to the total punishment deserved, even if not to the desert basis itself, see Fletcher 2000: 472–483; Moore 1994; Duff 1996: ch. 13; Simester 2021: ch. 14; Berman 2023; Dahan Katz 2023d.

Finally, some have argued that nothing can serve as a desert basis because humans lack the free will necessary to deserve punishment. The laws of physics might be thought to imply that we are no more free and responsible for our “choices”, and therefore no more capable of deserving punishment, than any other physical object, be it a falling tree or a wild animal, or at least not subject to any form of responsibility that involves blame or condemnation (G. Strawson 1986; Pereboom 2001; N. Levy 2011; Caruso 2021). One can resist this move by arguing that while we are physical beings, most of us have the capacity to weigh reasons for and against particular options, and to choose—these being the key abilities for being responsible agents who can deserve punishment if they act wrongly (Fischer and Ravizza 1998; Morse 2004; Nadelhoffer 2013; Brink 2021; Sartorio 2023). See the entry on compatibilism for a survey of a range of possible responses to this argument.

4.4 Who May Punish?

Assuming that wrongdoers deserve to be punished, who has a right to inflict the punishment? Who, in other words, are the appropriate desert agents?

David Dolinko (1991) points out that there is a difference between someone morally deserving something and others having a right to give it to her. In one example, he imagines a father who (perversely) gives his reprobate son almost everything in his will, and leaves his loving and respectful son a pittance. We may believe that the loving son deserves to inherit at least half of his father’s estate, but that would not entitle anyone to take property from the other son to give to him (1991: 544), or justify doing things that otherwise would violate rights. This question is often posed as a challenge to the state’s authority to meet out deserved punishment: “even if [wrongdoers] deserve to suffer, or to be burdened in some distinctive way, why should it be for the state to inflict that suffering or that burden on them” (Hoskins and Duff 2024).

One way to respond to Dolinko’s criticism is to adopt a relational conception of retribution and desert. On a relational conception, the state is not an instrument for realizing an impersonal value or good, or for realizing an impersonal form of justice that could in principle have been realized by other agents (e.g. if they were equally well positioned to achieve the relevant good). Instead, the state is understood to be the particular agent that is called upon to respond to the wrong, not only in virtue of the fact that a culpable wrong has been committed, but in virtue of the particular relation that exists between the state and the wrongdoer, which makes it the responsibility of the state to react retributively to the wrong. (On relational retributivism see Dahan Katz 2023c.) Examples of relational views include Duff’s communicative view (Duff 2013: 205); and Dahan Katz’s response-retributive view (Dahan Katz 2021).

Another way to respond to Dolinko’s criticism within a retributive framework is to distinguish two kinds of desert: desert that corresponds to a view about what would be a good outcome, and desert that concerns rights (Hill 1999: 425–426; Berman 2008: 271–281). Dolinko’s example concerns the first kind of desert. But insofar as retributive desert presupposes loss of the right not to be punished, it is unsurprising that there should be some agents who have the right to mete it out.

If one takes this second, rights-focused line, one might start, as Hobbes and Locke did, with the view that in the state of nature the victim has the right to punish, and that the reasons for creating a state include reasons for potential victims to transfer that right to the state (Hobbes 1651 [1962]: chs. 14 & 18; Locke 1690 [1980]: ch. 9). One worry about this sort of view is that it could license vigilante punishment. Social contract theorists can handle this by emphasizing that people not only delegate but transfer their right to punish, retaining only a vestigial right to punish in the case of minor punishments, such as would be doled out outside the criminal justice system, or if the state fails or is unable to act. Communitarians like Antony Duff (2011: 6), however, object to even a vestigial right to vigilante punishment. Duff sees the state, which speaks on behalf of the whole community, as the only proper punisher, at least in the context of crimes. (For an even stronger position along these lines, see Hegel 1821 [1942]: §102.)

Others take a different view about vigilantes, namely that anyone is pro tanto entitled to punish a wrongdoer. Some forfeiture theorists hold that restrictions on the right to punish others who have forfeited their rights not to be punished arise only as a matter of political morality (Wellman 2017: 30–31). It is hard to see why a desert theorist could not take the same position. Indeed, some retributivists think that what vigilantes do should at least count against the total punishment someone is due (Husak 1990: 441–442; but see Kolber 2013 (discussed in section 3 of the supplementary document Challenges to the Notion of Retributive Proportionality) for a challenge to the logical implication that vigilantes “punish”).

Even if the state normally has an exclusive right to punish criminal wrongdoing, questions arise as to whether the state is permitted to punish if it sustains or fails to address important social injustices (from distributive injustice to the denial of civil and political rights to socially disempowered groups). This is now mostly framed in terms of lack of standing to punish (see, e.g., Duff 2001: 184–200; Tadros 2009; Shelby 2016: ch. 8; Kelly 2018: ch. 6; Walen 2026: ch. 10), though it may also be framed as reflecting other failures such as absence of political authority to punish. (For an overview of the literature on this, see B. Ewing 2018.)

4.5 Proportionality

Proportionality is intrinsic to retributivism. The desert bases are the very parameters that determine the proper measure of punishment, which is presumably what is required for punishment to be proportionate. Other justifications of punishment may struggle to articulate plausible proportionality constraints. For example, if a utilitarian determines that severe punishment of a minor offender will bring about optimal level of deterrence, the utilitarian will have reason to endorse what is intuitively overpunishment. (But see, e.g., McKenna who takes himself to defend a form of retributivism without accepting a proportionality requirement (2024: ch. 4).) Still the question of how to quantify and operationalize the idea of desert looms large, owing to epistemic challenges, difficulties of measurement, and the task of translation into concrete forms of burdens and harm. Thus a vast literature on “proportionality” exists—essentially addressing the question of how to calibrate the specific burdens to be imposed on a wrongdoer in accordance with their desert, as well as the question of whether this calibration is at all possible. These are addressed in the supplementary document on Challenges to the Notion of Retributive Proportionality.

5. Implications

While it is clear that retributive theory has direct implications for substantive questions about punishment—who to punish, how much and under what conditions—there is a vast literature exploring what further implications retributive commitments might have.

One implication concerns modes of punishment. While many take retributivism to support harsh modes of punishment, including capital punishment, life without parole, shaming punishments and unsavory prison conditions (Whitman 2003: 93–94), others have argued that such punishments, and even incarceration, are to be rejected on retributive grounds (see e.g. McDermott 2001; Markel 2001), even arguing that retributivist principles require welfare-enhancing practices such as visitation and furlough for prisoners (Bulow and Dagan 2020), and expungement (Murray 2021).

Another literature examines the implications of retributivism for criminal procedure and evidentiary requirements for punishment. This includes its implications for, e.g., prosecutorial discretion, plea bargaining, and the beyond-reasonable-doubt standard (see e.g. Christopher 2003; Cahill 2007; Walen 2015; Galoob 2017; Kolber 2024).

There are also questions about how much of the criminal law can be justified in retributive terms and what it means for the law if hard treatment cannot be justified in those terms. Doug Husak, for example, has observed that “few if any retributivists seem enthusiastic about increasing rates of punishment” (Husak 2026: 67) and inquires why that should be. He raises five possibilities, including that the value of punishing the deserving is very small, or that it is outweighed by competing values. But even if the value of punishing the deserving is very small, it may still be true that desert is a necessary precondition of punishment, and that it structures what could count as a proportional punishment.

Following up on the proportionality point, Walen argues that proxy crimes—which allow the state to try to prevent unjust or socially destructive behavior by penalizing a proxy to the intrinsically wrongful behavior—make up a substantial amount of modern criminal law, and that the penalties that states use to enforce such laws often cannot be justified as proportional in retributive terms (Walen 2026: ch. 12). One possible response to this challenge is to demand that punishments for these offenses be reduced in order to conform to the limits of proportional punishment. Another is to regard the criminal law as comprising two kinds of law: “proper” criminal law, which is retributively justified, grounding criminal punishment, and an analogous kind of law (which Walen calls “penal law”) that is justified by appeal to the notion of fair forfeiture of the right not to suffer serious legal penalties (where penalties do not purport to reinforce censure or reflect the level of culpability in unlawful action). (For a defense of this contrast, see Walen 2026, part IV.)

Finally, within the vast literature on apology, remorse and repair, there is a live debate about whether retributivism has implications for the punishment of wrongdoers who have already seen the error of their ways. Some take ex-post first-personal reactions to wrongdoing to be irrelevant to retributive punishment, while others think such factors modify the agent’s desert or retributively apt punishment. (See e.g. von Hirsch 1993: 10; Duff 2007; Murphy 2011; Zaibert 2018; Walen 2026 ch. 7.3.)

6. Conclusion

Retributive justice remains a central, though contested, component of contemporary thinking about punishment. Its enduring appeal lies in its ability to capture powerful moral intuitions about responsibility, desert, and proportionality—intuitions that alternative theories struggle fully to explain. At the same time, its justificatory foundations remain deeply disputed, and the diversity of retributive views reflects ongoing efforts to reconcile these intuitions with broader moral and political commitments.
