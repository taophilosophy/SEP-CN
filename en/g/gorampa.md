# Gorampa [go rams pa]
**First published Mon Nov 23, 2026**

*Conlon, Ryan*

[*Editor’s Note: The following new entry by Ryan Conlon replaces the [former entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/gorampa/) on this topic by the previous author.*]

Gorampa Sönam Sengé (Go rams pa bSod nams seng ge, 1429–1489) is one of the most widely studied philosophers of Tibetan Buddhism. Both a polemicist and a systematizer, he is known especially for his fierce critique of Tsongkhapa and subsequent Geluk thinkers, and for formalizing the philosophical orthodoxy of the subsequent Sakya tradition. While Gorampa’s works faced suppression for centuries as the Geluk dominated Tibetan politics and access to printing resources, they were nevertheless widely contested by other polemicists, taken up as textbooks by Sakya monastic colleges, and influential on non-Sakya Tibetan intellectuals, such as the Nyingma scholar Ju Mipham (’Ju Mi pham rnam rgyal rgya mtsho, 1846–1912).

Like most Tibetan Buddhist philosophers, Gorampa identifies as a proponent of Madhyamaka (Middle Way) philosophy, adhering to ideas first developed by Nāgārjuna in second century CE India. In Tibet, by the fifteenth century, substantially different interpretations of this philosophy had emerged, which also began to demarcate sectarian divisions. Gorampa’s work represents a critique of views he characterizes as philosophical extremes—particularly those of Tsongkhapa (Tsong kha pa Blo bzang grags pa, 1357–1419) and Dölpopa (Dol po pa Shes rab rgyal mtshan, 1292–1361). Gorampa also presents a coherent system of concepts, definitions, and categories that are intended to reflect the thought of the early Sakya patriarchs (active from the eleventh through thirteenth centuries). While other scholars of his generation pursued similar projects—notably Taktsang Lotsawa (sTag tshang lo tsā ba Shes rab rin chen, 1405–1477) and Gorampa’s main rival, Shākya Chokden (Shā kya mchog ldan, 1428–1507)—Gorampa’s work proved most enduring within the Sakya school, admired for its thoroughness, consistency, and clean resolutions to exegetical cruxes.

While Gorampa also made substantial contributions to the study of epistemology and the literature of the Perfection of Wisdom, the present entry focuses on his Madhyamaka thought, highlighting his distinctive formulation of the two-truths doctrine, his account of argumentation and logic as they pertain to emptiness, and his treatment of Buddha-nature as the ontological ground supporting the realization of ultimate truth and the attainment of buddhahood.

## 1. Life and Works

Gorampa’s life is recorded in at least twelve extant biographies composed before the seventeenth century, some of which were written by close students. Jamtsho (2023)[^jamtsho-2023] currently provides the most detailed synthesis of this biographical material. He was born in 1429 in Eastern Tibet, where he entered a monastery at age eight and began studying and memorizing philosophical texts. At age nineteen, like many young men from the region, he traveled to Central Tibet to pursue more intensive training. He briefly attended Nalendra Monastery, a prominent Sakya institution, where he studied under the famed scholar Rongtön Sheja Künrik (Rong ston Shes bya kun rig, 1367–1449). Rongtön passed away the following winter, and Gorampa began traveling across Central and West-Central Tibet, receiving teachings from a variety of prominent masters. In addition to his extensive training in exoteric philosophy, Gorampa mastered a variety of tantric systems, including the Lamdré (*lam ’bras*), the defining esoteric practice of the Sakya school.

Biographers praise Gorampa’s conceptual understanding (*go blo*) during his student years and aptitude for debate (*rtsod blo*), but note that poor health prevented him from embarking on a planned “debate tour” (*grwa skor*) across the major monasteries of Central Tibet. The biographers’ emphasis on these details highlights the centrality of competitive oral debate in the monastic culture of fifteenth-century Tibet. Indeed, for subsequent students, Gorampa’s works are attractive partly because of their usefulness in oral debate: definitions and categorizations are structured so as to minimize logical loopholes, while some texts are even composed entirely using Tibetan syllogistic formulations. Such writing can seem overly technical to modern readers and poses challenges to natural-sounding translation, yet it is extremely helpful for students in the debate courtyard. Gorampa’s polemics likewise contain moments of humorous argumentation and are interspersed with barbed insults. In non-argumentative contexts, however, Gorampa speaks respectfully of his opponents. This reflects an enduring Tibetan debate culture that balances personal respect, humor, and rigorous formal logic.

At age thirty-two, Gorampa was appointed assistant lecturer (*zur chos pa*) at Dreyül Kyetsel (’Bras yul skyed tshal), the institution where he had studied the longest, and just four years later he served briefly as an interim abbot. In 1467, he founded a monastic college, and in 1473 established his own seat, Thupten Namgyel Ling (Thub bstan rnam rgyal gling). Encouraged by his teachers to revive the unique tenets of the Sakya school, Gorampa composed the majority of his philosophical texts during this period. His biographies attest to extensive teaching, travel, and material donations to Sakya institutions. In 1483, he was appointed abbot of Ngor Evaṃ Chöden (Ngor Evaṃ chos ldan), a prestigious Sakya center for tantric study and practice. He held this role until 1486, after which he retired to Thupten Namgyel Ling and passed away three years later.

Although during Gorampa’s lifetime Sakya institutions still extended across Tibet, the order no longer enjoyed direct political power. Instead, other religious orders, particularly the emergent Geluk school that took Tsongkhapa as its patriarch, expanded rapidly and attracted substantial patronage. The house of Phakmodru had violently ousted the Sakya and their Mongol (Yuan) backers in 1354, but conflicts within the new ruling dynasty led to a breakaway faction in West-Central Tibet known as the Rinpungpas. From his student days, Gorampa had received patronage from the Rinpung court. Sources suggest that the Sakya unsuccessfully attempted to leverage ties with the Rinpungpas to block Geluk expansion in West-Central Tibet (Cabezón and Dargyay 2007, 44)[^cabezón-2007]; when the Rinpungpas did eventually move against the Geluk, they did so in collaboration with another Sakya rival, the Karma Kagyü.

Perhaps more than practice or lineage, philosophical doctrine became the primary distinguishing characteristic of the new Geluk order. Its burgeoning success in securing patronage, which included ties to the Ming court and its printing technology, left the Sakya in a precarious position. If they were to prevail in the new religious and political reality, they would not only need to refute Tsongkhapa and his followers philosophically but also to establish a consensus on their own philosophical orthodoxy. While Gorampa’s works do not engage directly with power politics, this sectarian context forms the backdrop of his polemics and efforts to systematize the thought of the early Sakya patriarchs.

Gorampa’s biographies document his many pupils, with one account listing sixty-nine prominent figures including abbots, scholars, aristocrats, reincarnate teachers, and meditators (Jamtsho 2023, 57–58)[^jamtsho-2023]. When the Geluk achieved full political control of Tibet in the seventeenth century, the Ganden Phodrang government proscribed Gorampa’s writings, owing to their harsh critiques of Tsongkhapa. The printing and copying of his texts were banned, and existing copies were either confiscated or destroyed—a fate shared by most of the philosophical literature of the Sakya tradition. Although some of Gorampa’s works are now lost, they nevertheless mostly survived the suppression—in fact, Geluk authorities continued to target him over the subsequent centuries in their polemics. By the nineteenth century, a renaissance of philosophical inquiry, meditation, and philological studies developed in Eastern Tibet. There, Gorampa’s surviving works were collected and, for the first time, carved onto woodblocks and printed as a set in the early twentieth century.

Gorampa’s collected works comprise 144 titles spanning thirteen volumes (for a complete survey of the collection, see Jamtsho 2023)[^jamtsho-2023]. In addition to Madhyamaka treatises, he composed commentaries on Abhidharma, Vinaya, and the Perfection of Wisdom sūtras; treatises on logic and epistemology, including well-known commentaries on Dharmakīrti’s (sixth or seventh century CE) *Pramāṇavārttika* (translated in Kilty 2023) and Sakya Paṇḍita’s (Sa skya paṇḍita, 1182–1251) *Treasury of Epistemology* (*Tshad ma rigs gter*); exegetical works and practical manuals concerning Buddhist tantra; devotional hymns, prayers, and praises; as well as epistles, meditation guides, and a monastic charter. He also wrote extensively on Sakya Paṇḍita’s *Distinguishing the Three Vows* (*sDom gsum rab dbye*), which covers doctrinal and practical issues related to the vows of a monk, the trainings of a bodhisattva, and the esoteric commitments of a tantric practitioner. Four works are particularly important for his views on Madhyamaka:

1. *Distinguishing the Views* (*lTa ba’i shan ’byed*), a polemical text distinguishing Gorampa’s Madhyamaka position from those of Tsongkhapa and Dölpopa (English translation in Cabezón and Dargyay 2007)[^cabezón-2007].
2. *Dispelling Wrong Views* (*lTa ba ngan sel*), a commentary on Candrakīrti’s *Madhyamakāvatāra*.
3. *Synopsis of Madhyamaka* (*dBu ma spyi don*), a treatise exhaustively covering Gorampa’s interpretation of Madhyamaka, while also documenting the views of diverse Indian and Tibetan thinkers.
4. *Addendum to Distinguishing the Three Vows* (*sDom gsum kha skong*), a work that, although classified outside the Madhyamaka section of his collected works, elaborates on Gorampa’s conceptualization of Buddha-nature, alongside other issues related to the path and fruition (English translation in Jamtsho 2023)[^jamtsho-2023].

Each of these texts represents a slightly different exegetical style. In *Distinguishing the Views*, Gorampa devotes separate chapters to refuting the systems of Tsongkhapa and Dölpopa, which he characterizes as lapsing into the extremes of nihilism and eternalism, respectively. *Dispelling Wrong Views*, while offering resolutions for certain difficult points in Candrakīrti’s *Madhyamakāvatāra*, focuses on dismantling Tsongkhapa’s commentary on the same text by identifying fifty-eight exegetical and logical flaws. Throughout he refers to his opponent as “a certain self-styled Mādhyamika” (*dbu ma par rlom pa kha cig*). The *Synopsis of Madhyamaka* presents Gorampa’s own system according to the classical triad of ground (*gzhi*), path (*lam*), and fruition (*’bras bu*). These cover the two truths, the stages of spiritual practice, and the nature of complete awakening. Although still heavily preoccupied with refuting Tsongkhapa, the treatise is especially valuable for detailing the views of earlier Tibetan thinkers. Finally, while the *Addendum to Distinguishing the Three Vows* continues to criticize Dölpopa and certain followers of Tsongkhapa, it articulates Gorampa’s most detailed account of Buddha-nature, presenting it as the ontological ground that is at once the intrinsic nature of the mind and the basis enabling all sentient beings to attain buddhahood.

Throughout his polemical works, Gorampa often leaves his opponents anonymous, as is customary in Indian and Tibetan philosophical writing. For instance, he rather harshly criticizes Taktsang Lotsawa, who was a senior Sakya critic of Tsongkhapa, without once mentioning the scholar’s name (see the first opposing view on the extension of conventional truth detailed below). At other times, however, Gorampa clearly identifies specific opponents, going so far in his *Distinguishing the Views* as to cite folio numbers from Tsongkhapa’s treatises. At his harshest, Gorampa speculates that Tsongkhapa’s reported vision of Mañjuśrī may have been the work of a demon (*Distinguishing the Views*, p. 201), a remark that became infamous and no doubt helped to demonize his own work. When formulating his own positions, Gorampa consistently cites the Sakya patriarchs and presents himself as rearticulating their views. Occasionally, however, he acknowledges the originality of his interpretations. For example, when commenting on the *Abhisamayālaṅkāra*, a work attributed to the celestial bodhisattva and future Buddha Maitreya, Gorampa states that even if his reading diverges from all “moustached Indians and red-faced Tibetans,” he feels confident so long as his interpretation accords with the intention of that venerable old god residing in Tuṣita heaven (*Clarifying the Mother’s Meaning*, p. 110).

## 2. Gorampa’s Madhyamaka

Gorampa, like his Mādhyamika interlocutors, sees himself as elucidating the thought of Nāgārjuna, who is traditionally regarded as founding Madhyamaka through his independent interpretation of the Buddha’s discourses and creation of a philosophical system. Nāgārjuna’s corpus presents arguments for the emptiness of all phenomena or their lack of inherent nature and develops an account of dependent arising and nominal designation to explain the phenomenal world. It then ties these two together within a framework of two truths: ultimate truth (Skt. *paramārthasatya*) and conventional truth (Skt. *saṃvṛtisatya*). Nāgārjuna was writing before many later developments in Mahāyāna Buddhism, including the Yogācāra theories of mind and mental phenomena; the concept of the cosmic qualities of buddhahood and their guaranteed potentiality, or even innate presence, within the minds of all sentient beings; the advanced formalization of logic and epistemology initiated by Dignāga and Dharmakīrti; and the ritual and meditative techniques presented in tantric Buddhism. Madhyamaka was thus continually rearticulated in India to accommodate the ever-evolving doctrinal environment.

Although Buddhist study and practice had spread to Tibet by the eighth century, it was only in the eleventh century that Indian modes of logic and systematic philosophy were thoroughly introduced, beginning three centuries of intellectual flourishing that have been called the dawn of Tibetan scholasticism (Hugon and Vose 2009)[^hugon-2009][^vose-2009]. Not long after the Madhyamaka works of Candrakīrti were translated, they achieved a popularity in Tibet that surpassed anything they enjoyed in India (Vose 2009)[^vose-2009]. Paradoxically, Candrakīrti challenged the validity of logic and scholasticism through his skepticism of autonomous reasoning (Skt. *svatantrānumāna*) and his embrace of a pre-analytic, everyday perspective. Much of Tibetan philosophy from the fourteenth century onward thus sought to reconcile Candrakīrtian skepticism with the rigors of Dharmakīrtian logic and epistemology. By this time, Buddhism had largely disappeared from India, ending the transmission of new lineages, teachings, and texts into Tibet. Tibetans increasingly displayed their confidence in formulating accounts of Buddhism in its totality. Tsongkhapa’s corpus exemplifies precisely this, organizing myriad Buddhist themes into a coherent ethical foundation, a path of gradual practice, and an overarching framework of Indian tenet systems.

During what Seyfort Ruegg (2000) has termed the “classical-systematic period” (fourteenth to seventeenth centuries), Tibetan thinkers broadly agreed on the basic structures of Madhyamaka philosophy—the framework of the two truths, the need to directly perceive emptiness to achieve liberation and buddhahood, and the capacity of all sentient beings to actualize awakening. Nevertheless, the core concepts of emptiness and conventional truth emerged as sites of contestation, as did the status of logic and conceptual thought in understanding and practicing the system. This section examines Gorampa’s distinctive reading of Madhyamaka through three topics: (1) his conception of the two truths; (2) his understanding of the role of argumentation and logic, especially regarding negation; and (3) his soteriological account of Buddha-nature, the realization of emptiness, and the state of complete awakening. Since Gorampa’s positions often emerged in part from his reaction to other views, his thought will be presented in dialogue with his opponents, foremost among them Tsongkhapa.

### 2.1 The Two Truths

Buddhist teachings and scholastic discourse consistently distinguish between conventional truth, which operates in everyday contexts, and ultimate truth, which is disclosed through rational analysis or perceived by error-free cognition. Madhyamaka makes this distinction central to its philosophical and soteriological vision. In his *Synopsis of Madhyamaka*, Gorampa systematically delineates the two truths in terms of their taxonomy, instantiation, and definition.

In addressing what the two truths classify, Gorampa proceeds on two levels: their systemic role within Madhyamaka thought as a whole, and the precise phenomenal basis for their division. At the systemic level, the truths constitute the “middle way” itself, both individually and in their inseparability. Gorampa notes that the term Madhyamaka refers to either the textual tradition stemming from Nāgārjuna or the reality posited by that tradition, which covers three dimensions: the ontological ground, as the union of the two truths; the path of practice, as the union of meritorious deeds and gnostic insight; and the fruition of buddhahood, as the union of the Buddha’s physical forms that manifest to sentient beings and his *dharmakāya*, or “truth body,” which is an expression of the ultimate. Conventional truth is a middle way insofar as it is dependent arising, characterized by causal relationships that are neither random nor eternal. Ultimate truth is likewise a middle way because it transcends conceptual characterizations, including existence, non-existence, both, and neither. Finally, the two truths as a union also form a middle way. The unfailing manifestation of dependently arisen appearances precludes the extreme of annihilation (Skt. *ucchedānta*; Tib. *chad mtha’*) or the denial of conventional experience. At the same time, that these appearances have no inherent nature and are ultimately empty precludes the extreme of eternalism (Skt. *śāśvatānta*; Tib. *rtag mtha’*), which is innately assumed by sentient beings and reified by realist philosophies.

While Mādhyamikas agree that the two truths comprise the Middle Way, they debate over precisely what serves as the basis of their division (Tib. *dbye gzhi*; Lat. *fundamentum divisionis*). Gorampa maintains that conventional truth is deemed “truth” only from the perspective of erroneous cognition. He therefore rejects the idea that both truths are forms of truth. Then what common domain or genus do these two categories actually divide? Gorampa endorses four complementary formulations of their basis:

1. “Mere dependent arising” (*rten ’brel tsam*), since dependent arising can be distinguished based on its mode of appearance as the phenomenal world and its mode of reality as emptiness.
2. “Mere mind” (*blo tsam*), since whether something is posited as a truth or not depends on whether its cognition is veridical or erroneous.
3. “Mere objects of knowledge” (*shes bya tsam*), since all knowable phenomena are contained within the two truths.
4. “The mere subject matter of scripture” (*gsung rab kyi brjod bya tsam*), which Gorampa regards as the most fitting formulation, since the two truths are fundamentally a pedagogical framework used by the Buddha to guide disciples.

The qualifier “mere” is applied in all cases because both the basis of division and the resulting categories are established only conventionally, their purpose being to facilitate communication and transaction. They do not withstand analytical scrutiny and thus are not ultimately valid.

Given this taxonomy, what phenomena instantiate conventional truth and ultimate truth—in other words, what constitutes their individual extensions (Tib. *mtshan gzhi*)? On this question, too, Gorampa arrives at his position through a critique of earlier Tibetan scholars. In general, the category of “conventional truth” (Skt. *saṃvṛtisatya*) can have a broad or narrow scope, depending on context: broadly construed, it encompasses the entire domain of mistaken cognition, or everything that is concealing (Skt. *saṃvṛti*) with respect to the ultimate; strictly construed, it applies to the domain of everyday transaction and linguistic usage (Skt. *vyavahāra*). Concerning the latter, Gorampa identifies four flawed accounts by earlier Tibetans.

1. That conventional truth is mere worldly consensus—the unexamined assumptions of ordinary people—which Mādhyamikas, who have no theses of their own, merely report rather than endorse.
2. That conventional truth is whatever is known by unawakened people, whereas ultimate truth is whatever is known by advanced bodhisattvas.
3. That conventional truth, like ultimate truth, transcends all conceptual fabrications, being neither existent nor non-existent.
4. That conventional truth is mere conceptual and linguistic designation, entirely devoid of objective referents.

The final view is Tsongkhapa’s, advanced as a distinctive feature of Candrakīrti’s system to provide a thoroughly nominalist and anti-realist account of conventional truth. On Tsongkhapa’s reading, denying objective status beyond nominal designation also yields the most robust formulation of emptiness. While Gorampa agrees that conventional truth is relational and rooted in the conceptual assumptions of ordinary beings, he denies that this renders language devoid of an objective referent. If, on the conventional level, terms and their corresponding referents do not share the same ontological status, then the causal and relational character of dependent arising that Tsongkhapa wishes to enhance is compromised.

Gorampa maintains that, despite thoroughly refuting inherent existence, Mādhyamikas endorse their own specific account of conventional reality. As implied by the very term “conventional,” this domain cannot be ineffable or completely beyond the scope of conceptual categorization. Furthermore, conventional truth is not defined with reference to classes of individuals, since both ordinary and awakened beings are equally capable of cognizing both truths; rather, the two truths should be tied to specific cognitive modes within those individuals. For Gorampa, the conventional truth posited by Mādhyamikas must above all facilitate teaching fundamental Buddhist doctrines: karma and its effects, practices of the path, and the reasoning that establishes the ultimate truth. Conventional truth thus comprises phenomena that arise in dependence on causes and conditions (e.g., seeds producing sprouts) or on conceptual designation (e.g., “tall” in relation to “short”). When accepted, this everyday world is left completely untouched by analytical scrutiny, in accordance with ordinary cognition that, although ultimately mistaken, is free of adventitious causes of perceptual error.

Gorampa recognizes two principal Indian traditions regarding how conventional truth is posited: the Yogācāra-Madhyamaka approach, best represented by Śāntarakṣita (eighth century CE), and the Madhyamaka approach that adheres to worldly consensus (*’jig rten grags sde spyod pa’i dbu ma*), articulated most clearly in the work of Candrakīrti (seventh century CE). In the former, conventional reasoning refutes material objects, reducing all appearances to mental phenomena. By first dismantling the reification of matter, this approach gradually introduces students to emptiness while maintaining a type of metaphysical idealism on the conventional level. According to Gorampa, the Mādhyamikas who adopt this approach equate conventional reality with momentary and functional entities, thereby centering objects themselves as the source of conventional validity.

The second approach radically eschews analytical engagement with conventional phenomena, reserving such analysis strictly for demonstrating their emptiness. Mind and material objects are ontologically on par, as are parts and wholes, causes and effects, and terms and referents. This approach defines legitimate conventions not on the basis of functional entities but solely with reference to what is experienced by the unimpaired cognition of ordinary sentient beings, unencumbered by any philosophical or analytical complications. The result, Gorampa concludes, is a cleaner and more consistent view of dependent arising.

Gorampa endorses both accounts of conventional reality as pedagogically suitable for different practitioners. It is clear, however, that they are to some degree incompatible: one affirms mind-independent objects, while the other denies them. Gorampa explains that those who transition to Madhyamaka from other Buddhist philosophical systems often maintain their earlier metaphysical commitments when positing conventional reality. He goes so far as to admit the theoretical possibility of Vaibhāṣika-Madhyamaka, arguing that even though he could find no historical example of such a position, Vaibhāṣikas too can be persuaded by emptiness (Hugon 2020)[^hugon-2020]. Gorampa therefore accommodates diverse and conflicting accounts of conventional truth so long as they accord with dependent arising and can serve as a basis for teaching Buddhism and revealing emptiness. Nevertheless, Candrakīrti’s view remains for him the most elegant and coherent.

Gorampa’s signature formulation of ultimate truth is freedom from all conceptual proliferation (*spros pa thams cad dang bral ba nyid*). Because no intrinsic nature can be found when entities are analytically scrutinized, all phenomena are empty, and their emptiness constitutes ultimate truth. Crucially, emptiness itself does not withstand analysis; hence, ultimate truth cannot be defined simply as the absence of intrinsic nature in conventional phenomena. Gorampa sharply criticizes Tsongkhapa and his followers for treating ultimate truth exclusively as a simple privation in this sense. Rather, ultimate truth is beyond all conceptual proliferations, including existence, non-existence, both, and neither. Epistemically, it is not the object of language or discursive thought, except by way of conceptual approximation. At the level of this approximation, it relates to conventional truth as its intrinsic reality (Skt. *dharmatā*; Tib. *chos nyid*), not simply as an isolated property but as indivisibly “one in flavor” (Skt. *ekarasa*; Tib. *ro gcig*) with its phenomenal instantiations (Skt. *dharmin*; Tib. *chos can*).

Madhyamaka discourse deploys a variety of arguments to refute the intrinsic nature of phenomena. Through a process of gradual elimination, all conceptual proliferation can be dispelled from the mind, leading to an unmediated and error-free experience of ultimate truth. Gorampa distinguishes the ultimate truth established by reasoning and articulated through language, which he terms the “ultimate truth of instruction” (*bstan pa’i don dam*), from that which is experienced in non-conceptual gnosis, termed the “ultimate truth of realization” (*rtogs pa’i don dam*). While certain Madhyamaka traditions assign the former a merely nominal status (*rnam grangs pa’i don dam*), Gorampa prefers to speak of a single ultimate truth that can be either approximated through conventional means or experienced directly. This direct realization fully eradicates the innate apprehension of true existence in phenomena and, by gradually eliminating all latent traces of mental afflictions and cognitive obscurations, culminates in the attainment of buddhahood.

Despite disagreements among Tibetans over the taxonomy and extension of the two truths, Gorampa sees a consensus regarding their formal definitions: all Mādhyamikas agree that the truths are defined with reference to the cognitive modes that apprehend them. Thus, adapting Candrakīrti’s words, Gorampa defines conventional truth as “the object disclosed when false seeing apprehends the nature of an entity” (*mthong ba brdzun pas dngos po’i ngo bo rnyed pa’i rnyed yul*), and ultimate truth as “the object disclosed when veridical seeing apprehends the nature of an entity” (*mthong ba yang dag pas dngos po’i ngo bo rnyed pa’i rnyed yul*) (*Synopsis of Madhyamaka*, p. 91). The former definition applies to conventional truth in the broader sense mentioned above, as it also covers what Candrakīrti calls “erroneous conventions” (Skt. *mithyāsaṃvṛti*)—illusory or imagined objects apprehended through impaired sensory or cognitive faculties.

Mādhyamikas identify cognitive error (Skt. *avidyā*) as the root cause of conventional truth, which manifests as conceptually fabricated and deceptively dualistic, thereby perpetuating error and rebirth in *saṃsāra*. After directly experiencing ultimate truth, the practitioner no longer sees conventional phenomena as truly existent; nevertheless, during the post-meditative state, conceptually fabricated phenomena continue to present themselves dualistically. This form of conventional truth is called “the mere conventional” (Skt. *saṃvṛtimātra*; Tib. *kun rdzob tsam*), since it is no longer reified as truth. Finally, at the culmination of the path, when all traces of ignorance are eradicated, Gorampa asserts that the Buddha no longer intrinsically experiences conventional phenomena.

### 2.2 Argumentation and Logic

Mādhyamikas primarily employ analytic argumentation to establish emptiness: taking phenomena as the subject term of inquiry, they demonstrate the lack of inherent existence therein by exhaustively rejecting all conceivable modes of such existence. The opening verse of Nāgārjuna’s *Fundamental Verses of the Middle Way* (*Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*) provides the classic formulation of such an argument:

> There exists no thing whatsoever, in any place or at any time, that arises from itself, from another, from both, or without a cause. (*Fundamental Verses of the Middle Way* 1.1; author’s translation)

The upshot is that if things do not arise in any of these four ways—which exhaustively cover all modes of production—then there can be nothing that inherently exists.

These four modes of production are one example of a tetralemma (Skt. *catuṣkoṭi*), a schema that, analogous to a classical dichotomy, is intended to exhaustively partition all logical possibilities. A hallmark of Gorampa’s view is his application of a tetralemma to establish ultimate truth as freedom from all conceptual proliferation. In doing so, he frequently cites the *Compendium of the Essence of Wisdom* (*Jñānasārasamuccaya*), traditionally attributed to Āryadeva (modern philology does not accept this attribution; the verse can also be found in a variety of other sources):

> Mādhyamikas know the reality that is free of the four extremes: it is neither existent, nor non-existent, nor both existent and non-existent, nor of the nature of neither. (*Jñānasārasamuccaya* 28; author’s translation)

Like all Mādhyamikas, Gorampa believes that the analytical refutation of these extremes should be transposed into a meditative practice that reduces and eventually eliminates the cognitive reification of phenomena by unveiling a liberating, unmediated experience of reality. In fifteenth-century Tibet, however, questions about what the tetralemma negates, and how a refutation of both existence and non-existence can be logically viable, came to dominate Madhyamaka discourse. On these matters, too, Gorampa articulates a distinctive stance.

#### 2.2.1 Refuting the Extreme of Existence

Gorampa maintains that freedom from all conceptual proliferation must be approached gradually through the refutation of each individual extreme. The extreme of existence is targeted first for several reasons. Primarily, the apprehension of entities as truly existent is regarded as the root cause of rebirth in *saṃsāra*, and its eradication is a prerequisite for achieving even the lower, non-Mahāyāna states of liberation, such as the attainment of arhatship. Furthermore, conceptual thought is by its very nature capable of engaging only one refutation at a time—it is unable to simultaneously negate both existence and non-existence. Finally, the arguments found throughout Madhyamaka literature are primarily aimed at refuting the first lemma. In summarizing these arguments, Gorampa follows earlier Tibetan scholars in classifying them into “five great reasons” (*gtan tshigs chen po lnga*), which probe for an inherent nature in phenomena either in terms of their mode of production or their mode of existence. The “diamond splinters” argument (*rdo rje gzegs ma*) is expressed in the above-cited passage from the *Fundamental Verses of the Middle Way*: phenomena are not inherently existent because they do not arise in any of the four possible modes.

Tsongkhapa’s Madhyamaka discourse is noted for stressing the need to correctly identify the object of negation in these arguments. Refuting too much leads to a complete denial of conventional truth, whereas negating too little is ineffective in counteracting reification. Some of Gorampa’s sharpest critiques frame Tsongkhapa’s cautious approach as an overreaction: in trying to avoid falling off the left side of the horse, Tsongkhapa leans too far and falls off the right (*Distinguishing the Views*, 95). But when it comes to refuting the first lemma, the extreme of existence, both scholars agree: the object of negation is strictly the intentional object of reification—namely, truly existent things. Gorampa does not target mere appearance, and unlike Tibetan thinkers such as Taktsang Lotsawa and the Ninth Karmapa (Yakherds 2021)[^yakherds-2021], he affirms a uniquely Madhyamaka account of conventional reality.

It is in fact Dölpopa Sherap Gyeltsen, rather than Tsongkhapa, whom Gorampa regards as erring fundamentally when negating the extreme of existence. Dölpopa restricts his object of negation to the dualistic phenomena of *saṃsāra*: these, he says, are “self-empty” (*rang stong*) and cannot be found under analysis. However, the phenomena of *nirvāṇa*—the luminous nature of mind, the Buddha’s gnosis, and emptiness itself—are “other-empty” (*gzhan stong*) because they are empty of adventitious, dualistic defilements, but not of inherent existence. By excluding these phenomena from the object of negation, Dölpopa’s system becomes a form of eternalist realism that, in Gorampa’s estimation, blends Yogācāra terminology with non-Buddhist metaphysics. Gorampa maintains that all phenomena—be they pure or impure, conditioned or unconditioned—are unfindable under analysis.

Concerning the logical form of arguments that negate inherent existence, Gorampa and other Tibetan exegetes, extrapolating from Candrakīrti’s *Prasannapadā*, recognize two Madhyamaka sub-schools that advocate distinct approaches. In modern secondary literature, these sub-schools are referred to by the Sanskrit retro-translations Svātantrika- and Prāsaṅgika-Madhyamaka (Tib. *rang brgyud pa’i dbu ma* and *thal ’gyur ba’i dbu ma*). Tibetan authorities debated numerous differences between these two putative schools, with Gorampa himself enumerating fourteen major and minor points of divergence (*Dispelling Wrong Views*, p. 336 ff.). In terms of formal argumentation, however, Svātantrikas are said to deploy arguments using terms that both Mādhyamikas and their opponents acknowledge, whereas Prāsaṅgikas avoid this by formulating arguments as reductios (Skt. *prasaṅga*; Tib. *thal ’gyur*) or as inferences that are only acknowledged by the opponent (*gzhan la grags pa’i rjes dpag*).

For Gorampa, this formal distinction does not entail different accounts of ultimate truth; it does, however, lead to certain methodological advantages and disadvantages. By negating inherent existence through a reductio, Mādhyamikas need not implicitly accept a common subject of debate; if they were to accept one in the context of analytically scrutinizing the nature of phenomena, this would imply that the subject phenomenon withstands that analysis. Prāsaṅgikas also do not establish an affirmative position that holds ultimately. By dismantling belief in the extreme of existence without committing to any secondary thesis in its place, reductio arguments lead the student or opponent more directly to freedom from all conceptual proliferation. Gorampa sharply criticizes Tsongkhapa’s account of the Prāsaṅgika preference for reductios, but as these critiques are largely procedural and formal, they will not be elaborated here (see Dreyfus and McClintock 2003)[^dreyfus-2003].

A tension between the negation of the four modes of production and the status of conventional truth is highlighted by a crucial distinction Gorampa identifies between the Svātantrika and Prāsaṅgika approaches. Following its particular methodological orientation, Svātantrika-Madhyamaka prioritizes logical consistency across both truths. Moreover, like Yogācāra-Madhyamaka described earlier (which Gorampa regards as a subset of Svātantrika-Madhyamaka), the system grounds its account of conventional reality in momentary, functional entities. Thus, since a seed and a sprout are distinct particulars, they and all other causes and effects must be different. Gorampa, following Candrakīrti, contends that this position undermines the Svātantrikas’ desired logical consistency: the Madhyamaka refutation of production from an effect that is distinct from its cause cannot be maintained at face value if conventionally cause and effect are different entities. Moreover, since causes and non-causes are equally distinct, causal regularity (and the induction that relies on it) cannot be guaranteed. In the Prāsaṅgika system, then, following pre-analytical and transactional norms, cause and effect are neither the same nor different. Is this a rejection of binary logic on the conventional level? Gorampa firmly denies this. The question of whether cause and effect are identical or distinct is not a transactional question, but one that concerns the nature of these phenomena. It automatically crosses the boundary of conventional discourse and enters the register of analytical inquiry and ultimate truth.

#### 2.2.2 Refuting All Extremes

The refutation of true existence alone does not immediately establish freedom from all conceptual proliferation. Conceptual thought naturally reifies the “absence of inherent existence” as itself a reality. This extreme, the second lemma of non-existence, is refuted by understanding that non-existence is a mere fabrication, formed in relation to the positing of existence. When no existent thing stands before analysis, its mere privation too cannot remain. In making this point, Gorampa cites Jñānagarbha’s *Distinguishing the Two Truths* (*Satyadvayavibhaṅga*): “Because the object of negation does not exist, the negation itself is clearly unestablished in reality” (cited in *Synopsis of Madhyamaka*, p. 112). A philosopher or meditator might still attempt to assert the remaining two lemmas: conjunctive existence and non-existence; or their mutual denial, neither existence nor non-existence. However, these possibilities are quickly dismissed after the first two lemmas have been eliminated: the third violates the law of non-contradiction, and the fourth violates the law of the excluded middle.

Gorampa’s systematic description of this negation process in his *Synopsis of Madhyamaka* is partly a reaction to Tsongkhapa’s system. According to Tsongkhapa, the four lemmas are interpreted through strict, contextual qualifiers: the refutation of existence negates inherent existence, whereas the refutation of non-existence negates conventional non-existence or nihilism. Tsongkhapa also maintains that the non-affirming negation (*med dgag*) established by the negation of true existence constitutes the final ultimate truth, which is epistemically justified and thus impossible to further negate.

Gorampa rejects this interpretation of the tetralemma as philosophically unsound and counterproductive to Buddhist soteriological aims. The identification of ultimate truth exclusively with a simple privation effectively accords that privation ultimate existence, whereas Mādhyamikas claim that nothing exists ultimately. It entails a reification of emptiness, which for Mādhyamikas constitutes a subtle form of nihilism. Moreover, by insisting on qualifying negations lest conventional reality be undermined, and by treating the refutation of the extreme of non-existence as proof of conventional reality, Tsongkhapa establishes conventional truth within the domain of ultimate analysis. For Gorampa, Mādhyamika—and specifically Prāsaṅgika—analysis neither positively establishes an ultimate non-existence nor confirms conventional existence: it simply does not find anything, whereas conventional phenomena are otherwise findable as long as they are left unanalyzed.

As this strategy of non-finding systematically eliminates all concepts, cognitive fixation, and even the perception of subtle marks, it culminates in the complete cessation of conceptual proliferation. Gorampa cites Buddhist scripture extensively to support his view that the direct realization of ultimate truth is beyond all concepts, language, and dualistic experiences—reducing its object to a simple privation degrades the profundity of this path. Only such an ineffable experience, he contends, is capable of eliminating the subtlest cognitive obscurations and bringing about buddhahood.

Tsongkhapa and his followers maintain that alternative interpretations of the tetralemma, such as Gorampa’s, are logically untenable. Given the law of the excluded middle and the principle of double negation (*dgag pa gnyis kyi rnal ma*), the negation of non-existence strictly entails existence. Tsongkhapa’s reading of the tetralemma thus safeguards both conventional truth and the universality of logic. For Gorampa, binary disjunction and double negation hold at the conventional level: either there is a glass on the table or there is not, and asserting the presence of a glass on the table is equivalent to denying its absence. Ultimate analysis, however, does not yield an affirming negation (*ma yin dgag*) that implicitly establishes positive properties. Moreover, it does not operate in a context where the subject term remains intact. When no positive property is being established, and when the subject term or substratum of analysis itself collapses, the ontological requisites for predication are no longer present, leaving no domain for classical logical operators to apply.

Gorampa recognizes that critics may view his stance of freedom from proliferation as equivalent to the fourth member of the tetralemma, neither existence nor non-existence. If “neither existence nor non-existence” were asserted as an ontological claim, it would fail, as it does in conventional contexts, precisely because it violates the law of the excluded middle. Gorampa’s position, however, is one of radical non-finding: because ultimate analysis entirely voids the domain of predication, the resulting ultimate truth cannot be the object of linguistic referents or conceptual understanding. For Gorampa, it can only be realized by the complete cessation of conceptual thought.

### 2.3 Buddha-Nature, Mind, and Awakening

While Gorampa emphasizes that Madhyamaka provides a coherent account of the everyday world and its ultimate nature, his broader objective is to establish the philosophy as an effective foundation for pursuing Buddhist soteriological goals. The soteriological aspects of his Madhyamaka thought address three interrelated issues: the capacity of sentient beings to attain awakening, the mind’s ability to comprehend and meditate on the reality established through the Madhyamaka view, and the coherence of buddhahood given the epistemic powers ascribed to it.

The concept of Buddha-nature (Skt. *tathāgatagarbha*), although not attested in Nāgārjuna’s analytic corpus, became central to Mahāyāna Buddhist discourse, generating diverse and often conflicting interpretations. By the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries in Tibet, radically opposing views were vigorously contested. In his *Addendum to Distinguishing the Three Vows*, Gorampa sets out to defend a Sakya orthodoxy on the subject, rejecting the interpretations of Geluk, Jonang, and certain later Sakya exegetes.

Mahāyāna Buddhists broadly agree that Buddha-nature is a pure constitutive feature of sentient existence that accounts for a being’s capacity to become a fully actualized buddha. Tibetan scholars speak of Buddha-nature as the ground (*gzhi*) because it serves as the ontological foundation from which both *saṃsāra* and *nirvāṇa* manifest, depending on whether this naturally pure element is obscured by adventitious defilements. Gorampa identifies Buddha-nature as the unconditioned, indivisible unity of luminosity and emptiness. Here, luminosity is the mind’s intrinsic phenomenal vividness, not to be conflated with the disclosure of objects or reflexivity. Cognitive error and karma, both regarded as beginningless, produce the various conventional appearances of the world, which appear to be real and thereby perpetuate the cycle of delusion. When error is removed through the practice of the Buddhist path, the luminous and empty nature of mind manifests without distortion, allowing the qualities of enlightenment to be fully actualized.

Gorampa clarifies his formulation of Buddha-nature by critiquing competing accounts. Certain Sakya scholars—notably Gorampa’s own teacher, Rongtön—assert that the luminous aspect of Buddha-nature is a conditioned, conventional phenomenon. Dölpopa and his Jonang followers, by contrast, hold luminosity to be truly existent: it both withstands analytical scrutiny and remains present in the direct realization of ultimate reality. At the other extreme, one of Tsongkhapa’s foremost disciples, Gyeltsap Darma Rinchen (rGyal tshab dar ma rin chen, 1364–1432), denies that luminosity is an aspect of Buddha-nature at all. On this view, what grounds a sentient being’s capacity to become a buddha is simply the mind’s lack of inherent existence.

Gorampa argues that the luminosity of mind cannot be a conditioned phenomenon because it is not a temporary or adventitious quality; rather, it is the mind’s fundamental nature, uncreated by causes and conditions and unceasing. A conditioned property, moreover, is unfit to serve as the ontological ground in Madhyamaka. Conversely, luminosity is not ultimately existent, for, like emptiness, it cannot withstand analysis. Contrary to Gyeltsap’s view, excluding luminosity from Buddha-nature leads to absurdities, especially when what remains of Buddha-nature is exclusively conceived of as a simple privation. The emptiness of inherent existence is established with respect to all conventional phenomena, yet pots and tables do not become buddhas. Gorampa accepts that luminosity remains present during the unmediated experience of emptiness, even when all conventional phenomena cease to appear. *Pace* Dölpopa, this does not mean that luminosity is truly existent, but only that it is an uninterrupted clarity that is inseparable from emptiness, like a pure crystal that does not waver from its intrinsic clarity regardless of the objects it may reflect.

A major concern throughout Gorampa’s epistemology is determining the degree to which conceptual thought, speech, and experience can engage ultimate truth. The analytical establishment of ultimate truth necessarily involves approximation and superimposition—words point to categories that help dismantle reification and reveal ultimate reality, but since ultimate truth is beyond all conceptual categorization, any linguistic description is inaccurate. Gorampa’s theory of conceptual cognition closely follows that of Dharmakīrti in its core assumptions. Both maintain that conceptual thought is by nature erroneous in conflating universals and particulars, but nevertheless capable of pragmatic efficacy. Gorampa asserts that conceptual cognitions and verbal expressions of ultimate truth can likewise be pragmatically efficacious, even when they force ultimate truth into the very structures it transcends.

Because conceptual thought operates through exclusion (Skt. *anyāpoha*; Tib. *gzhan sel*) or determining its object by eliminating a dissimilar class, it can only isolate and negate one extreme of the tetralemma at a time. For the meditator, a process of staged conceptual focus on the negation of each extreme acts like rubbing sticks together to ignite a fire, causing conceptual thought to exhaust itself and allowing a direct experience of reality to unfold. Therein, all concepts, signs, and dualistic phenomena are absent, and the ontological ground, the union of emptiness and luminosity, is “seen in the manner of non-seeing”—that is, the event is ineffable, but is approximately described as “seeing.” Gorampa’s emphasis on the need for conceptual analysis to generate this state, and on the sequential nature of that analysis, distinguishes his approach from that of Buddhists who advocate sudden enlightenment. However, like all Buddhists, Gorampa believes that the direct experience of truth constitutes the primary antidote to rebirth in *saṃsāra*. In the immediate aftermath of this experience, conventional reality reappears, but only as a dream or an illusion.

Following the initial non-conceptual realization of ultimate truth comes a prolonged path of cultivation, on which meditative equipoise continues to remove subtle obscurations, and the practitioner’s many meritorious deeds are dedicated toward the welfare of sentient beings in perpetuity. Eventually, the practitioner enters their final meditative equipoise and becomes a buddha. Gorampa asserts that, at this stage, conventional phenomena do not reappear: because the cognitive error and karmic traces that generate them have been completely eradicated, a fully awakened being remains undisturbed in the union of luminosity and emptiness. Unlike certain Mādhyamikas, however, Gorampa rejects the notion that awakening is a complete void, akin to an extinguished candle. The Buddha retains a conscious aspect, although Gorampa holds that the pure luminosity that remains in this state is more accurately characterized as non-dual gnosis (*ye shes*) rather than ordinary intentional mind (*sems*).

In Tsongkhapa’s system, the Buddha sees conventional reality directly and flawlessly while at the same time perceiving emptiness; in this way, the Buddha alone is truly omniscient. Gorampa too maintains that the Buddha is omniscient, but distinguishes between the Buddha’s intrinsic experience (*rang snang*) and how this experience is seen by others (*gzhan snang*). To sentient beings, the Buddha appears to have full knowledge of conventional reality across past, present, and future. However, this appearance is generated through a combination of those beings’ karmic receptivity and the Buddha’s previously accumulated merit and aspiration. These apparent physical forms and conventional cognitive powers are not identical to the Buddha’s own awareness, which never wavers from the basic sphere of reality (Skt. *dharmadhātu*; Tib. *chos kyi dbyings*). The Buddha’s omniscience is thus a multidimensional phenomenon, grounded in his uninterrupted experience of ultimate truth while effortlessly projecting forms and teachings within the conventional perception of others.
